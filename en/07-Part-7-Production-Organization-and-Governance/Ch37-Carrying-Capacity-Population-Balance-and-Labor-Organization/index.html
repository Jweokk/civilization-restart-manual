    Chapter 37: Ecological Carrying Capacity, Population Balance, and Labor Organization¶

    Chapter Objectives¶

    After reading this chapter, a settlement should be able to produce four documents:

    Document Content Use
    Carrying-capacity estimate document The supportable population range computed by the three-ceiling method Decides "whether more people can be taken in, whether a new furnace should be lit"
    Off-farm balance table Agricultural surplus → number of artisans supportable → technology-step ceiling Decides "which chapter's technology to build up to now"
    Shift and watch schedules The minimum shift establishment for each continuous process Keeps the blast furnace, long kiln, and steam engine from stopping for lack of hands
    Skill matrix and responsibility boundaries Skill grades for the ten posts, resource red lines, change control Keeps the technology chain from breaking because any one person is lost

    Capability Boundaries¶

    Can do:

    • Given at least one complete farming-season record, estimate carrying capacity to an order of ±30–50%.
    • Compute the off-farm artisan quota (historical range 5–15%) and lay out the minimum shift schedule that satisfies the continuous processes.
    • Define the minimum skill sets, training periods, and assessment evidence for the ten posts.

    Cannot do:

    • Predict sudden population changes from epidemic, conflict, or migration in and out. This chapter can only partially hedge with the redundancy coefficient (Section 37.2.4); it cannot predict.
    • Give a reliable estimate without farming-season records. In that case every result can only be marked "provisional figures", with error up to ±100%, and is forbidden for furnace-lighting and capacity-expansion decisions.
    • Replace the safety procedures of the individual process chapters. This chapter governs "how people are arranged and how accounts are kept", not "how the furnace is fired".

    External dependencies (counted on the C list):

    1. Yield records for at least one complete farming season (including under-yielding plots and their causes) — this is the foundation of every estimate.
    2. A health roster of the whole settlement (who can work a full day under the blazing sun and who cannot).
    3. An inventory of leftovers from the start (metal tools, containers, clothing, medicine stocks) — this sets the buffer for the first two years.

    Pre-Work Verification¶

    Checklist style. If any criterion fails, the corresponding estimate must be downgraded to "provisional figures", and the cover of the estimate document must state which item is missing.

    # Verification item Criterion (confirm you have X in hand, and the standard is Y)
    1 Population roster Every person registered with name, age, health status; the "full laborers" (recommended definition: aged 15–55 with no long-term injury or illness) can be counted
    2 Grain stock count Everything weighed or converted by volume measure, error ≤10%; registered in three columns: "edible, moldy, seed stock"
    3 Arable land register Areas measured by survey-rope pacing (Chapter 3 method), in three columns: "under cultivation, fallow, reclaimable"; each plot carries a soil note
    4 Forest land register Area and round-trip labor hours to the nearest cutting site; at least 3 quadrats of 10 m×10 m with tree-species and DBH (diameter at breast height) records
    5 Water source records Dry-season minimum flow estimate (Chapter 2 method) + water actually drawn by people and livestock over 3 consecutive days
    6 Fuel account Day-by-day weighing records of household firewood for 7 consecutive days; specific fuel consumption of furnaces and kilns already built copied from each chapter's acceptance ledger
    7 Farming-season records For at least one season: sown area, actual harvested weight of unprocessed grain, under-yielding plots and their causes
    8 Weights and measures One set of length, volume, and mass standards for the whole settlement (Chapters 3 and 22), cross-checked on the spot once between the account office and each workshop's handlers

    37.1 Energy Flow and Per-Capita Consumption Benchmarks¶

    Balance starts from "what one person consumes in a year". Every number in this section is an empirical value that must be field-calibrated, each with its calibration method attached. Until calibration is done, the ranges may be used only for order-of-magnitude judgment and must not be used for production scheduling or furnace-lighting decisions.

    37.1.1 Per-Capita Annual Consumption Benchmark Table¶

    Item Empirical range Applicable conditions Field calibration method
    Food ration (unprocessed grain) 200–300 kg/person·year, of which cereals 180–250, legumes 20–40 Normal labor intensity; children counted at 0.5, the elderly at 0.7; heavy labor (miners, forging smiths, loggers) raised 20–30% Current grain stock ÷ number of people ÷ months it can sustain × 12; cross-check against the actual-harvest account; if deviation >20%, re-weigh the grain stock
    Household firewood 2–4 kg/person·day Temperate zones; take the lower bound for cooking only, the upper bound for winter heating; workshop fuel is accounted separately and must never be mixed into this line Pick 3 typical households and weigh the wood they actually burn day by day for 7 consecutive days; take the median
    Fiber (hemp/cotton/wool) New fiber 2–4 kg/person·year Garments held: 3–4 pieces per person, each lasting 4–8 years Inventory the garments in use and the year's mending hours; back-calculate the annual replacement quantity
    Salt 3–5 kg/person·year Includes pickling and preservation; fatigue, cramps, and a sudden drop in appetite are salt-deficiency signals Record actual issues in the account; check the stock difference monthly
    Housing materials Service life of earth-and-timber houses 10–20 years Annual maintenance ≈ 1/10–1/15 of the material for one new room Inspect houses before and after the rainy season; register repair labor and materials

    ◎ Judging whether a grain bin is full or hollow (tapping the bin and listening to detect mold and voids) is a hand-feel step. Practice method: practice 20 times on grain bins that have already been weighed, then estimate blind. Misjudgment compensation: insert a probe tube into each bin to take a mid-layer sample for weighing, and spot-check 10% of the bins.

    37.1.2 Workshop Specific Fuel Consumption (Separate Account)¶

    Workshop fuel is not spread into per-capita figures; each furnace keeps its own account. A furnace or kiln with no specific-consumption record must first burn one run and record one run before production scheduling is discussed.

    Furnace/kiln Empirical specific-consumption range Calibration method Source chapter
    Batch pottery kiln 0.3–0.8 t firewood per firing Weigh the wood loaded for the whole kiln, or convert by wood-bundle volume Chapters 11–12
    Iron-smelting fuel ratio Charcoal : iron = 4–8 : 1 (by weight) Record charcoal charged and iron produced per furnace run Chapter 18
    Charcoal yield 1 kg charcoal ≈ 4–6 kg firewood Weigh everything in and out of the kiln Chapter 6
    Lime kiln 0.3–0.5 t firewood per t lime Keep accounts kiln by kiln Chapter 13

    Specific consumption for the glass melting furnace, steam engine fuel, and the like is accounted in the corresponding chapters; this table does not fill it in on their behalf.

    37.1.3 Energy Flow Directions¶

    Farmland ─→ food ration + seed stock (10–15%) + fodder + reserves
Forest ─→ household firewood + workshop wood/charcoal + construction and implement timber (all three draw on the same standing stock)
Water ─→ drinking and cooking + irrigation + workshop cooling and washing
Human labor ─→ farm work + gathering and transport + workshop labor + night watch shifts (one person's hours are a hard constraint)

    Two corollaries feed directly into what follows: first, every furnace lit is in fact drawing on two budgets at once — the forest and the agricultural surplus; second, human hours are a balancing item just like grain — this is why the shift scheduling of Section 37.4 exists.

    Draft-animal conversion: one draft ox consumes 8–12 kg of hay per day (or grazes 4–6 h), and its annual feed equals 0.3–0.6 ha of grassland or cropland by-products; this must be counted when reckoning cultivated area and supported population.

    37.2 The Three-Ceiling Method for Carrying Capacity¶

    Carrying capacity is not one number; it is the thinnest of three chains:

    Supportable population N = min(food ceiling N₁, fuel ceiling N₂, water-and-building-materials ceiling N₃) ÷ redundancy coefficient k (1.3–1.5)

    37.2.1 Food Ceiling N₁¶

    Formula: N₁ = arable area S (ha) × yield y (kg/ha·year) × multiple-cropping index × net retention ratio ÷ per-capita ration r (kg/person·year, weighted by population structure).

    Empirical yield ranges (unprocessed grain):

    Farming method Empirical yield range Applicable conditions
    Slash-and-burn 500–1000 kg/ha·season Cropped 2–3 consecutive years, then fallowed 8–15 years (Chapter 50 scenario)
    Unfertilized dry farming 600–1200 kg/ha Slopes, no irrigation, no compost
    Dry farming with composted rotation 1000–2000 kg/ha With compost and legume rotation (Chapter 10)
    Irrigated rice 2000–4000 kg/ha·season Plains, controllable water source, ample draft power

    Calibration: leave 3 harvested quadrats of 10 m×10 m in each field class, thresh and weigh them separately; scale the quadrat result up to the whole field, then cross-check against the season's total harvest; if deviation >20%, switch to the total-harvest basis and discard quadrat extrapolation.

    Net retention ratio: deduct 10–15% for seed stock and 10–20% per year for storage losses (rats, insects, mold); the edible net ≈ harvest × 0.65–0.80.

    37.2.2 Fuel Ceiling N₂¶

    Formula: N₂ = (forest area × annual growth × 0.7–0.8) ÷ (per-capita household firewood + workshop fuel converted to a per-capita share).

    • Empirical annual growth: temperate mixed forest 2–5 m³/ha·year (air-dry weight about 1.2–3.5 t/ha·year); tropical secondary forest is higher, but the part that cannot be hauled out in the rainy season must be deducted (Chapter 50).
    • The 0.7–0.8 coefficient: reserve 20–30% of the growth for construction timber, implements, and forest enclosure; burning the entire growth as fuel is not allowed.
    • Fuelwood coppice runs on a rotation cycle of 10–20 years: required fuelwood stand area = annual wood consumption × rotation cycle, and each year wood is taken from only 1/cycle of the area.
    • Calibration: set 3–5 quadrats of 10 m×10 m and measure the DBH increment year by year; without year-by-year records, fell 1–2 sample trees, weigh them, and count the growth rings to back-calculate the annual increment per hectare.

    ◎ Eyeballing "which stand has been thinned by cutting" (stand-structure hand feel) is a hand-feel step. Practice method: practice 10 times against quadrats that have already been measured. Misjudgment compensation: stump counts inside the quadrat + a measured DBH distribution; in a thinned-out stand the DBH distribution shifts left as a whole.

    37.2.3 Water and Building-Materials Ceiling N₃¶

    • Water: drinking and cooking 20–50 L/person·day; peak irrigation demand per the field accounts of Chapter 10; workshop cooling and washing counted separately per the specific consumption of Chapters 26 and 28. Criterion: if the dry-season minimum flow < the sum above → the ceiling binds on water, and only the irrigable part of the arable land is counted.
    • Building materials: register every extraction point for clay, sand, stone, and straw. Criterion: if round-trip extraction hours exceed 1/3 of the day's working hours, that source is deemed unsustainable — recalculate the cost using the farther source, or lower the building standard.

    37.2.4 Redundancy Coefficient k = 1.3–1.5¶

    Redundancy rationale Magnitude (empirical values)
    Bad-year fluctuation (drought, flood, insects) ±30%
    Annual reserve set aside 10–15% of output
    Storage and transport losses 10–20%
    Downtime from illness and injury 5–10%

    Setting the value: two full seasons of records and no major disaster → 1.3; less than one season of records or frequent disasters → 1.5; interpolate between according to circumstances. k may not go below 1.3 unless the settlement already has back-verification records for three consecutive seasons.

    37.2.5 Worked Example (for instruction; the numbers are hypothetical)¶

    Suppose a settlement has 300 people: arable land 80 ha, composted rotation, y taken as 1500 kg/ha, net retention 0.70 → net grain 84 t; weighted r = 250 kg → N₁ = 336 people. Forest 400 ha, annual growth 2.5 t/ha, coefficient taken as 0.75 → usable 750 t/year; total per-capita wood (household 1.1 t + workshop-converted 0.75 t) = 1.85 t → N₂ = 406 people. Water: sufficient in the dry season → N₃ does not bind. min = 336, k taken as 1.4 → N = 240 people < the current 300 people. Conclusion: this settlement is already overloaded, and its options are only three — reclaim land and/or compost to raise yields, seal furnaces to cut workshop wood, or split off part of the people to establish a separate outpost. This example shows what the three-ceiling method is for: what it often tells you is not "how many more people can be taken in" but "what must be cut right now".

    [Card 37-01 Carrying-Capacity Estimate Card] (eight fields)

    1. Input materials: population roster, arable and forest registers, weighed grain-stock records, 7-day fuel weighing records, farming-season harvest records; volume measures and scales (Chapter 3; cross-checked against standard weights before use).
    2. Required tools: survey rope and ranging poles (Chapter 3), 10 m quadrat rope, scales, account book. Calibrate before use: compare the survey rope's length against a standard rule; if error >0.5%, replace the rope; check the scales' zero point and full scale with known weights.
    3. Operating sequence and control points: ① Check the 8 pre-work verification items and mark missing ones on the cover; ② compute N₁, N₂, N₃ item by item, each on its own line; ③ take the min, then divide by k, obtaining a range rather than a single value; ④ Reverse check: if the current population exceeds 80% of the ceiling, put production cuts, land reclamation, or furnace closure on the agenda and write this into the estimate document's conclusion field; ⑤ Transcribe into the ledger, noting the data cutoff date.
    4. Empirical parameters and calibration: y, annual growth, net retention ratio, and k take their values from the tables of Section 37.2, with the raw quadrat and weighing data attached; filling in numbers from impressions is not allowed.
    5. Process observation and recording items: the location of each quadrat, the weighing date, and the handler; the first page of the estimate document states the "data cutoff date" and the "missing-items list".
    6. Pass / rework / scrap criteria: pass = each of the three ceilings has an independent measured source and the arithmetic can be recomputed; rework = a ceiling used the range default instead of measurement (re-measure that item and recompute); scrap = estimates substituted for weighing records (the whole card is voided and the inventory redone).
    7. Common failures and troubleshooting order: result clearly too large → first check whether seed stock and storage losses were not deducted → check whether workshop wood was mixed into the household-firewood line → check whether dividing by k was forgotten; result too small → check whether the quadrats happened to be placed in under-yielding fields → check whether inaccessible slopes were counted as forest → check whether r was marked up twice.
    8. L1 / L2 / L3 evidence: L1 = an estimate document containing the arithmetic and the missing-items list; L2 = the estimate document + back-verification records of one farming season's actual harvest and actual fuel consumption (deviation ≤50%); L3 = two consecutive seasons of back-verification + ledger update records + resource red-line patrol records (Section 37.6.4).

    Blank record sheet (for copying the card):

    Item Year 1 Year 2
    Population N / full-laborer count
    Arable land S (ha) / measured yield y
    Net grain output Y (t)
    N₁ formula and result
    Forest area (ha) / measured annual growth
    Household wood + workshop wood (t/year)
    N₂ formula and result
    Dry-season minimum flow / N₃ result
    min ÷ k = supportable range
    Conclusion (take in people / hold steady / overloaded)
    Missing-items list

    37.3 Agricultural Surplus Rate and Off-Farm Artisan Balance¶

    37.3.1 Surplus Rate and Off-Farm Quota¶

    Formulas:

    • Surplus grain S_sur = net grain output Y − N×r − seed stock − reserve increment
    • Fully off-farm people supportable D = S_sur ÷ r
    • Off-farm ratio = D ÷ N

    Empirical range: the long-term off-farm ratio of stable traditional agricultural societies is 5–15%; in good years with irrigation plus compost it can briefly reach 20%; in bad years it is 0 or negative. Any long-term plan laid out at an off-farm ratio >15% must present measured evidence for irrigation or compost (Chapter 10), or it is deemed overload overdraft.

    Two corrections:

    • Artisans eat more: rations for heavy-labor posts (miners, forging smiths, loggers, kiln workers) are raised 20–30% (Section 37.1.1); in the balance, count these people at r×1.25.
    • Half off-farm counts as half: part-time artisans who return to the fields for the busy season and enter the workshops in the slack season count as 0.5 of a quota.

    37.3.2 Population Size → Technology-Step Reference Table¶

    The table below turns "how many people does a settlement need to reach which chapter" into arithmetic. "Stable support" in the table means the materials, fuel, watch-keeping, and succession of that step can be sustained long-term; "having made one piece once" does not count (that is L1).

    Settlement size Off-farm headcount (on the 5–15% range, midpoint 10%) Technology steps it can stably support What it cannot support
    A village of 50 people 3–5 people (mostly seasonal part-timers) Parts 2 and 3: stone tools and woodworking (Chapters 5–9), pottery, brick, and tile (Chapters 11–13), compost agriculture and grain storage (Chapter 10), small glass items in a batch crucible furnace (Chapter 14) A continuously run blast furnace (one non-extinguished blast furnace alone requires a 7-person establishment, see Section 37.4.2), machine tools, steam engines, electricity of any kind
    A settlement of 300 people 15–45 people Parts 4 and 5: bloomery smelting and small blast furnaces fired seasonally (Chapters 16–18), steel and tool forging (Chapter 19), a single machine tool and standard parts (Chapters 20–22), glassware and basic chemistry (Chapters 14, 23–26), simple instruments (Chapter 27) Year-round non-extinguished metallurgy, continuous power beyond the waterwheel, the power grid (Chapter 32), electronic devices
    A market town of 2000 people 100–300 people Most of Part 6: year-round blast-furnace production, machine-tool work crews, a single steam engine (Chapter 28) and water and wind power (Chapter 29), an electromagnetism laboratory (Chapter 30), a single generator and in-plant distribution (Chapter 31), carbon-filament lamps and trials of simple vacuum devices (Chapter 33) Grid-scale power supply (Chapter 32), semiconductor-grade purification (Chapter 34), integrated circuits (Chapter 35) — these require division of labor at the ten-thousand-person scale and a stable chemical industry

    Three principles for reading the table:

    1. The step is set by the thinnest ceiling. Even if a 50-person settlement finds a complete set of drawings, it cannot field a 7-person blast-furnace establishment — what is missing is not knowledge but off-farm people and surplus grain.
    2. The threshold of Chapter 35 is organizational, not technical. Going from 2000 people to the ten-thousand scale adds not "smarter artisans" but a higher grain commercialization rate, transport, and the account-office system.
    3. The environment editions move the thresholds. The four environments of Chapters 50–53 each rewrite this table: the fuel ceiling is tighter in the rainforest, the food ceiling tighter in permafrost, water and minerals tighter on islands. Re-run the three-ceiling method of Section 37.2 for each environment; do not copy this table as-is.

    37.3.3 Busy-Season Pullback¶

    Most artisans are still relatives of farm households and will be pulled back to the fields for the 20–40 days of the busy season. Production rule: any workshop plan spanning the busy season is pre-scheduled at a reduction factor of 0.5; continuous processes (blast furnace, long kiln) must either shut down for maintenance before the busy season or lock the stay-behind shift schedule in advance and buy out the labor with double rations.

    37.4 Continuous Processes and Shift Scheduling¶

    37.4.1 Watch-Keeping Classification and Labeling Principle¶

    Throughout the book, processes are labeled in three classes by watch-keeping demand, and every chapter's process cards use the same vocabulary:

    Class Criterion Examples
    Single-person process Interruptible; stops when hands stop; no hazard accumulation Woodworking, textiles, the account office, card copying
    Two-person process Heavy labor requiring rotation, or a need for mutual safety supervision Underground mining (Chapter 16), sledgehammer forging (Chapter 19), logging (Chapter 7)
    Crew process Continuous and non-interruptible; shutdown means scrap or an extremely costly restart Blast furnace, long-kiln firing section, steam engine running, large glass melting furnace

    The classification is a hard constraint: a single-person process needs only one person scheduled; solo work is forbidden on two-person processes; a crew process must not be lit until its establishment is complete.

    37.4.2 Blast-Furnace Shift Schedule (Must Not Go Out)¶

    Once a blast furnace is lit, molten iron and furnace condition must never be left unattended overnight. Minimum establishment: 3 shifts per 24 hours × 2 people per shift + 1 float spare = 7 people, with cross-shift responsibility held by the furnace head (a grade-3 forging smith; see Section 37.5).

    Shift Hours Post A Post B Handover contents
    Morning shift 06–14 Charging worker (weighs material per the ledger's batching sheet) Tuyere worker (watches flame color, logs blast conditions) Batching progress, iron-tapping and slagging records, the three anomaly columns
    Mid shift 14–22 Same as above Same as above Same as above
    Night shift 22–06 Same as above Same as above Same as above + hourly tuyere records

    Rules: no solo night shifts; each handover takes 15 min of face-to-face checking with dual signatures; the spare rests by day and covers vacancies at any time. The smaller the furnace, the less you should skimp on these 7 people — skimping ends with a frozen hearth at three in the morning, and the cost of reopening exceeds three months of wage grain.

    37.4.3 Shift Schedule Templates for the Long Kiln, Steam Engine, and Glass Melting Furnace¶

    Facility Continuous-run duration People per shift 24-hour establishment Notes
    Long kiln (firing section) 20–60 h 2 (stoking + fire-watching and temperature taking) 3 shifts × 2 = 6 people Before the firing section starts, wood must be stockpiled beside the kiln and weighed
    Steam engine running The entire run 1 driver + 1 fireman 6 people Add 1 electrical-watch recorder when coupled to a generator
    Large glass melting furnace Melting–forming 24–72 h 2 in the melting section; 4–6 in the forming section 6–10 people depending on furnace size In the forming section gathering rotates; one person gathers ≤2 h at a stretch

    ◎ The hand feel of gathering glass and of reading the fire is not covered in this chapter; follow the practice methods of the corresponding chapters (Chapters 12, 14, 18, 19). This chapter only fixes "how many people per shift and how many hours per rotation".

    37.4.4 Fatigue and Night Shifts (Iron Law 7)¶

    Empirical values: past 12 h continuously on duty, or from the 4th hour of a night shift onward, error rates rise significantly; the night-shift scrap rate runs 1.5–3 times the day-shift rate. Calibration: tally scrap counts by shift (record field of Card 37-02); three weeks yields the settlement's own ratio.

    Control rules:

    1. Night shifts are always two-person; solo watching of a furnace, kiln, or machine is forbidden.
    2. Key parameters (batching ratios, gate openings, steam-pressure limits) require two-person recheck with signatures.
    3. Mandatory 8 h of rest after a night shift; running straight into the day shift is not allowed.
    4. No more than 4 night shifts per person per week; the account office checks the schedule for violations.

    [Card 37-02 Shift Schedule Card] (eight fields)

    1. Input materials: the skill matrix (Card 37-03), the minimum establishment of each continuous process (Sections 37.4.2/37.4.3), the ration quota table (Section 37.1), the time basis from moon phase or sundial (Chapter 3).
    2. Required tools: the time basis (Chapter 3 sundial or water clock, cross-checked against the noon shadow before use), a schedule board with chalk or cards, an account book. Accuracy requirement: shift-change error ≤15 min.
    3. Operating sequence and control points: ① List the continuous processes to be lit this ten-day period and their minimum establishments; ② schedule the night shifts first (starting from the hardest hours), then the day shifts; ③ check three lines per person: ≤4 night shifts per week, ≤12 h continuously on duty, 8 h rest after a night shift; ④ List the spare crew on a separate page; ⑤ Post publicly and copy to the account office and the mess kitchen (those with occupation-based ration uplift draw rations against the schedule).
    4. Empirical parameters and calibration: shift length 8 h (empirical value); verify the settlement's actual work-rest pattern by "scrap rate tallied by shift"; if one shift's scrap rate stays more than 50% above the mean, check fatigue before checking the process.
    5. Process observation and recording items: dual signatures on every shift handover, hourly records (whichever applies: tuyere/kiln temperature/steam pressure), scrap counts by shift, absences and who filled in.
    6. Pass / rework / scrap criteria: pass = establishment complete, no one violating the three lines, handover records complete; rework = a solo night shift or overtime double shift occurred (re-lay the schedule within the same ten-day period and recheck that shift's output); scrap = running without records (all output of that period is treated as an unfinished process and may not be accepted, per Iron Law 6).

    37.6 Institutions and Responsibility Boundaries¶

    37.6.1 Resource Use Rights and Maintenance Duties¶

    Resource Use rules Maintenance duty Records
    Mine Going underground against the annual extraction quota, logged as two-person entry The on-shift miner does support and ventilation; major drift repairs are chartered through the account office Underground entry register, sampling records
    Forest Firewood taken against the annual cutting-area quota (Section 37.2.2); closed forest areas are off-limits Whoever cuts hauls and replants; young-stand tending rotates within the logging crew Cutting-area register, replanting survival counts
    Water source Intake and outfall separated upstream/downstream; settling-basin dredging rotates Beneficiary households rotate; during epidemics a well closure has a designated keeper Water account and dredging records
    Roads and bridges Beneficiary workshops share the labor The sharing list is agreed at the start of the year, supervised by the account office Road-repair labor account
    Workshop furnaces and kilns Daily upkeep by the users Overhauls chartered as projects; spares carry a separate maintenance account Furnace and kiln operating ledgers
    Warehouse Two people, two locks Monthly inventory by the account office; discrepancies >5% must be resolved within the month Monthly inventory records

    Principle: a shared resource with unclear responsibility will certainly be overused — the forest dies first, then the mine drifts and the roads. Every rule line must come down to "who, for how long, recorded where".

    37.6.2 Accident Response Authority¶

    1. The right to stop work: anyone facing a risk of death or disabling injury may halt the process on the spot. No blame attaches to stopping; the review looks for institutional faults; blame attaches only to concealing risks.
    2. Three things within 24 h of an accident: treat the injured (the medic post), preserve the scene (do not move key physical evidence), and make a written record (time, people, process, parameters, injuries).
    3. Before production resumes, the process must pass a change check (Section 37.6.3) confirming the accident cause has been dealt with.

    37.6.3 Change Control: Who May Alter Process Parameters¶

    • Altering a parameter = the dual signatures of the holder of that process card (grade 3) and the workshop supervisor; neither suffices alone.
    • Five entries in the ledger: date, old parameter value, new value, reason, and the verification results of the first batch of 3 samples.
    • For an alteration without dual signatures, the entire batch's output is treated as an unfinished process and may not be accepted (Iron Law 6).
    • Right of rollback: if verification fails, anyone may demand restoration of the old parameters; the rollback is recorded the same way.

    37.6.4 Historical Failure Mode of Over-Cutting¶

    The typical death chain: in year 1 firewood is cut nearby and fuel looks plentiful on the books → by year 3 the cutting radius has doubled and haulage hours eat the net fuel gain → by year 5 the tree line has retreated into the watershed and erosion smothers the fields → metallurgy and firing are cut off, the artisans disperse back to their villages, and the technology step falls back two parts.

    Red-line criteria (any one triggered forces forest closure and production cuts):

    Red line Threshold (empirical value; must be calibrated year by year)
    Annual increase in cutting radius >20%
    Firewood haulage hours ÷ total fuel-procurement hours >40%
    Young-stand share of the fuelwood forest <30%

    Red-line patrols run once per season and are entered in the ledger; when a line is triggered, cut workshop wood first (seal the furnaces), not household wood.

    37.7 Internal Exchange and Labor Accounting (completing C7)¶

    The settlement's internal "who owes whom" must be computable: labor chits — non-farming posts (workshops/guards/teaching) are issued labor chits per standard workday, redeemable against rations and common supplies (the redemption rate is set quarterly by the council and entered in the ledger); a barter account — cross-post barter (smith ↔ potter) is priced in both parties' labor chits and entered in the account, eliminating the distortion of "favor debts"; inter-workshop transfer notes — materials moving between workshops must carry a three-column note (item and quantity/issuer's work number/receiver's work number), reconciled monthly against the materials account of Chapter 38. Criterion: any "debt" can be stated on one page with its principal and its booking date. Labor chits are not money (no accruing interest, no use outside); the boundary is fixed to prevent financialization.

    Safety Boundary¶

    This chapter contains no direct high-temperature or high-pressure processes, but the following three items are part of its process steps; not executing them counts as not completing this chapter:

    1. The solo-night-shift ban: every furnace, kiln, and machine carries at least two people on night shift; violators have that shift's output processed under the scrap rules of Card 37-02.
    2. The underground and felling two-person rule: the original procedures of Chapters 16 and 7 apply, and this chapter's scheduling must not split up the pairs.
    3. The fatigue red line: ≤12 h continuously on duty, 8 h rest after a night shift, ≤4 night shifts per week; the account office checks violations every ten days and posts them publicly.

    Electric-shock first aid is a mandatory assessment item for the electrician post; accident response authority and the 24 h three items are in Section 37.6.2.
