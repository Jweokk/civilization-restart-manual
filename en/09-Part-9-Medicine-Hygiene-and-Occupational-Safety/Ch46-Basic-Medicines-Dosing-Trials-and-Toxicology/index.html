    Chapter 46 Basic Medicines, Dosing Trials, and Toxicology¶

    Chapter Goals¶

    After reading this chapter and implementing its institution, a settlement should produce five deliverables:

    Deliverable Content Use
    Medicine-material register Registration form + evidence grades I/II/III (46.2), one page and one registration number per medicine All medication starts from a registration number, never from orally transmitted drug names
    Prohibited list Whole-family exclusion of highly toxic plant families + local poison register (46.5.4), copied and posted at the medical office and the gathering teams Stop what can kill first; only then discuss what can heal
    Dose-ladder institution Four-level ladder, body-weight conversion, frequency and day-count caps (46.4), with a dosing record sheet The source of doses in an age without a pharmacopoeia
    Preparation and storage procedures Four lines — drying, decoction, infusion, powder/pill — and the label/expiry institution (46.3) Every batch under the same registration number is traceable for potency
    Complete demonstration-case record Willow-bark decoction (red line: during febrile viral illness in children/adolescents, all salicylate-containing preparations are forbidden — Reye syndrome risk; this rule saves more lives than the dose ladder) walking the four steps "register → prepare → dose → compare" (46.7.2) The standard operating template of the whole institution, for those who come after to copy

    Capability Boundary¶

    Can (this chapter gives methods and criteria): establishing the local four-step institution of register—prepare—dose—observe; poison screening and building the prohibited list; disciplined use of a few "engineering-clear" materials (alcohol for disinfection, fever rubs, and analgesia; soap for cleaning; honey and sugar for dressings at registration grade; salt-sugar oral rehydration; charcoal powder at empirical grade; water and rest); the limited means against pain and fever (physical cooling, alcohol rubs, and one demonstration case that walks the four-step institution end to end).

    Cannot (explicitly "not done"):

    Not done Reason When it can resume
    Antibiotics, vaccines, biologicals Master-outline "explicitly excluded technologies": require controlled culture, aseptic filling, batch testing, cold chain Microscope + aseptic technique + strain library + assay system (Chapters 44–46 jointly define the boundary)
    Purification and quantification of alkaloids and potent constituents No chromatography, no assay titration; "purification" narrows the therapeutic window to the point where no dose error is tolerable — crude drug from 1/4 on a ladder is controllable, purified extract is out of control After the purification and testing systems of Parts Five and Six mature
    Plant medicines with extremely narrow therapeutic windows (cardiac glycosides, opium-class, strychnos-class) The effective dose lies too close to the lethal dose, with no antidote and no blood-level monitoring; classed as candidates for the permanent prohibited list External analytical testing system
    Any efficacy claim not yet locally verified Review Iron Law: "treating traditional names as confirmed ingredients" is pseudoscience Never — this chapter permits only "local records show," never "this medicine cures"
    Trial-period medication for children and pregnant women Small body mass, poor metabolism, teratogenic risk; extreme caution required beyond the boundary: children limited to grade I/II medicines, weight-converted, still on the ladder; pregnant women by default take no trial-period medicine —

    Honest declaration (more important than the capability list): until the register produces its first piece of grade I evidence, this settlement's "medicines" are only the few engineering-clear materials listed in 46.6. Everything else — including the willow bark mentioned in this book — is merely a registered entry awaiting verification. "Having no medicine" is this chapter's default state, and the four-step institution is the only channel for earning evidence out of that default state.

    External dependencies (counted in the C list): legacy medicines (consume only, no resupply; booked into Chapter 38 — one pill used, one pill recorded); antidotes (no route; 46.5's management is "stop the medicine + support + record"); external pharmacopoeia and testing systems.

    46.1 Engineering the Medicine Problem: Why a "Drug Name" Cannot Be Trusted¶

    46.1.1 The Four Sources of Drift¶

    Iron Law Three says "iron ore does not equal iron." The medicine problem is fully isomorphic: a "drug name" does not equal a medicine. From "someone said some medicine treats some disease" to "such-and-such a person in this settlement swallowed such-and-such grams of such-and-such a batch today," every step in between drifts. The four sources of drift are listed below — they are not theoretical risks but measured errors occurring every day in every orally transmitting society:

    Source of drift Typical manifestation Consequence Institutional countermeasure
    Same name, different thing Plants called "XX herb" in two places may belong to different families and genera; closely related species look alike yet differ enormously in toxicity (edible water dropwort and poisonous hemlock grow side by side at the water's edge) At best ineffective, at worst a whole batch poisoned Registration-number institution (46.2.1): medicine enters use only by registration number + retained sample, never by oral name; whole-family exclusion (46.5.4)
    Provenance and soil composition differences The same plant species in different soils and with different companion organisms can vary several-fold in active-constituent and toxic-constituent content Copying "the dose from elsewhere" equals dosing blind Each gathering site registered separately; new provenance = new medicine — re-walk the ladder (46.4.2)
    Collection season and plant-part differences Root, bark, leaf, flower, fruit, and seed differ in composition; the same plant differs by season (early-spring bark vs midsummer bark) "15 g of willow bark" without knowing which season's, which part's 15 g The registration form mandatorily records part + season (46.2.1); preparation keeps retained samples
    Chaotic dose units "A handful, one qian, three roots, a pinch" — hands vary in size, roots in thickness, and the "qian" was never equal across eras Dose error can exceed 3-fold (same verdict as salt-grabbing in Chapter 44) Convert everything to dry weight in grams (Chapter 3 balance); the recorded quantity itself is only the ladder's starting point, not a trusted value (46.4.1)

    The institutions the four countermeasure rows point to are the body of this chapter. Note that the drift sources stack: when an oral folk prescription takes all four drifts at once, the actual error of "filling the prescription as written" is not several-fold — it is "unknown." That is precisely the technical reason for the capability boundary's "never assert efficacy."

    46.1.2 How "Effective" Is Judged: A Plain Version of Controlled Comparison¶

    "The medicine was given and the illness cleared" is not evidence that "the medicine works" — most minor illnesses clear on their own anyway, and in a doctorless world this confusion is especially deadly: mistaking self-healing for efficacy cements useless, even toxic things into a pharmacopoeia. The local standard for judging "effective" has three criteria; all three are required:

    1. Faster than the natural course: the treated group's median time to symptom relief is shorter than the natural course of comparable past patients (data accumulated from the Chapter 47 case register and the 44.10.3 injury record sheets).
    2. Repeatable: across different people, different material batches, and different seasons, results point the same direction. One improvement is a lead; three cases pointing the same way are needed for grade II.
    3. Controlled: patients from the same pool are split into two groups — one takes the medicine, the other gets only the 46.6 basic care (water, rest, rehydration) — with all other conditions as equal as possible, then the courses are compared. This is Chapter 25's control-group and single-variable method applied directly to patients — change one thing at a time: the group getting the new medicine must lose none of the other care.

    The ethical rules of control grouping are written in the safety boundary: grouping must be voluntary, open, and decided by lot; the standard of care for trial and control groups must not be lowered in any way. Also record the limits of the control honestly — patients know they are drinking medicine ("blinding" is impossible), and self-reported symptoms skew toward improvement, so choose observation items that can be recorded objectively (time course of fever fall, number of vomiting episodes, time until able to eat) and minimize "feels better."

    The cost of an ineffective medicine must also be written plainly: an ineffective medicine is not just "swallowed for nothing" — it occupies caregiver hands, masks the true course of illness, and ushers toxic accumulation (46.5.1) through the door. On this route, eliminating ineffective medicine matters as much as finding effective medicine — this is exactly Chapter 43's negative-results archive applied to medicine.

    Finally, a counterpoint as important as this whole section: "watchful waiting" is itself a treatment. For a patient with mild illness, low fever, and the ability to drink, giving nothing — only water, rest, and day-by-day records — usually ends better than hastily pushing a grade III medicine in, because the worst outcome of not medicating is the illness following its natural course, while the worst outcome of the wrong medicine is turning a natural course into a poisoning course. The medical office's default posture is "record first, medicate later": medicating always needs a justification (registration number + grade + ladder); not medicating never needs one.

    46.2 The Local Medicine-Material Registration Institution: Prohibited List First, Permitted List After¶

    46.2.1 The Registration Form: One Medicine, One Page, One Registration Number¶

    Registration-number rule: Medicine-SiteCode-Year-Serial (e.g., "Med-D3-Year3-07"). From then on, all dosing records, labels, and case-file references use only the registration number, never the name — the name stays only in the registration form for traceability. The registration form has nine fixed fields:

    Field What to enter Why it is required
    Name and local name Local usage + outside aliases (record if any) Guards against same-name-different-thing: aliases are the archive of drift sources
    Family/genus clues Descriptive morphology ("tree; branches pliant; flowers before leaves in early spring; catkins; favors watersides"); Latin name attached if available Does not demand taxonomic skill, demands re-checkable morphological evidence + retained specimen
    Retained specimen 1 dried whole plant or typical part, tagged with the registration number When same-name-different-thing disputes arise, the physical specimen adjudicates
    Gathering site and season Location (matching the gathering fallow map, 46.8), month, phenology (before budding / flowering / fruiting) Corresponds to drift source three
    Part Root/bark/leaf/flower/fruit/seed/whole herb Same as left
    Preparation method One of 46.3's four lines + parameters (water amount, reduction ratio, infusion strength) Different preparation = different medicine
    Claimed use Who said it, when it was said, and the original wording A claim is only a grade III lead; it is not evidence
    Evidence grade I/II/III (46.2.2), with the page reference of the current basis attached The switch for medication decisions
    Registrant and date Dual signature (registrant + medic) Chapter 43 archive accountability

    46.2.2 Evidence Grading: Medication Decisions Recognize Only Grades I/II¶

    Grade Definition Criteria (checkable) Permitted use
    I A local controlled record exists At least one 46.7.2-style controlled grouping completed, direction favoring the medicine, records dual-signed and archived Enters the procedure; routine use on the ladder
    II Multiple consistent observations ≥3 independent cases, different people and different batches, observation items pointing the same way, no poisoning records Use on the ladder under medic supervision; keep accumulating to rise to I
    III Single-case hearsay Oral claims, outside drug names, one-off improvement records Must not be given to people; may only queue to enter trials (46.4 ladder + 46.7 comparison)
    Prohibited A member of a 46.5.4 excluded family, or a local poisoning record Whole-family exclusion, or one credible poisoning Never used; its location is registered to prevent mistaken gathering

    The upgrade channel is one-way and open: III→II→I relies on accumulated records (the Chapter 47 case register is the data source); downgrading takes effect immediately: any single credible poisoning drops the medicine to Prohibited on the spot, with no waiting for review. Better to wrongly kill ten grade IIIs than to let one poison slip through — the same principle as 46.2.3.

    46.2.3 Poisons First: Why the Prohibited List Is Built First¶

    The engineering rationale for the ordering: the cost of one lethal poison mixed into the medicine pile (a human life, plus the collapse of trust in every medicine thereafter) far exceeds the cost of ten effective medicines missing (falling back to the 46.6 basic care). This chapter's build-out order is therefore fixed: Week 1 — the family exclusion table copied out in full + local poison register ≥5 entries; Month 1 — the prohibited list copied and posted; only after that does any talk of "permitted" begin. The prohibited list is posted in two places: the medical office (for medicine-givers to check) and the gathering-team outposts (for gatherers to exclude on site), with drawings and registered locations.

    [Card 46-01 Medicine-Material Registration] (eight fields)

    1. Input materials: paper, ink and forms (Chapter 41); dried-sample bags (boiled cloth bag or clay jar); charcoal pencil; specimen press (2 thin boards + cord). Per entry: 1 page + 1 retained specimen (dry weight ≥10 g; for tincture lines a ≥50 mL retained sample sealed in a clay jar).
    2. Tools required: balance (Chapter 3, for dry and retained samples, balancing error ≤1–2% of load; no balance that day means no registration into the medicine stock); water clock or sundial (Chapter 3, for preparation time); magnifier (Chapter 24, for mould and insect damage).
    3. Sequence and control points: Read the urgency criteria first — in this order: (1) someone has taken a suspect material and shows acute signals (vomiting, numb lips or limbs, palpitations, abnormal pupils, laboured breathing) → rescue first, paperwork after: stop the medicine, seal the batch, work Card 46-03 and the six steps of 46.5.2, and back-fill the registration later; (2) the prohibited list is not yet established (the 46.5.4 family exclusion table not posted in both places — infirmary and gathering party — or fewer than 5 local poisons registered) → no new medicine enters the settlement and no new page is opened; (3) anything matching a poisonous-family morphological clue (compound umbels at a waterside, tuberous root with palmate deeply cut leaves, bell- or trumpet-shaped flowers, white latex when broken, unidentified seeds) → register its location per 46.5.4, place it on the prohibited list, do not gather, do not use, do not taste; (4) claimed use is oral tradition only → the evidence grade can only be III and it may not be given to anyone. Main sequence: (1) record the nine columns on site (name and local name, family/genus clues, retained specimen, collection site and season, part, preparation method, claimed use, evidence grade, registrar and date), ≥5 morphological clues, no entry if short; (2) number the collection site against the rotation map (46.8); (3) press or shade-dry the specimen the same day and label it with the registration number; (4) the medic reviews and dual-signs; (5) assign the registration number (med-site code-year-serial) and transcribe into the register sub-ledger (Chapter 43). Control point: no retained sample = invalid registration and no place in the trial queue; from then on every dose record, label and case note cites the registration number, never the name.
    4. Empirical parameters and calibration: retained sample dry weight ≥10 g, ≥5 morphological clues, shelf-life estimates (dried material 12 months; powders and pills 6 months; decoction same day; tincture 24 months) — all empirical. Calibration: quarterly spot check of registration pages; one case of "queued without a retained sample" sends the whole batch back for re-registration. This card registers only and gives no dose: this chapter gives no dose — follow the Chapter 46 medicine-material cards and trial increments (Card 46-02 ladder: start at 1/4 of the recorded amount, 1/8 for tinctures, and only grade I/II medicines are eligible).
    5. Observations to record: phenology on the collection day, companion plants, soil (wet/dry, colour), whether the specimen changed colour on drying, whether re-weighing on day 7 shows moisture regain — in the notes of the registration page.
    6. Pass / rework / scrap criteria: pass = nine columns complete + retained sample present + dual signature. Rework = fewer than 5 morphological clues (add them and re-sign). Scrap = retained sample mouldy or lost (registration void; the material goes through collection and registration again). Hard gate: one credible poisoning case demotes that medicine to prohibited on the spot, without waiting for review; a grade III material may not enter any dosing process.
    7. Common failures and troubleshooting order (by frequency): (1) a "medicine name" brought from elsewhere put straight into use → first check whether it has a registration number (no number, no name) → seal it as unregistered material and re-run registration; (2) after registration the origin cannot be found → first check whether the site code matches the rotation map → check whether the season column was left blank; (3) two medicines confused → first compare the retained specimens → then compare registration number and batch.
    8. L1 / L2 / L3 evidence: L1 = fill 1 sample registration page and self-assess; L2 = 3 real medicine materials registered to standard (retained sample present, dual-signed, re-checkable); L3 = the register sub-ledger runs continuously + quarterly spot checks with no "no retained sample" entries + 1 person brought through to register independently.

    46.3 Preparation and Storage: One Chain from Material to Medicine¶

    Preparation is the work step that fixes "registration number + part + season" into "repeatable batches." The principle in one sentence: changing any parameter (water amount, heat, infusion strength) means changing the medicine, and a new batch record must be opened. The four preparation lines each have their parameters, failure criteria, and label rules.

    46.3.1 Drying Line (continuing Chapter 10's dryness criteria)¶

    All fresh material is dried before it enters use as medicine — dry weight is the basis of the dose unit (fresh weight fluctuates too much in water content to be used for dosing). Drying method and criteria follow Chapter 10's food-drying line directly: spread thin (≤30 mm thick) in a shaded, ventilated place; no exposure to direct sun (chlorophyll and constituent drift, and the result cannot be attributed). Four dryness criteria (Chapter 10; only all four met counts as dry): a bite gives a crisp audible snap; it snaps when bent; the cut face shows no dark damp patches; no cool feeling against the lip. On the 7th day in storage, spot-check and reweigh (Chapter 3 balance) — anything that has regained weight from moisture is re-spread and re-dried. The moisture pass value for materials follows Chapter 10's dryness moisture table (herbs and leaves <13%, empirical value).

    46.3.2 Decoction Line: Water Amount and Reduction Ratio Must Be Recorded¶

    The decoction is this route's main dosage form (the lowest equipment threshold — ceramic pot + fire). The key to repeatability is not "boil it down" but recording the starting water amount and the final volume:

    • Mixing basis (empirical value; calibration method = re-verify after each batch record): 1 part dry material to 30 parts water (mass/volume, e.g., 15 g dry bark to 450 mL water).
    • Reduction ratio fixed at 3:1 (450 mL boiled down to about 150 mL); when the reduction ratio changes, the "one-dose amount" under the same registration number has changed.
    • Heat: after boiling, low fire (crab-eye bubbles turning on the surface, the material barely rolling without spattering); timing from full boil for ≥30 min (water-clock timing).
    • ◎ Reduction look-and-feel: reduce until the liquid surface stands 5–10 mm above the dregs and the liquid coats the spoon when stirred (the decoction leaves a visible film on the wooden spoon). Practice method: practice eyeballing the 150 mL liquid line with clear water 3 times. Misjudgment compensation: pre-engrave a 150 mL gradation line on the ceramic pot's inner wall; go by the line, not the eye.
    • Filtering: filter through 4 layers of boiled filter cloth, discard the dregs, and finish taking the decoction within the day; decoctions left overnight are scrapped without exception (no preservation means; spoilage is invisible).

    46.3.3 Infusion Line: Alcohol Infusion and Oil Infusion¶

    • Alcohol infusion (proto-tincture): 1 part dry material + 5 parts Chapter 23 distilled spirits (ignitable grade, ≥50%) (mass/volume), sealed in a glass bottle (Chapter 14), kept in a cool place ≥14 days, shaken once daily. Record yield and strength (starting spirit volume, final volume).
    • Oil infusion: mainly external use (chapped skin, dressing base). 1 part dry material + 5 parts cooked oil (Chapter 8 heat-processed fats and oils, cooled to room temperature), same method, ≥14 days.
    • Infusions carry higher risk than decoctions: alcohol extracts active and toxic constituents with equally high efficiency, so the infusion is always treated as a dosage form that "concentrates the drift" — only medicines already holding grade II or better evidence may enter the infusion line, and the dose ladder starts at 1/8 of the infusion rather than 1/4.

    46.3.4 Powder and Pill Line¶

    • Powder: grind dry material fine in a stone mortar, sieve through a cloth sieve (mesh aligned with the Chapter 23 sieves), weigh the powder on the balance and pack in doses.
    • Pills: medicine powder + binder rolled into pills; the binder is one of two — honey (stored sealed, unfermented, same standard as 44.9.5) or starch paste (Chapter 10 starch paste). Spot-weigh 5 pills per batch; max–min difference ≤20%; an out-of-tolerance batch is remade in full (uneven pill weight = uneven dose — this is the pill line's biggest silent failure).
    • ◎ Pill-rolling hand-feel: start at powder to honey 2:1 (by mass); roll until neither sticky nor cracking. Practice method: roll 30 pills from the same powder batch and weigh them. Misjudgment compensation: the spot-weigh data always governs.

    46.3.5 Failure Criteria and Label Institution¶

    Failure criteria (any one appearing means scrap; there is no "looks fine" exemption):

    Criterion Observable standard
    Mold Visible mycelium, mold spots, white bloom (Chapter 24 magnifier, or an oblique view under direct sunlight)
    Off odor Sour rot, rancidity, mustiness (compare against the retained sample and the new batch's odor)
    Discoloration Marked darkening or blackening compared with the retained specimen
    Insect damage Boring dust, insect holes, insect carcasses
    Moisture regain Weight gain at the day-7 reweigh (same method as Chapter 10); powder caking

    Label institution: one label per container (ceramic jar, glass bottle, cloth bag) bearing four fixed lines — registration number / preparation date / estimated expiry / batch number. Estimated expiry (empirical values, revised by re-verification): dry material 12 months; powder and pills 6 months; decoction the same day; infusion 24 months. Moisture-proof labeling: oil-soaked paper labels (Chapter 41) or branded ceramic tags; pencil writing does not smear when damp, ink writing needs an oil coating. An unlabeled container = an unknown substance; it may not enter any medicine and is sealed in place pending verification.

    [Card 46-03 Decoction Preparation] (eight fields)

    1. Input materials: dried medicine material (registration number present, the four dryness tests passed [bites brittle with a sound, snaps when bent, no dark damp patch on the break, no cool feel against the lip], none of the five failure criteria); clean water (Chapter 45 drinking-water line; decoction water meets drinking standard); boiled filter cloth, 4 layers. Substitutes and losses: none — the decoction line is never downgraded; missing material stops the line. Per-dose quantity: this chapter gives no dose — follow the Chapter 46 medicine-material cards and trial increments (Card 46-02 ladder).
    2. Tools required: clay pot (Chapter 11, with a 150 mL line ground inside); water clock (Chapter 3, checked against the noon shadow); graduated glass bottle (Chapter 14) or a lined clay bowl; balance for weighing the medicine (Chapter 3, balancing error ≤1–2% of load, monthly check on file).
    3. Sequence and control points: Read the urgency criteria first — in this order: (1) within 0.5–4 h of a dose: vomiting, numbness of lips or limbs, palpitations, pupils dilated or pinpoint, laboured breathing → stop the medicine at once, seal the whole batch, and work the six steps of 46.5.2 (stop → symptomatic care per Chapter 44 → record → retain evidence → prohibited-list assessment → visit every other person on the same batch that day); (2) unconscious, drowsy or convulsing → nothing by mouth, no emetic, roll to the side against aspiration (44.7.2); (3) ingestion ≤1 h ago, fully conscious, non-corrosive → only then consider an emetic (≤3 rounds); corrosives and oil fuels are absolutely barred. Main sequence: (1) weigh the dried medicine (balance, recorded in g); (2) add 30 parts water (1 part dried medicine : 30 parts water, e.g. 15 g dried bark to 450 mL) and record; (3) from the full boil, low fire ≥30 min (crab-eye bubbles across the surface, a gentle simmer that does not spit); (4) reduce to 3:1, judged by the 150 mL line on the pot wall, never by eye; (5) filter through 4 layers of boiled cloth, discard the residue; (6) take it all within the day — decoction left overnight is always scrapped; (7) the seven batch-record items: registration number / medicine weight / water volume / final volume / fire / time / preparer. Control point: changing any parameter (water, fire, steep concentration) makes it a different medicine and requires a new batch record.
    4. Empirical parameters and calibration: water 30 parts, reduction 3:1, low fire ≥30 min, ≤3 doses a day, ≤7 consecutive days (stop ≥2 days for observation after 7) — all empirical (parameter table). Calibration: compare each batch record with the response to it, compiled quarterly — when several batches give "same amount, different effect", check first whether the reduction ratio has drifted. The tincture line is separate: 1 part medicine : 5 parts spirit (≥50%), ≥14 days shaken daily, dosing from 1/8.
    5. Observations to record: amount and colour of foam before the boil, final colour of the decoction (against the retained sample), residue volume, measured final volume — in the batch record; the 1 h and 24 h observations after a dose go on the dose record sheet (46.4.5).
    6. Pass / rework / scrap criteria: pass = final volume within ±10% of the line + all seven batch-record items + used the same day. Rework = under-reduced (continue simmering to the line). Scrap = past the line (over-strength; never dilute to make up the volume — the proportions are now unknown, scrap and record the batch); left overnight (scrap); any one of the five failure criteria — mould, off smell, colour change, insect damage, moisture regain (scrap and seal the batch). Hard gate: any new symptom after a dose is attributed to the medicine until the record proves otherwise; there is no antidote on this route — the whole of management is stop + support + record, stated honestly, with no invented remedy.
    7. Common failures and troubleshooting order (by frequency): (1) effect unsteady batch to batch → first check the reduction ratio and final-volume records → check whether the material is the same registration number and batch → check the five failure criteria; (2) several people unwell after dosing → stop the medicine first (46.5.2) → check whether that day's batch had a new preparer → check whether a second new medicine was added at the same time; (3) rash, numb mouth or nausea on days 4–7 of continuous dosing → manage as accumulation toxicity (stop + prohibited-list assessment), not as "the illness getting worse" to be dosed through.
    8. L1 / L2 / L3 evidence: L1 = one full practice run with clear water, records included; L2 = 3 real decoction batches, every final volume within ±10% of the line with complete records; L3 = batch records bound continuously into a volume + quarterly re-verification + 1 person brought through L2.

    46.4 How Doses Are Set: The Dose Ladder for an Age Without a Pharmacopoeia (This Chapter's Core)¶

    46.4.1 Why No "Traditional Dose" May Be Copied¶

    Once 46.1's four drift sources stack, the true-meaning interval of any oral-tradition dose ("three qian," "one handful") is too wide to carry engineering value. This route therefore has only one source of doses: local ladder measurement. The recorded traditional quantity has a single use: capping the ladder — if the ladder climbs to the recorded quantity and still shows no effect, stop (46.4.2 rule 5); the recorded quantity is not a safety guarantee, still less a reason to dose higher.

    46.4.2 The Dose-Ladder Method (Four Levels)¶

    Preconditions: the medicine has passed registration (Card 46-01), passed the prohibited-list check, and been approved by the medic to enter trials. Trial subjects go through the 46.7.2 controlled process first; a patient starting treatment directly follows this ladder.

    Level Dose (dry weight or the equivalent decoction amount) Timing and observation Condition to step up
    Level 1 1/4 of the recorded traditional quantity Observe ≥2 h after taking (acute signal window 0.5–4 h, 46.5.1); no step-up the same day; record the 24 h response No acute adverse signals
    Level 2 1/2 of the recorded quantity Taken the next day; observe as above No adverse signals
    Level 3 3/4 of the recorded quantity Third day; as above No adverse signals
    Level 4 The full recorded quantity From the fourth day End of the line — never exceed the recorded quantity

    Five iron rules:

    1. An adverse signal at any level: stop the medicine immediately (46.5.2); the medicine is downgraded or prohibited; do not retry from one level lower.
    2. Stop stepping up once it works: if some level works, fix that level as the maintenance dose; do not chase "a little stronger."
    3. Stop at the top: if level 4 is taken 2 days running and still shows no effect, judge "no effect seen," stop, and record the negative result (Chapter 43) — it is equally precious.
    4. The ladder admits no skipped or merged levels: healthy trial subjects (Safety Boundary item 1) and patients use the same ladder; no one's life is trial consumable.
    5. New batch, new provenance, new plant part, new preparation line = re-walk from Level 1.

    When the recorded quantity is too small to weigh (e.g., a recorded quantity <2 g), use the equal-division method: divide the same powder batch into 4 equal parts to realize 1/4 (balance-paired halving done twice — the Chapter 3 balancing method); never fob it off with "eyeballing a quarter."

    46.4.3 Body-Weight Conversion and the Weighing Institution¶

    The adult dose ladder is based on a reference adult body weight of 60 kg (empirical value). Calibration method: weigh every adult in the settlement on the Chapter 3 balance (the person lying on a stretcher hung by rope from a tripod, balanced with weight stones on the other side), take the median weight, and transcribe it into the ledger — this settlement's "60 kg" must be replaced by the measured median, and from then on all doses are computed with the settlement's own number.

    Children (body weight <30 kg): dose = adult amount × the child's weight ÷ the settlement's reference weight; after conversion, still start the ladder at 1/4 of the converted amount, and only grade I/II medicines — grade III and trial-period medicines are never given to children; extreme caution required beyond the boundary. Infants (<10 kg) and pregnant women: trial-period and grade III medicines are prohibited by default; only a grade I medicine, and only when the medic judges "not using it is the more dangerous," is started by weight conversion at half dose, with dual-signed records.

    Body-weight data source: quarterly whole-population weighing (same calibration method), entered in the population roster (an extension of the status field of Chapter 45 verification item 7); a sick child is reweighed just before taking medicine.

    46.4.4 Maximum Daily Frequency and Consecutive-Day Caps (the Dumb Method Against Accumulation)¶

    Cumulative poisoning (46.5.1's third class) is the hardest poisoning on this route to detect — a dumb method, but it works:

    Cap Empirical value Calibration method
    Daily frequency Decoction ≤3 times/day; infusion ≤3 times/day; pills ≤3 times/day Re-verified against local poisoning records; any accumulation case tightens it
    Consecutive days The same medicine taken ≤7 days running Same as above
    Off-observation day After 7 full days the medicine must stop ≥2 days; observe for new symptoms before discussing continued use The record sheet has a mandatory "day-8 observation" column to enforce it

    46.4.5 The Dosing Record Sheet: Every Dose Recorded¶

    Institution: giving medicine without a record counts as not giving it — no, stricter than that: dosing without a record is forbidden. Sheet template:

    Date/time Registration no. + batch Person taking Weight Dose (g/mL + ladder level) Dose no. that day / consecutive day 1 h observation 24 h observation Recorder

    Three usage rules: one line per dose, no leaving it to "general memory"; the 1 h column records presence or absence of acute signals (vomiting, mouth numbness, palpitations), the 24 h column records efficacy observation items (fever fallen, pain reduced, no change); the sheet is archived with the Chapter 43 ledger, and key items are copied into the Chapter 47 case register.

    [Card 46-02 Dose-Ladder Card (with Record Sheet)] (eight fields)

    1. Input materials: medicine material that has passed registration and the prohibited-list check (registration number and batch present); blank dosing record sheets; first-aid preparation (Chapter 44 first-aider + first-aid kit).
    2. Tools required: balance + weights (Chapter 3, monthly check passed); water clock (Chapter 3); body-weight hanging-weigh rig (tripod + stretcher + weight stones).
    3. Operating sequence and control points: (1) verify registration number, batch, and label expiry date; (2) weigh the person (or check the quarterly weighing records); (3) Level 1 = 1/4 of the recorded quantity (equal-division method with balance pairing), ≥2 h observation after taking + 24 h follow-up, no step-up the same day; (4) step up through 1/2→3/4→full, one day apart each; (5) stop stepping up once it works, stop at the top, stop immediately on adverse signals; (6) one record-sheet line per dose.
    4. Empirical parameters and calibration method: the 1/4 start, 2 h observation, 7-day cap, and 3-times/day cap are all empirical values.     6. Pass/rework/scrap criteria: pass = the four levels walked through, or step-up stopped on effect partway, with no missing lines anywhere in the record. Rework = re-walk after a batch or provenance change (not a failure; it is the institution). Scrap (process failure) = skipped levels, dosing without record, or a missing 24 h observation — any one voids the entire trial case; start over and log a failure case.
    7. Common failures and troubleshooting order: "no response" → first check whether the recorded quantity itself is credible (drift source four) → check the batch reduction ratio → only then may "this medicine is ineffective" be considered. "Some response but unclear" → check whether the 1 h column was omitted → check whether a second new medicine was added at the same time (violating single-variable).
    8. L1/L2/L3 evidence: L1 = recite all four levels and the five iron rules + fill 1 sample sheet; L2 = fully walk 1 case under supervision (four days of records with no missing lines, dual-signed); L3 = record sheets run 1 continuous month with no missing lines + quarterly parameter re-verification on file + brings 1 person through L2.

    46.5 How Poisoning Is Judged and Managed¶

    46.5.1 Three Classes of Poisoning Signals¶

    Class Time window Observable signals Hardest part
    Acute 0.5–4 h after taking Vomiting, abdominal pain, diarrhea, numb lips or limbs, dilated or constricted pupils (compare against the person's normal), sweating heavily or not at all, labored breathing, pulse too fast, too slow, or irregular (compare resting 60–80 beats/min) Easily confused with the original illness's symptoms — anything newly appearing after taking is treated as poisoning first
    Chronic Weeks to months on end Weight loss (quarterly weighing data), skin discoloration (yellowing, pigmentation), poor appetite, fatigue, numb hands and feet No single event to point to; trace "who is taking what" back through the register
    Cumulative After several days of consecutive taking Nothing wrong while taking it day after day, then new symptoms on days 4–7 (rash, mouth numbness, edema, nausea) Easiest to miss — usually taken as "the illness worsening" and the medicine continued (failure-mode table row 1)

    General reading rule: any new symptom on the medication timeline is attributed to the medicine by default, until records prove it unrelated.

    46.5.2 The Immediate-Stop Principle and the Management Sequence¶

    The first action is always stopping the medicine, not debating "whether it is the medicine." The management sequence is fixed at six steps: (1) stop the medicine (seal and quarantine the whole batch); (2) symptomatic care per the Chapter 44 first-aid procedure (airway, circulation; an unconscious person takes nothing by mouth — same rule as the Chapter 48 safety boundary); (3) record (timeline, dose, batch, symptoms); (4) preserve evidence (remaining medicine, vomitus, the first urine, sealed and labeled in ceramic jars); (5) enter the event into prohibited-list evaluation (46.2.2: a credible poisoning drops it to Prohibited immediately); (6) check on every other person who took the same medicine from the same batch, one by one, that same day. This route has no antidote (C list) — the entire content of management is "stop the medicine + support + record"; say so honestly and do not invent an antidote.

    46.5.3 The Boundary of Induced Vomiting¶

    Induced vomiting is considered only when ≤1 h has passed since taking, the person is fully conscious, and what was swallowed is non-corrosive, and the performer must have Chapter 44 training. Boundary table:

    Vomiting may be induced Vomiting absolutely forbidden Reason
    ≤1 h after swallowing a plant medicine, conscious and cooperative Coma, drowsiness, convulsions Aspiration of vomitus kills (same reason as Chapter 44 Safety Boundary item 4)
    What was swallowed is non-corrosive Strong acids, strong alkalis, quicklime (Chapter 23) Second-degree burning of the esophagus (same lineage as 44.6.2's no-neutralization principle)
    — Oil fuels (Chapter 6) Vomitus inhaled into the lungs
    — Breathing difficulty, pregnancy Worsened hypoxia; miscarriage risk — stricter for pregnant women

    Method: drink 300–500 mL of warm water → stimulate the back of the throat with the corner of a boiled cloth or a feather → drink and stimulate again, ≤3 rounds, until clear liquid comes up; after vomiting, a charcoal-powder suspension may be taken (46.6). If vomiting cannot be produced, do not force it; record and proceed per 46.5.2.

    46.5.4 Highly Toxic Plant Families: The Whole-Family Exclusion Method¶

    Identification demands "confirming one species"; exclusion demands "striking out an entire family" — the latter needs no botanical training, only a few coarse morphological features. Whole-family exclusion rule: anything falling into a row below, however harmless its local name sounds ("earth ginseng," "wild celery"), is not gathered, not used as medicine, and not put in any mouth before it has passed registration + trials.

    Family (coarse class) Morphological clues (checkable on site) Exclusion rule
    Carrot-family hemlock types Compound umbel flower heads, hollow ribbed stems, a mild celery scent, mostly growing at watersides and in wet grassland Waterside "celery-like" plants are excluded whole-class from medicine and from the mouth (same lineage as the Chapter 10 section 10.1 edible-plant risk rules)
    Buttercup-family monkshood types Tuberous roots, palmately deep-lobed leaves, blue-purple helmet-shaped flowers Whole class prohibited, even for external application (transdermal poisoning)
    Nightshade-family belladonna/datura types Bell- or trumpet-shaped flowers, berries or spiny fruits, plants with a distinctive foul odor Whole class prohibited
    Spurge types (white-latex plants) Any broken part exudes white latex All white-latex plants go on the prohibited list pending verification, no exceptions
    Unknown seeds and ferns Oilseed-like unknown seeds, fern fiddleheads Not used as medicine; fern chronic toxicity has livestock records to go by

    Prohibited does not mean destroyed: register the poison's location (drawn and entered in the book, one copy in every gathering-team hand) rather than uprooting it — this both prevents mistaken gathering and preserves the physical material for later re-verification. Local screening execution: within the register's first month, screen the settlement's one-day walking circle row by row against the table above; registering ≥5 poison locations is an L2 acceptance item.

    46.5.5 A Plain Discipline for Drug Interactions¶

    With no interaction table, substitute discipline: no more than two medicines at once; add only one new medicine at a time; while a new medicine is being added, do not adjust old-medicine doses. This is Chapter 25's single-variable method translated directly — when a reaction appears, stopping each medicine in turn reveals which one it was. Taking two new medicines at once = when anything happens both are suspect, and the whole set of results is voided.

    46.7 The Limited List Against Pain and Fever¶

    46.7.1 Physical Cooling and Fever-Treatment Indications¶

    Rule first: fever is a signal, not an enemy. Do not force down low fever. The indications for fever treatment are only two: high fever (markedly hotter than a normal person by the back-of-hand comparison, or higher than past readings on the Chapter 27 thermometer) accompanied by agitation or delirium, or fever so high the patient cannot drink. When neither is met, the treatment is 46.6's water, rest, and observation records.

    Warm-water sponging: soak cloth in warm water (not icy — same lineage as the 44.4.3 cold-water procedure; ice constricts vessels and thereby blocks heat loss), wring half-dry, wipe the sides of the neck, the armpits, and the groin, 20–30 strokes per area as one pass; stop at the first shiver (shivering generates heat — the more you rub, the hotter they get); after stopping, cover with a thin blanket, wait 30 min, and reassess. Maintain fluids throughout. This method serves adults and children alike and is the first line of fever care, outranking every medicine.

    Fever-course recording (the observational basis for controlled records): from first presentation, a febrile patient's heat level (four grades: normal / mildly hot / hot / very hot) is recorded every 2 h by the back-of-hand comparison (or measured on the Chapter 27 thermometer), linked into the day's fever-course line. The line has three uses — judging whether this patient needs fever treatment (46.7.1's indications), serving later as the natural-course baseline for comparable patients (46.1.2 item 1), and carrying the time axis of any fever measure's effect (sponging, rubs, medicines). A "the fever broke" without a fever-course line is not evidence; it is an impression.

    46.7.2 Demonstration Case: Willow-Bark Decoction Walking the Four-Step Institution¶

    This section's deliverable is the four-step record itself, not the conclusion "willow bark works." Willow bark is used in traditional records against fever and pain; its constituent lead is a bitter principle (traditionally called salicylate-type substances) — a constituent lead only aids registration and does not constitute efficacy; until local evidence is built, it is grade III hearsay.

    Step 1: Registration (Card 46-01). Name: willow bark (local name transcribed verbatim + aliases); family/genus clues: tree or shrub, long slender pliant branches, flowers before leaves in early spring, catkins, mostly at watersides (≥5 items); retained sample: dried branch-bark specimen + 10 g dry powder; collection: the D3 riverbank willow stand, early spring before budding (when bark peels easily — the traditional collection period; phenology recorded), part = bark of second-year shoots; preparation method: decoction line (Card 46-03); claimed use: oral tradition "boil and drink when feverish illness brings body aches" (grade III; source and date transcribed verbatim); evidence grade: III — queued to enter the controlled trial.

    Step 2: Preparation (Card 46-03). The recorded traditional quantity converts to 5 g dry bark per dose (the conversion process is recorded: for the oral "one handful," a medium-sized hand grabs a full handful of dry bark strips, this is weighed three times, and the median taken — conversion is what kills the "handful" as a unit). Decoction: 5 g to 150 mL water, boiled on low fire down to 50 mL (3:1), taken in divided doses within the day. All seven batch-record items complete.

    Step 3: Dose ladder (Card 46-02). Level 1: 1.25 g (one quarter of 5 g dry powder by the equal-division method), 2 h observation + 24 h follow-up; then day by day 1/2, 3/4, and the full amount. ≤3 times a day, ≤7 days running, medicine stopped for day-8 observation — every parameter copied from 46.4, not one line missing.

    Step 4: Controlled record (46.1.2's three criteria). Febrile patients of the same period split voluntarily by lot: the trial group takes willow-bark decoction + basic care; the control group takes basic care only (water, rest, rehydration, warm-water sponging — identical in both groups). Observation items are the objectively recordable ones: time to fever fall (the first moment it drops to what the patient self-reports as tolerable), time until able to eat, number of vomiting episodes; "feels better" goes only into the notes, not the main table. The open-trial limitation is transcribed verbatim (the patients know they are drinking medicine). Each case is dual-signed and archived (Chapter 43), copied into the Chapter 47 case register.

    Conclusion box (copied verbatim into the registration page): what this case establishes is the institution and the record. The verdict on willow-bark decoction's efficacy will be answered by every controlled record accumulated afterward — until grade I evidence appears, the evidence-grade field of the registration page reads "III/II, under trial," and no word "effective" is ever written.

    46.7.3 Honest Declaration: Limited¶

    The mainstay of pain management remains 44.9.1's immobilization and cold water; the mainstay of fever care is warm-water sponging and rehydration; even if a plant medicine rises to grade I, it is only "one more means" and will not change a world without antibiotics or strong analgesics. Writing limited means down as limited saves more lives than inflating them — inflated means get used on patients beyond their capability.

    Mold spots on the medicine pot yet it is still used Drying below standard or moisture regain; the five failure criteria not enforced Naked eye + magnifier (Chapter 24) compared against retained samples Scrap and seal the whole batch; check on everyone who took it, one by one Four dryness criteria + day-7 reweigh + expiry date on the label
    A "drug name" brought from elsewhere goes straight into use Same name, different thing (drift source one) Check the registration number — no registration number means no name Seize as unregistered material and re-run registration All medication recognizes the registration number, never the name
    A child dosed at "half the adult amount" No weight conversion done Check the record sheet's weight column Stop and observe immediately; reconvert and re-walk the ladder Mandatory weight column + quarterly weighing institution
    An unconscious person force-fed medicine or water, or made to vomit Violating the Chapter 44 safety boundary Consciousness assessment Stop at once; side-lying position to prevent aspiration, 44.7.2 Nothing by mouth for the unconscious is an absolute rule
    Two medicines taken on a trial day Violating single-variable discipline (Chapter 25) Check the "other medicines taken concurrently" column Stop both medicines; log the event as a failure case The ≤2-medicine cap, one new medicine at a time
    Improvement after taking an "unverified" medicine spreads as "miraculous" Mistaking self-healing for efficacy Check whether a controlled record exists Hearsay stays hearsay: the registration page's grade field must not be altered Evidence grading recognizes only I/II
    An alcohol rub used to bring down an infant's fever Exceeding the 46.6 boundary Ask the performer Stop the rub at once, substitute warm-water sponging, watch for signs of alcohol intoxication The first line for children's fever is warm-water sponging
    The decoction "works one batch, fails the next" Reduction-ratio drift, or a batch change without re-walking the ladder Check final volume and registration number against the batch records Suspend the medicine; reconsider once the batch is clarified The line governs + re-walk on batch change

    Safety Boundary¶

    Hard-gate restatement (implementing the Part declaration): wherever a criteria section of this chapter's medical cards writes "beyond the boundary," all hands stop; the only management left is warming/rehydration/analgesia/accompaniment/recording (see the Chapter 44 Part declaration).

    The following items are part of the process steps (Iron Law Seven); not executing them counts as not completing the process step:

    1. Trial-subject institution: only healthy adults may take first doses in trials; voluntary, informed, marked with a signed pledge; one person, one medicine (no other medicines during the trial period, no alcohol, no second medicine trialed); on every trial dose a Chapter 44 L2 first-aider and a monthly-inventory-passed first-aid kit are on hand; no skipped ladder levels; any new symptom means stop and record. The fasting, those newly recovered from serious illness, and those worn out by days of labor do not serve as trial subjects.
    2. Until the prohibited list is built, the settlement takes in no new medicine — stop the poisons first; talk medicine after.
    3. Children and pregnant women: extreme caution required beyond the boundary. Children take only grade I/II medicines, weight-converted, still on the ladder; pregnant women by default take no trial-period or grade III medicines, and exceptions require the medic's dual signature.
    4. Nothing by mouth and no induced vomiting for the unconscious or convulsing (same rule as Chapter 44); for swallowed strong alkalis or acids, no induced vomiting and no neutralization.
    5. Alcohol's three dead lines: half-drunk suffices; alcohol rubs for children prohibited by default; ≤3 consecutive days of oral use.
    6. No dosing without a record; unlabeled containers sealed in place pending verification; overnight decoctions scrapped.
    7. Medicine-garden poison isolation: poisons are never planted inside the garden; garden signs and registration numbers correspond one to one.
