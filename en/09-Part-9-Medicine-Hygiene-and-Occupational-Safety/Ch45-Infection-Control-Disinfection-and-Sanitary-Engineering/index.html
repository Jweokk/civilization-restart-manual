    Chapter 45: Infection Control, Disinfection and Sterilization, and Sanitary Engineering¶

    Chapter Objectives¶

    After reading this chapter, a settlement should be able to produce four documents and four facilities; missing any one means this chapter is not finished:

    Deliverable Content Use
    Three-tier cleaning–disinfection–sterilization system Tiered handling procedures for instruments and utensils + observable criteria Making sure "what enters a wound" and "what washes dishes" are never held to the same standard
    Drinking-water treatment line Water source tiering + isolation distances from pollution sources + the sedimentation–sand-filtration–boiling production line Cutting the largest transmission route, water to mouth
    Sewage and vector-control facilities Latrines, composting yard, refuse points, rat-proofing and fly-proofing devices Sending excrement back to the field, not back into bellies
    Epidemic isolation plan + sanitary inspection registers Activation criteria, isolation-hut procedures, five-item inspections, case registration Stopping the twentieth case after the first one

    Capability Boundaries¶

    What can be done:

    • Establish the three-tier cleaning–disinfection–sterilization system, with a hand-checkable observable criterion at every tier (protein-coagulation indicators).
    • Build the drinking-water treatment line (sedimentation, sand filtration, boiling) and work out the fuel-cost account for boiling.
    • Build the sewage system (three latrine types, seepage prevention, dead-animal disposal) and the ten-day inspection regime for vector control.
    • Establish the isolation procedures and the five-moments handwashing system, and keep the case register — this is Chapter 47's only raw data.

    What cannot be done (declared honestly):

    • Pathogen identification. We do not know the enemy's name, only that it lives in feces, lives in water, and dies in heat. This chapter therefore does not distinguish "is this dysentery or cholera"; it sets its defenses purely in symptom language (vomiting and diarrhea, fever, rash).
    • Vaccines, antibiotics, and biological agents — the G01 exclusion table explicitly provides no manufacturing route; the recovery conditions (microscope + aseptic technique + strain collection + assay system) are bounded in Chapters 44–46. Without these four things, this chapter's system is the settlement's entire firepower against epidemic disease; there is no second line of defense.
    • Direct testing of effectiveness. Whether disinfection or sterilization has "killed everything" cannot be assayed; only indirect criteria exist (indicators, timing, case counts). Every criterion here is a lower-bound proof, not a full proof.

    External dependencies (counted in the C list):

    1. Microscopes and pathogen-testing systems (cannot be manufactured on this route; see G01).
    2. Scale production and concentration testing of disinfectants (alcohol, soap, and lime have small-scale routes in Chapters 23 and 13; scaling up is in Chapter 46).
    3. Chemical testing of water quality (turbidity can be replaced by a visual criterion; chemical contamination cannot).

    45.1 Cleaning, Disinfection, Sterilization: The Three-Tier Distinction¶

    The general principle in one sentence: cleaning removes soil, disinfection kills most living microbes, sterilization kills everything, spores included. The tiers progress in order and cannot be skipped: without cleaning, disinfection and sterilization both lose most of their force — organic matter (blood, feces, food scraps) wraps up the living things inside it, and neither heat nor chemicals can reach in. The procedure is therefore always: wash first, then disinfect, and sterilize only when needed.

    45.1.1 Three-Tier Definition Table¶

    Tier Definition Means (on this route) Applies to Hand-check criterion
    Cleaning Physically removing soil and most microorganisms with water and cleaning agents (soap, ash water, sand) Scrubbing under running water, soap washing, ash-water washing, sand rubbing The first step for all utensils, floors, clothing, and bodies No stain visible to the eye; the water film on the vessel wall is even and does not bead (beading = an oil film remains); a finger run through the crevices comes away without black grime
    Disinfection Killing most vegetative microorganisms; spores not guaranteed Boiling for several minutes, alcohol wiping, fumigation smoke, sun exposure Tableware, chamber vessels, a patient's surroundings, clothing, hands Temperature (or concentration) and contact time both met (see each means' subsection); missing either, treat as not disinfected
    Sterilization Killing every microorganism, spores included Intermittent steaming (3 days in a row); flame burning (surfaces of small metal items — not sterilization) Deep surgical instruments, childbirth instruments A record of 3 consecutive days of steaming + the indicator coagulated (45.1.3)
    High-level disinfection Killing vegetative forms and most viruses; unreliable against spores Held at a rolling boil ≥20 min Ordinary instruments and tableware; deep procedures are forbidden when this tier cannot be reached Indicator coagulated + timing met + water level never fell below the instruments

    45.1.2 The Means, Taken One by One¶

    Boiling — the mainstay of this route. Instruments submerged one finger-joint below the surface, held ≥20 min from the start of the full rolling boil (an experience value; calibration in Card 45-02). The fire of Chapter 6 plus the ceramic pot of Chapter 11 executes it, with no prerequisites. Honest boundary declaration: 20 min of boiling is high-level disinfection — it kills vegetative forms but is unreliable against tetanus, botulism, and gas-gangrene spores (boiling water at atmospheric pressure does not sterilize); "absolute sterility" cannot be proven by any test available here — so for every instrument that enters a wound, add two disciplines on top of the full boil: taken fresh at the moment of use, never touches the ground, never kept overnight; anything that touches the ground or sits overnight is reworked and reboiled without exception.

    Intermittent steaming — the upgraded answer to spores. Steamer-basket style: bamboo or wooden baskets seated on a ceramic pot, steamed over a fierce fire ≥30 min a day, 3 days in a row, the load held at room temperature between steamings. Principle: the first steaming kills the vegetative forms; surviving spores germinate into vegetative forms during the holding period, and the second and third steamings kill them one after another — using time to force "invisible spores" into "visible vegetative forms that can die." Applies to: cloth-wrapped dressings and batch processing of heat-tolerant instruments. Criterion: at each steaming, the indicator block at the center of the cloth bundles in the basket coagulates (45.1.3) + the timing is met.

    Flame burning. Small metal items (needles, probes) heated in an open flame until red-hot, then used once cooled. Only for small cross-sections; burning knives and scissors anneals them and ruins the edge (Chapter 19) — forbidden; they still go through boiling.

    Alcohol (distilled spirits, Chapter 23) — a disinfection-tier agent only, never a sterilization-tier one. Two limits declared honestly: first, it does not work on spores; second, concentration is critical — experience range 60–75% (by volume). Too high (>80%) and surface proteins coagulate at once, laying a shell over the layers beneath; too low and it cannot kill all the way through. Rough field test (when the alcohol cannot be gauged): light a spoonful — if it will not light = too weak (about <40%), unusable as a disinfectant, reassign it to other uses; if it lights with a blue-white flame and little water is left in the spoon afterward = roughly up to standard. This test judges only the lower bound, never the exact value; when conditions allow, take the middle fraction of the Chapter 23 distillation and register the concentration grade. In use, keep the surface wet ≥1 min; if it evaporates fast, reapply. Never pour it into a deep wound — the tissue damage outweighs the benefit; wound irrigation follows the Chapter 44 procedures.

    Fumigation smoke — an experience-based means, limits declared honestly. Smoke suppresses surface flora somewhat, but it cannot be quantified, cannot be verified, and penetrates poorly. Auxiliary only (in the terminal treatment of an epidemic house, together with ventilation and sun exposure); it must not replace boiling and steam; whenever written into the system it always ranks last.

    Sun exposure. Direct sun on a clear day ≥4 h (experience value), turned over once; overcast hours do not count toward the total. Applies to: bedding mats, quilts and clothing, sleeping mats — the only routine treatment for bulky items, and a fixed step after isolation is lifted.

    45.1.3 Indicators: Fitting an Observable Eye onto "Did the Temperature Get There"¶

    Without a thermometer, neither "how long has the water boiled" nor "did the steam get through" can rest on impressions. This route's self-devised criterion is the protein-coagulation indicator: a fingertip-sized piece of starch paste or egg paste, tied up in cloth, boiled or steamed together with the instruments. Starch gelatinizing and egg paste coagulating proves the temperature at that point crossed the protein-denaturation line of about 70 °C. The evidence chain for sterilization temperature is three items as one: the indicator coagulated + the timing (counted from the full rolling boil) + the water level never fell below the instruments.

    The reasoning and the limits of this criterion must be written out (the standing rule for self-devised observable criteria): coagulation proves only that "the denaturation temperature was reached at some point," not that "it reached 100 °C," and still less that "it held for 20 min" — the other two are supplied by watching for "the whole pot rolling in big waves" and by the clock. The indicator witnesses that the temperature arrived; the clock witnesses that the duration was served; release only when both witnesses are present. Either one absent, treat as sterilization not achieved (that tier = high-level disinfection; deep procedures may not claim sterility). The indicator must be tied at the center of the instrument bundle (the hardest place for heat to reach); an indicator tied on the outer layer does not count even if it passes.

    ◎ Judging whether the egg paste has coagulated "through to the core" is a hand-feel step. Practice method: boil egg-paste blocks in a pot of plain water, taking blocks out when the water is about to boil, at the first boil, 10 min into the full rolling boil, and 20 min into the full rolling boil; observe the cross-section color and the depth of coagulation; practice 20 times to build a comparison memory. Misjudgment compensation: keep the physical indicator block from every sterilization batch, labeled with its ledger number (Chapter 43); a batch whose coagulation is poor is reworked as unsterilized — releasing it because "it looks about right" is not allowed.

    2. Scrap = indicator not coagulated (reboil the whole batch; if it fails twice, check the lid for steam leaks and whether the first boil was mistaken for the full boil). Hard gate: for any instrument entering a wound, 20 min of boiling is high-level disinfection only and is unreliable against tetanus, botulism and gas-gangrene spores — never claim sterility; where the sterilization tier is required, use the steam-intermittent method (3 consecutive days).
    7. Common failures and troubleshooting order (by frequency): (1) instruments were boiled, the wound is still infected (links to the 44.9.2 escalation criteria) → first check whether they touched the ground or sat overnight after boiling → check whether timing started from the first boil by mistake → check whether they ever passed the cleaning tier (organic matter shielding); (2) knives and scissors rust after boiling → wipe dry at once and wipe on a thin film of oil, scald the film off in boiling water before use; (3) indicator tied on the outside yet judged "pass" → rebind at the centre of the bundle and reboil; an outside indicator does not count.

L1 / L2 / L3 evidence: L1 = one complete sterilization batch with records and the physical indicator kept; L2 = 3 consecutive batches with every indicator coagulated and complete timing records, including one executed instance of "left overnight after boiling, therefore reworked"; L3 = quarterly ledger statistics with no missing entries + one apprentice brought through to execute independently to standard + the interface by which this chapter's instrument-related infections feed the Chapter 44 infection-rate statistics.

    45.2 Water Source Protection and Drinking-Water Treatment¶

    The review subject table's warning for this section is "mistaking clear water for safe water". Clear water and safe water are two different things: sand filtration strains out the turbidity you can see, not the living things you cannot. This section's production line is therefore three stages in series — sedimentation to cut turbidity, sand filtration to raise quality, boiling to kill — the first two stages save fuelwood for the third, and the third is the real line of defense.

    45.2.1 Water Source Tier Table¶

    Tier Type Normally drinkable straight after filtering? Treatment required Protection required
    I Springs with an outflow; deep wells (water surface ≥3 m below ground and the well wall sealed) Yes Add boiling when the water turns turbid in the rainy season Fencing, a cover, daily patrol upstream of the intake point
    II Shallow wells No Filter + boil Well platform 0.3 m above ground, with a curb, a well cover, and a dedicated communal bucket
    III Rivers and lakes (draw from flowing stretches) No Must filter, must boil Intake upstream of the sewage outfall, ≥50 paces (about 35 m) between them
    IV Stagnant ponds and water pooled in hollows Drinking forbidden Washing, livestock, and irrigation only Dumping any filth into them is forbidden

    45.2.2 Pollution-Source Isolation Distance Table¶

    Experience values, and they must be field-calibrated; the calibration method is the soul of this table — the salt tracer: dissolve 5–10 kg of table salt at one go into a suspect seepage pit (a latrine, livestock-pen drainage), then for 7 days taste the intake water and the downstream well water day by day (the taster must boil the water and let it cool through, use a dedicated spoon, one mouthful a day). The water turns distinctly salty = the groundwater is connected; abandon that pollution source or that well at once. The saltiness itself is harmless, but a connection proves the fecal water has a road to travel — this is precisely the developing fluid that makes "the invisible fluid leak" show itself.

    Pollution source Distance from intake point Additional conditions
    Latrines, seepage pits ≥30 paces (about 20 m; measured with the marked pole, no eyeballing), and always on the downstream side of the groundwater flow
    Livestock pens, composting yards ≥30 paces Drainage ditches carry the runoff away; no spreading flow
    Refuse dumps, ash heaps, animal-slaughtering waste points ≥30 paces Enclosed, so rain cannot wash them into the runoff
    Laundry, vegetable-washing, and bathing wastewater Carried away in open ditches Must not discharge upstream of the intake stretch

    Common knowledge on the direction of groundwater flow (how to judge it without instruments): groundwater generally moves toward low ground, following the surface slope and the lie of the valleys; wet ground and the places that seep first after rain are downstream. When two sites are in doubt, defend on the assumption that they "may be connected," and always take the upper limit of distance.

    45.2.3 The Treatment Line: Sedimentation → Sand Filtration → Boiling¶

    • Sedimentation: turbid water into a vat, left to stand ≥4 h (overnight is better). Criterion: half a spoonful of the upper clear layer held to the light shows no suspension, and stirring raises nothing from the bottom. In the rainy season, high-turbidity water first passes an assisting-settling vat of wood ash or alum (if available, Chapter 23) — with no agent, extend the settling to a full night.
    • Sand filtration: cuts turbidity and traps most suspended matter, saving fuelwood for the boil. Sand filtration does not sterilize — after filtering, whether to boil is still decided by the water source tier; this sentence must be copied onto the wooden placard beside the filter vat.
    • Boiling: the main force against microbes. A full rolling boil is enough (no need to boil it long); cooking water is taken care of along the way with the cooking.

    45.2.4 Principle and Maintenance of the Sand Filter Vat (Slow Filtration)¶

    Layer order top to bottom: fine sand 40–50 cm → coarse sand 8–10 cm → pebbles (jujube-sized) 8–10 cm → outlet (wrapped in cloth). The charcoal sits in a separate small vat placed upstream (charcoal layer 15–20 cm; regenerated monthly: baked over an open flame until smokeless), its job being to strip odor and part of the impurities — charcoal does not sterilize, declared honestly. The place where slow filtration truly works is the brown sticky "filter skin" that forms on the top 2–3 cm of the fine sand after a few weeks of running: it is alive, the water level must be kept ≥5 cm over the sand surface, and the filter bed is never left exposed to air and never allowed to dry out. Cleaning only scrapes the top 1–2 cm; turning over the whole vat is forbidden — carrying the filter skin down to the bottom is putting everything it intercepted back into the water.

    Empirical criterion for the filtration rate: after you stop adding water, the water level at the vat surface drops ≤1 finger-width (about 2 cm) per quarter-hour (15 min). Dropping too fast = short-circuiting or a broken layer; slowed to half = the top layer is due for scraping. Output capacity: a vat of 0.4 m inner diameter filters 150–300 L a day, drinking water for 50–100 people; a 200-person settlement needs 3–4 vats in parallel, or one big wooden-cabinet filter basin.

    ◎ Judging the scraping depth of the filter layer is a hand-feel step. Practice method: on a scrapped vat, practice scraping until you have the feel of "sand color going from black-brown to light yellow," checked against depth measured with a marked wooden rule 10 times. Misjudgment compensation: record the scraped depth of every scraping in the ledger; stop at once past 3 cm and hunt for the source of the turbidity.

All empirical; revised against the output ledger and turbidity spot checks.

    6. Pass / rework / scrap criteria: pass = output clear in a full bowl against the light, bottom carving readable, filtration rate within limits. Rework = turbid output (filter skin not yet formed in the first ten-day period — extend the discard period; if short-circuiting, re-tamp along the wall and reload); very slow output (scrape the top 1–2 cm; if the scrape exceeds 3 cm, stop and trace the source of the turbidity). Scrap = the vat cracks and leaks, or the sand bed turns black and foul throughout (wash and reload the whole bed, run first-filtration as for a new vat). Hard gate: filter output never replaces boiling under any circumstances; the all-boil policy of 45.2.6 is unaffected by the state of the filter.
    7.     45.2.5 Engineering Protection of the Well¶

    The well is the settlement's most expensive water asset. The four-piece protection set: well platform (0.3 m above ground with a curb, keeping surface runoff out of the well), well cover (kept shut whenever water is not being drawn), communal bucket (a dedicated drawing bucket whose rope never touches the ground — private buckets and private ropes are strictly barred from the well; one dirty bucket bottom contaminates the whole well), and drainage ditch (leading the flow away beyond the curb, discharging downstream). The household on duty self-checks the platform each time it draws water; the hygienist inspects every ten days (45.9).

    45.2.6 The Fuel-Cost Account of Boiling (connects to Chapter 38)¶

    "How much firewood is burned for every mouthful of boiled water" must be computed before the policy is set. The arithmetic and the order-of-magnitude check:

    • Heating 1 L of water from room temperature (about 20 °C) to a full rolling boil takes about 335 kJ; fuelwood's heating value is about 14–16 MJ/kg, the open-fire stove's efficiency about 8–15%, so the useful heat is 1.2–2.2 MJ/kg.
    • Experience value: 1 kg of fuelwood boils 3–6 L of water (take the low end for a three-stone open fire, the high end for a stove with a firebox and stove ring). Calibration: burn it for real — a fixed charge of wood boils a fixed charge of water; weigh the wood and keep the account, and one ten-day period gives you your own stove's number.
    • Each person drinks 2–3 L a day; boiling everything converts to 0.4–1.0 kg of firewood per person-day (experience value). Set against Chapter 37's daily-life fuelwood of 2–4 kg per person-day: full boiling in an epidemic raises the fuel burden 15–30%.
    • Reasonable-range check: a 200-person settlement drinks about 600 L a day, converting to 100–200 kg of wood a day — roughly the wood of half a firing of an intermittent pottery kiln (Chapter 12). This order of magnitude says: full boiling is an epidemic-emergency policy, not a steady-state policy, and it must pass through the Chapter 38 energy ledger in advance.

    Normal times, Tier II–III sources Must boil after filtering The risk of shallow wells and river water is always there

    45.3 Sewage and Excreta Management¶

    Feces is a resource and a bomb: composted back onto the fields it is one link in Chapter 10's fertility chain, but the same pile harbors the settlement's entire population of intestinal enemies. This section's task is to give feces a one-way, controllable road: from the squat point to the composting yard, no step along the way may meet water, food, or hands.

    45.3.1 The Three Latrine Types¶

    Type Construction Applies to Key rules
    Pit latrine + wooden cover Pit dug 1–1.5 m deep; the squat point spans wooden beams, tightly closed with a wooden cover General purpose, starter choice One ladle of ash or dry earth over it after every use; when the fill comes within 0.3 m of the pit mouth, seal and bury it and dig a new pit
    Composting latrine (twin-pit alternation) Two pits side by side, each used 6 months; one in use while the other is sealed and maturing Settlements short on fertility and rich in labor May be cleared out only after ≥6 months sealed and the pile has heated (see the 45.3.3 maturation criteria); mature feces goes only onto field crops already standing, never onto vegetable plots eaten raw (worst-case defense)
    Chamber-bucket system Buckets indoors (for the old, weak, sick, and disabled; the rainy season; epidemics), collected daily by a designated person Supplementary type Buckets are lidded; after collection and emptying, rinsed with ash water and dried in the sun

    Three rules of squat-point design: a narrow squat opening (less exposed surface), a tight cover (the wooden lid weighted with a straw bundle or a stone, so flies cannot enter), and a raised earth platform around it with a drainage ditch dug (rainy-season surface runoff must not pour into the pit).

    45.3.2 Seepage Prevention and Siting¶

    • Pit bottom ≥1 cubit (about 0.5 m, measured with the marked pole) above the water table. Criterion: seepage on the pit walls in the rainy season, or water visible at the pit bottom = the distance is insufficient; stop use at once, seal and bury, and move the site.
    • Ram the pit walls with clay (the rammed-earth method of Chapter 13) to reduce lateral seepage.
    • Three siting checks: ≥30 paces from the intake point (the 45.2.2 table), on the downstream side of the groundwater flow, on ground above the rainy-season water line.
    • Run one salt tracer (45.2.2) before a new pit enters service — half a day of digging buys the settlement ten years of peace of mind.

    45.3.3 Fly and Maggot Control and the Compost Maturation Criteria¶

    Three moves against flies and maggots: cover (a tight lid flies cannot enter), ash (wood ash is both drying and alkaline — eggs cannot hatch, maggots cannot grow), and diligence (a weekly clean-out carried to the composting yard; no feces left overnight at the squat point). Criteria: on inspection the squat point shows no maggot clumps, no fly swarms, and no residue at the squat opening.

    Compost maturation criteria (they decide when feces turns from bomb back into resource): ① sealed ≥6 months; ② the core of the pile heated early in the sealing period — push a wooden stick into the pile core and pull it out half a day later; a stick tip hot to the touch (too hot to keep hold of, about ≥50 °C) is evidence of heating; ③ when the pit is opened, no foul smell and a dark-brown crumbly texture. Missing any one of the three, keep it sealed.

    ◎ Judging the pile-core temperature as "too hot to keep hold of" is a hand-feel step. Practice method: mix stove-side warm water and boiling water into basins of different temperatures and practice the stick check against them 20 times. Misjudgment compensation: leave a stick in the pile for half a day, then press an egg-paste block against the tip; the egg paste visibly thickening is proof enough of the temperature — the 45.1.3 indicator idea reused in this spot.

    45.3.4 Rainy-Season Plan and Dead-Animal Disposal¶

    Three things for the rainy season: before the rains, check each pit's fill level and seal-and-bury early anything over half full; overhaul the squat-point platforms and the drainage ditches; during flooding, latrines in the low ground are closed and everything switches to the chamber-bucket system.

    Dead-animal disposal (any death of unknown cause is handled as the worst case): no eating, no skinning or selling, no throwing into water bodies. Deep burial: a pit ≥2 cubits deep (about 1 m, marked pole); lime scattered over the carcass (Chapter 13), earth backfilled and tamped; ≥50 paces (about 35 m) from the intake point and ≥30 paces from dwellings. The burial site is registered in the ledger, and is neither disturbed nor cultivated for half a year. Handlers soap-wash and change clothes afterward (45.8).

    45.4 Vector Control (Flies, Mosquitoes, Rats, Lice)¶

    The order may never be inverted: environmental management first, physical means second, chemical means last. Using agents (smoke, poisoned bait) without removing the breeding grounds is bailing water out of a pool that keeps being refilled.

    45.4.1 Environmental Management: Cut Their Food and Their Nests¶

    • Standing-water removal (against mosquitoes): mosquito larvae develop only in still water; any still water stored more than 7 days is a hatchery. Every ten days, empty everything on the standing-water container list: broken jars, broken urns, scrap barrels, bamboo troughs, urn-lid hollows, tree holes, millstone hollows, roof water channels. What can be flipped is flipped over, what can be filled is filled with earth, and what must stay in use is covered or given fish (fish eat mosquito larvae, no chemical harm).
    • Refuse out of the house (against flies and rats): kitchen waste leaves the house every day — composted or deeply buried, never left lying overnight; refuse points are enclosed and cleared every three days.
    • Feces kept sealed (against flies): all of 45.3 is the main force of fly control.
    • Ditches kept clear: dead-water stretches are mosquito nurseries; keep the drainage ditches flowing.

    45.4.2 Physical Means¶

    Target Means Key points

    Flies Covers (food shields, latrine lids), hanging sticky strips Cooked-food stalls must be shielded
    Rats Rat-guard bowls under chest legs (a shallow water-filled ceramic bowl under each of the granary chest's four legs — rats cannot climb past a bowl of water; or smooth ceramic bowls set upturned as foot pads), board snap-traps, bamboo cages Granary boards tightly jointed, door bottoms sheathed in sheet iron; the bowl water kept shallow and wide, for the sake of small children

    • Smoke against mosquitoes (mugwort, weed smoke): repulsion first of all; fumigate the room before sleep, people leave first, and ventilate before re-entering. Declared honestly: it repels and does not kill, its effect has no criterion to measure, and it may not stand as the only means.

    45.5 Food Hygiene¶

    The cookhouse is the mouth shared by the whole settlement, and the mouth that can fell the most people in one stroke. Five rules:

    45.5.1 Raw–Cooked Separation¶

    Two boards, two knives, marked by carved notches: one board for raw meat, one for cooked food (notches carved into the side of the board; never swapped). Criterion: after the cooked-food board has cut raw meat, raw fish, or raw offal, it must be washed and scalded with boiling water before it cuts cooked food again; bowls and basins follow the same rule — a vessel that has held raw meat does not hold cooked food.

    45.5.2 Tableware Boiling Cycle¶

    Communal cookhouse tableware is boiled once a day (≥5 min at a full rolling boil); during epidemics, and in any household with a case of diarrhea, boiled at every meal. Personal bowls and chopsticks are washed and used by their owner, marked with a carved name; a patient's tableware is dedicated, boiled separately after use, and never mixed into the communal boiling vat.

    45.5.3 Kitchen-Worker Rules (the first personnel rule)¶

    Those with diarrhea do not enter the kitchen — the first personnel rule of this whole chapter. The other three: anyone with a festering hand wound does not touch cooked food; kitchen workers must soap-wash after the privy (the five moments of 45.8 bind kitchen workers equally); kitchen workers' aprons and cloths are changed, washed, and sun-dried every day. Self-reporting symptoms before a kitchen shift is a duty; a worker who conceals diarrhea and keeps on cooking is dealt with under Chapter 37's Section 37.6.2 accident response authority — any diner may halt their food-serving eligibility on the spot.

    45.5.4 Leftover Reheating System¶

    Leftovers are reheated until steam billows from the center and a rolling boil holds for 2–3 min before they are handed out (without a thermometer, "big rolling bubbles" is the standard; rather too hot than not hot enough). Congee, meat, and beans left from a previous meal or overnight are all reheated before distribution; anything off in smell or taste is scrapped and deeply buried, not fed to the livestock (worst-case defense: spoiled food is a risk to livestock too, and losing livestock is the heavier loss).

    45.5.5 Communal-Meal Poisoning Walkthrough: How One Banquet Fells Half a Settlement in 12 Hours¶

    Walkthrough table (for teaching; every row is a checkpoint that could have intercepted it):

    Time Event Checkpoint that could have intercepted it
    Afternoon of the day before Beasts slaughtered and ingredients made ready; the meat left overnight at room temperature in a big basin (night temperatures cannot check the breeding of living things in the filth) Ingredient-prep rule: only dry goods and vegetables the day before; the meat slaughtered that same day
    Morning of the day Meat cut on the raw board, then unwashed and unscalded, the same board and knife cut the cold platters Two boards two knives, raw–cooked separation (45.5.1)

    That night into the next morning Within 12 h, dozens vomiting and purging; many households down at once — all three checkpoints failed, nothing left to intercept
    The next day Half the settlement's labor down together: fields missing the farming season, furnaces without their watch (the Chapter 37 shift schedule broken), the caregiving hands sick themselves The institutional cost is cashed in here

    The conclusion is written permanently on the cookhouse wall: The communal meal is the hygiene system's stress test. Of the three checkpoints — prep, raw–cooked, reheating — any one holding cuts this outbreak off; when all three fail, felling half the settlement is not an accident, it is the standard ending.

    45.6 Ventilation, Damp, and Dwelling Hygiene¶

    45.6.1 Dwelling Ventilation: In Low, Out High¶

    The same geometry as the Chapter 12 furnace and kiln draft: air comes in low and goes out high. Dwelling practice: an intake under the door or at a low window, an outlet vent high on the gable. Criterion: hang a paper strip at the outlet mouth; wind makes it flutter; the flutter stops = the ventilation is dead — check whether the intake is blocked. In winter it must not be sealed up entirely — for the fixed vent, better small than absent; the paper-strip criterion applies all the same.

    45.6.2 Winter Heating and Carbon Monoxide Prevention (connects to Chapter 6 Section 6.8 and Chapter 28)¶

    Three iron rules: charcoal basins and fire basins do not go into bedrooms; after the fire is banked and the coals pressed down, the flue and the vents must be checked; several people waking at once with headache, nausea, and dizziness = a CO alarm — everyone out of the house and the doors open immediately. Symptoms easing after ventilation confirms it; no one re-enters until the fire is dead out. Flame color is a daily watch item: a stove flame gone yellow and sullen = not enough air; ventilate first, then add fuel. This rule joins onto the Chapter 44 CO first-aid procedure (44.7).

    45.6.3 Damp and Mold¶

    • Beds ≥0.3 m off the ground (raised on frames or stacked blocks) so the ground's damp cannot climb into the bedding.
    • Straw mattresses sun-dried monthly and turned every ten days; mold criteria: black spots, a moldy smell, damp straw stems that snap at one bend — seeing any one of the three means the whole mattress is replaced, and moldy straw is burned without leaving the compound.
    • Rainy-season clothes that cannot be sun-dried are not piled up to dry inside the dwelling (wet clothes hung in the house = damp circulating indoors); drying in the stove room's leftover heat comes first.
    • Ground damp-proofing and raised foundations follow the Chapter 13 building procedures.

    45.6.4 Daylight and Dwelling Density [Background]¶

    Historical experience: dwellings crowded, dark, and unventilated for the long term go with the spread of consumption (wasting lung disease) — this route cannot verify the mechanism; it is adopted as background knowledge, and its engineering meaning is carried out as usual: window area no less than 1/10 of the floor area (experience value, measured with a rule); windows turned south to win the sunlight; not many people sleeping in one room, bunks at least one arm-length apart; the sick and the old housed in sun-facing rooms first. Tagged [Background] = it does not enter the acceptance criteria; it does enter design and allocation.

    45.7 Isolation Procedures¶

    45.7.1 When It Starts¶

    Nearly all of isolation's cost is concentrated at one point: "starting late." Activation criteria table (fixed in writing; no debating it on the spot):

    Signal Criterion Action
    Single severe case High fever that will not break + rash, or violent vomiting and purging without stop Isolate that day; do not wait for a second case
    Clustering ≥2 cases with the same symptoms within 5 days and linked (same stove, same well, same house, same table) Isolate that day; report to the hygienist
    Waterborne outbreak Vomiting-and-purging cases appearing at several points within one day and night Isolate + seal the suspect water source and switch to the backup (the 45.2.1 tiers)
    Epidemic confirmed Any of the above + cases rising day by day Start the full Card 45-03

    Activation authority is devolved: any hygienist may start on the table, ratified afterward; the council may not veto it with "let us watch two more days" — the same logic as Chapter 37's Section 37.6.2 accident response authority: stopping draws no blame, concealment does. Empirical walkthrough (an order-of-magnitude statement, not a precise forecast): most epidemic diseases run a generation in 3–5 days; every day the start is delayed past the first case roughly doubles the next generation's cases — the price of "watching two days" is four times the patients.

    45.7.3 Contacts and Release Criteria¶

    Contacts (same house, same stove, caregivers) are registered on a roster and watched for 7 days (experience value; corrected against this settlement's measured interval from first onset to next onset, entered in the ledger). Release from isolation: after the fever is gone and every symptom has cleared, isolation continues another 2–3 days (experience value) — with no test available, better one day of isolation more. Settlement-wide release from epidemic standing: 7 days after the last case with no new case. On the day a release is granted, the patient's clothes and bedding are boiled in water, the bedding mats are sun-exposed, and the house is ventilated for 1 day before it is used again (smoke only as an auxiliary, 45.1.2).

    45.7.4 Handling Bodies and the Boundary of Mourning¶

    The bodies of the epidemic dead are handled by dedicated personnel: the mouth, nose, and limbs wrapped, spillage avoided while moving, soap-washing and a change of clothes afterward; buried ≥1.2 m deep with lime scattered, ≥50 paces from the intake point. During an epidemic, mourning is reduced: shared vigil meals are suspended, the collective grand encoffining is suspended; a name plaque records the dead and the rites are performed after the epidemic — write this sentence out plainly: this is the system, not heartlessness: one funeral can set off twenty, and twenty funerals can push a settlement from a lean harvest to the loss of a generation. The date of the rites to be performed later is announced publicly the moment the epidemic begins — leaving grief an outlet, putting a gate on gathering.

    45.8 Handwashing and Personal Hygiene System¶

    45.8.1 The Five-Moments Table (mandatory settlement-wide, doubled in an epidemic)¶

    # Moment The specific acts it covers
    1 After the privy Always wash on leaving the latrine; if there is no soap, ash water
    2 Before attending a birth The birth attendant washes hands and forearms to the elbow, dried where possible with a boiled cloth
    3 Before treating a wound Connects to Chapter 44: clean hands are the step before the first step of debridement
    4 After touching a patient After delivering meals, giving care, handling feces, handling bodies
    5 Before eating Including before preparing food; kitchen workers are held to the stricter 45.5.3

    45.8.2 Washing Method and Cleaning Agents¶

    Method: running water is best; without it, the three-ladle method — the first ladle rinses off the loose soil; soap and work up a lather, rubbing to a silent count of 100 slow strokes (about half a quarter-hour), working the webs of the fingers, the palms, and the backs of the wrists; the second and third ladles rinse it off. A family washing from one shared basin is forbidden — washing to the end out of one basin hands the first person's filth to the whole family. Cleaning agents come in two tiers: soap (Chapter 23) first; ash water as the substitute — 1 part wood ash to 5 parts water, stirred and settled (Chapter 8), and the clear liquid on top is what you use.

    ◎ Judging ash-water strength by feel (the clear liquid feels slippery like thin soapy water) is a hand-feel step. Practice method: mix one basin at 1:5 and one at 1:10 and use them against each other 10 times. Misjudgment compensation: judge by the emulsification test — ash water stirred with a little grease that emulsifies and whitens passes; if the oil beads still separate after stirring, it is too weak — add ash and mix again.

    45.8.3 Wound and Secretion Etiquette¶

    Wounds are covered with clean cloth and not left bare; wash hands after every dressing change (dressing procedures connect to Chapter 44); coughs and sneezes are covered with the inside of the arm, not the hand — the hand has food to hold and things to pass; the patient does not share the washing basin, the towel, or the bowls; sputum and mucus go into the ash crock — never spat on the ground, never upstream into the ditches.

    45.9 The Hygienist Post and the Inspection System¶

    45.9.1 Hygienist Duty List¶

    The hygienist is a post, not volunteer work; graded and assessed under the Chapter 37 Section 37.5.2 skill matrix (the hygiene branch of the medic post); a 300-person settlement is allotted 1–2 (of whom ≥1 reaches grade 2):

    1. Run, score, and post the ten-day vector inspection (45.4.4) and the weekly five-item inspection (45.9.2).
    2. First report of epidemics: start isolation on the 45.7.1 table, exercising the pre-authorization.
    3. Keep the case register — the sole data source of the Chapter 47 epidemiological investigation; a ten-day period missing is dereliction of duty.
    4. The sterilization ledger and the indicators (Card 45-02), the filter-vat ledger (Card 45-01).
    5. Hygiene teaching: the five moments, the communal-meal three checkpoints, the three CO iron rules — re-taught to everyone once a quarter.

    45.9.2 Weekly Sanitary Inspection Table (five items, scored)¶

    One fixed day each week; a 0/1/2 three-point scale, 10 points in all; below 6 issues a rectification order (deadline and re-inspector named):

    Item What is checked Pass criteria

    Latrines Cover, ash, maggots, fill level, location The 45.4.4 standards
    Kitchen Two boards two knives, tableware boiling, kitchen-worker symptom self-report, food shields All four checks pass; a worker with diarrhea on duty that day is an immediate 0
    Refuse Out of the houses, enclosed, hauled No spillage, no overnight kitchen waste

    45.9.3 Monthly Ranking and the Case Register¶

    The ranking is folded into the Chapter 37 organizational interface: households (or districts) are ranked by inspection score each month and posted; two months running at the bottom, the council calls them in for rectification — honor and rectification orders are used, not ration penalties (a ration penalty would turn concealment into the rational choice). The case register's fields are fixed: number, name, age, date of onset, main symptoms, dwelling, intake point, cook-stove, contact history, outcome. The register is transcribed once a month and archived (Chapter 43); it is the whole of the raw data for Chapter 47's diagnostics and epidemiological investigation — if this chapter does not register, Chapter 47 has nothing to consult.

    Safety Boundaries¶

    Hard gate restated (the Part declaration made concrete): wherever the criteria sections of this chapter's medical cards say "beyond the boundary," all hands stop at once; the only treatments left are warmth, fluid replacement, pain relief, accompaniment, and record-keeping (see the Part declaration in Chapter 44).

    This chapter's safety boundaries carry the same weight as the processes themselves (Iron Law 7); failing to execute any of the following counts as not finishing this chapter:

    1. The bodies of the epidemic dead and of dead livestock are handled by dedicated personnel, wrapped, with soap-washing and a change of clothes afterward — bare-handed handling and skipping the wash afterward are this chapter's highest-risk acts.
    2. The three CO iron rules (45.6.2), joined to Chapter 44's 44.7: several people with a morning headache means everyone out of the house; treatment before diagnosis.
    3. Rat bait out at night and gathered at dawn, numbered and registered; dead rats picked up with fire tongs and buried deep, bare hands forbidden.
    4. Lime work with eye and hand protection (the Chapter 13 procedures); ash water and lime slurry must not reach the eyes — if one does, flush at once with great quantities of clean water and transfer to the Chapter 44 chemical-in-the-eye procedures.
    5. Smoke work: people leave the house first and return only after ventilation; never performed in the same room as a patient.
    6. The salt-tracer taster: the water must be boiled and cooled through, a dedicated spoon, one mouthful a day, and the test registered when it is done.
    7. Rat-guard bowls hold shallow water and are not set in young children's activity areas.
    8. The boiling pot and the steam baskets are worked by grade-2 skill holders (the Chapter 37 grading); scalds are handled per Chapter 44.
