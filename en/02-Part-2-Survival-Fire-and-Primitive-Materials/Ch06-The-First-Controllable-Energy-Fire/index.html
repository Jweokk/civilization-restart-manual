    Chapter 6 The First Controllable Energy Source — Fire¶

    Part 2 · Survival, Fire, and Primitive Materials. Starting Point A standard: fire lighting may depend only on natural materials such as stone, wood, fungi, and minerals. The bow drill requires cord, whose full specification is given in Section 7.6; this chapter's official routes are the hand drill, fire plow, fire saw, and percussion fire lighting.

    Chapter Goals¶

    • What is established is not "being able to light a fire once," but a repeatable energy technology: fire lighting (friction and percussion routes) → ignition → stable heat source (coal-bed management) → fuel management (firewood grading, wood → charcoal conversion) → temperature and airflow control → safety boundaries.
    • Deliverables: each of at least two fire-lighting methods succeeding multiple times; one fire bed that can be banked overnight; one batch of qualified charcoal from a kiln; one combustion log.
    • This chapter is the starting point of the energy lineage (Lineage 3): fire → charcoal → furnaces and kilns. Its results directly support Chapter 8 (pyrolytic glue), Chapters 10–13 (cooking, pottery, furnaces and kilns, lime).

    Minimum Prerequisites¶

    Item Requirement Source
    Tools Stone-flake knife, scraper, hand axe, hard hammer, pointed flake Chapter 5
    Knowledge Fuel and heat-source resource survey, combustible identification Chapter 2
    Measurement Length and volume measurement, record sheets Chapter 3
    Materials Dry firewood, tinder materials (fungi, grass, birch bark), (optional) pyrite nodules Chapter 2 resource map
    Constraint No cord — the bow drill is not yet available Completed in Section 7.6

    Bow drill material-matching preview (configure this way when the bow drill unlocks in Section 7.6; v2 completion): the drill board uses straight-grained wood softer than the drill shaft (willow, poplar types — produces powder fast and gathers heat); the drill shaft uses hard straight wood (or a later-era steel rod); the bearing block uses hardwood with a soapstone/bone-plate socket (wear-resistant and low friction) — the three-part hardness mismatch (soft board, hard shaft, slippery bearing) is the hidden prerequisite of bow drill efficiency; an all-hard configuration only polishes and produces no powder.

    6.1 Natural Fire Sources and Combustible Materials¶

    Natural fire sources: lightning fires (patrol along the smoke line within 24 hours after a thunderstorm; approach from crosswind when closing in), margins of volcanic eruptions, and rare self-ignition of peat or coal seams. Iron Law: once you have an ember, the first task is keeping the fire, not using it — keep the ember alive and you need not strike fire again for a week.

    Two methods of keeping an ember:

    • Banked fire bed: heap the coals into a pile, cover with 5–10 cm of dry ash, press a thin layer of soil over the ash, and leave one finger-thick air hole. Next morning, pull it open and blow — it ignites.
    • Ember tube: roll thick birch bark into a tube holding a dark-smoldering piece of rotten wood (or a tinder-fungus chunk) together with semi-dry moss; leave air gaps; inspect every 2–4 hours and replace the chunk when the ashen, burnt-down part grows short.

    Four-tier table of combustible materials:

    Tier Representative materials Size criterion Function
    Tinder Cattail down, dry-grass down, thin inner-bark sheets of birch, pounded tinder-fungus fluff, dry bird and beast down A pinch can be kneaded into a ball Receives the smoldering ember and is blown into open flame
    Kindling I Fine dead twigs, pine-needle bundles Toothpick to chopstick thick Bridges from open flame to firewood
    Kindling II Small branches Finger thick Establishes a stable small fire
    Main fuel Branch and trunk wood Wrist thick and up Maintains the heat source, produces charcoal

    Dryness criteria: dry grass snaps with a brittle crack; inner bark held to the cheek feels not cool and not sticky; standing dead wood beats fallen wood (fallen wood regains moisture from the ground); after rain, all firewood is split open to check core color (dark = damp).

    6.2 How to Select Tinder Materials and Fuel¶

    Three qualification criteria for tinder (usable for formal fire lighting only when all three pass):

    1. Fully dry: no cool feeling against the lips (cool = moisture absorbed).
    2. Elasticity: kneaded into a ball and released, it springs back without collapsing.
    3. Pre-burn: shaped into a nest and gently blown for 5 seconds, it gives off a scorched smell or shows faint smoke.

    Tinder preparation:

    • Tinder fungus (the hoof-shaped polypore on standing birch and oak): cut away the outer crust, pound the inner flesh into fluff on a stone slab, air-dry and store in a bag. This is the first-priority tinder.
    • Dry grass: roll and rub it into fluff on a flat stone; remove the stems.
    • Birch bark: discard the hard outer skin; tear the inner layer into thin rolls matchstick-wide; it contains oils and remains flammable even in light rain.

    Charring preparation (v2 completion: "bake" the tinder before storing it): pack young-twig bast and cloth-grade plant fluff into a pottery jar (after Chapter 11) or bury in an ash pit; smother-roast over a low fire until evenly scorched brown throughout, igniting at a touch yet not self-igniting — when the color turns from yellow-white to brown-tan it has reached doneness (◎ judge by color; compensation: light a pinch as a test — sparks spreading into a patch without raising open flame passes). Charred fluff ignites at a far lower temperature than raw fluff and is the key intermediate layer for turning smolder into open flame.

    Smolder-to-flame transition (breath-volume control, v2 completion): after the tinder smolders and smokes, blowing is "pulsed," not "one long blow" — two or three short puffs (each on the order of a tenth of a lungful), with pauses that let the smoke recede and deepen; when the red spot inside the tinder has widened to cup-mouth size, wrap it in fine kindling and one long breath raises open flame. One long blow all the way through = blowing the heat away, and the red spot dies instead — "blowing the fire out" is not a metaphor; it is a real operational failure. Practice method: rehearse 20 times with scrap tinder and count the minimum number of puffs needed "from smolder to open flame."

    Fire-lay structure (fire nest): the tinder forms the innermost nest; toothpick-sized sticks form a cone, each outward layer growing coarser up to finger thickness; sticks within each layer cross loosely, never fitted tight, preserving air gaps; the cone's opening faces the upwind side and the lighting port stays on the lee side. Wrong sequence is the number-one cause of ignition failure: sticks too thick, gaps too dense, or an opening facing the wind that gulps air too hard — any one of the three will blow the flame out.

    Fuel grading management:

    Wood class Representative species Burning characteristics Use
    Softwood Willow, poplar, linden, pine Ignites fast, tall flame, does not burn long Kindling, quick fire
    Hardwood Oak, locust, elm Steady fire, good charcoal, long-burning Coal bed, charcoal making
    Resinous wood Pine knots, burls, old pine roots Fierce fire, moisture-resistant Embers, wet-weather kindling

    Reserve quantities (empirical values; must be field-calibrated): heating one person through one night takes roughly 15–25 kg of wrist-thick firewood; cooking one meal about 2–4 kg; double the reserve for the rainy season. Stack the woodpile raised off the ground and roof it with bark against rain.

    [Card 06-02 Preparing Charred Tinder] (eight fields)

    1. Input materials: inner bark of young shoots or plant down at rag grade (dry grass down, bast fiber both work); a clay pot (after Chapter 11) or a freshly dug ash pit; storage pouches. Raw and charred tinder must be labeled and stored apart — mixed together, you cannot tell a material problem from a firing problem when it fails to light.
    2. Tools required: clay pot or ash pit (deep enough to bury the charge), stone slab (for spreading to dry), an established ember bed (Section 6.5). Calibration source: readiness is judged by color plus a burn test, not by a thermometer.
    3. Sequence and control points: load pot or bury in ash pit → slow bake over low heat (never over open flame — a flame turns the batch to ash in seconds) → watch the color turn from yellow-white to brown → pull a pinch and test-light it. Control points: low heat throughout; both the color change and the burn test must pass — color alone runs under-charred.
    4. Empirical parameters and calibration: readiness = uniformly char-brown, ignites when touched to a spark but does not self-sustain; fallback when color is ambiguous = pull a pinch out, it should spread sparks in a sheet without breaking into flame (flame = under-charred; ash = over-charred). Bake time varies with material and fire position — no fixed minutes are given; judge by color plus test, and record this batch's time for the next person.
    5. Observations to record: per batch — source material / bake time / final color / burn-test result (sheet of sparks / flame / ash) / date bagged.
    6. Pass / rework / scrap criteria: pass = char-brown, burn test gives a sheet of sparks with no flame; rework = color short or test gives flame, return to heat in 10-minute steps, testing between; scrap = self-burned to ash (heat too high — redo the batch and note the fire position to avoid next time).
    7. Common failures and diagnosis order: (1) will not light → check for moisture first (is the pouch sealed), then under-charring; (2) turns to ash at a touch → over-charred, fire too close or too long; (3) half raw, half charred in one batch → uneven heating, pot packed too full (fill to seven-tenths).
    8. L1 / L2 / L3 evidence: L1 = one passing batch plus burn-test record; L2 = three batches from three different source materials, all passing, with times and final colors comparable; L3 = a second person produces a passing batch from this card alone, with matching burn-test results.

    [Card 06-03 Pulse-Blowing an Ember into Flame] (eight fields)

    1. Input materials: an established ember (black powder cake or a glowing spot in charred tinder); fire-nest material (toothpick twigs → fine twigs → finger-thick sticks, added in layers); tinder. Build the nest before you blow — blowing first and gathering wood after is a common way to lose the ember while it waits.
    2. Tools required: none beyond breath; a small reserve of tinder for re-seeding.
    3. Sequence and control points: two or three short puffs (each about one-tenth of a lungful) → pause to let the glow deepen → glow spreads to the size of a cup mouth → tuck fine twigs around it → one long breath brings flame. Two control points: blowing is pulsed, not continuous; do not add twigs before the glow reaches cup-mouth size (twigs added early smother it).
    4. Empirical parameters and calibration: about one-tenth lungful per puff; two or three puffs per set, resting two or three breaths between sets; cup-mouth (about a palm wide) is the moment to add twigs. Individual variation is large — practice method: run 20 trials on waste tinder and count the minimum number of puffs from glow to flame; record that number as a personal yardstick for judging ember quality.
    5. Observations to record: per attempt — puff count / pause length / peak glow diameter / whether the glow shrank at any point / time to flame after twigs went on.
    6. Pass / rework / scrap criteria: pass = flame established and nest intact; rework = glow shrinking (heat blown away), stop blowing at once, let it recover, restart the count; scrap = ember out, go back to Card 06-01 and record the puff count to find the cause.
    7. Common failures and diagnosis order: (1) glow shrinking → blowing too long or too hard, carrying heat away ("blowing the fire out" is not a metaphor, it is a real operating failure); (2) heavy smoke but no flame after adding twigs → twigs too thick or packed too tightly, rebuild the nest with air gaps; (3) flame pushed to one side → nest opening faces upwind, turn the opening upwind and keep the lighting gap downwind; (4) repeated failures → check tinder for moisture (back to Card 06-02 for a burn test).
    8. L1 / L2 / L3 evidence: L1 = one successful light plus puff count; L2 = after 20 practice runs the minimum count is stable (last five within 2 puffs); L3 = a second person succeeds from this card and the two counts are comparable (a wide gap means one of you has different tinder quality).

    6.3 Principle and Tools of Friction Fire Lighting¶

    Principle: friction power = normal force × sliding speed. The drill shaft rotates in the hole of the hearth board; the abraded wood powder accumulates in the hole; once the interface temperature rises to about 300–400°C (empirical value; must be field-calibrated), the powder pyrolyzes into black charcoal dust and begins to smolder, forming a glowing ember — no open flame. Move the ember into the tinder nest and blow to add oxygen; only when the temperature rises above about 500°C (empirical value) does open flame appear. The process therefore has four stages: friction → black-powder smolder → transfer ember into tinder → blow to flame — not "drilling out a flame."

    Hand drill method (this chapter's official route):

    Component parameters:

    Component Specification Material
    Drill shaft Length 40–70 cm, diameter 0.6–1.2 cm, straight and round Dry straight-grained medium-hard wood (willow, elder, etc.)
    Hearth board Thickness 1.5–2.5 cm, width about 5 cm, length 30 cm or more Dry softwood
    Powder notch V-notch on the board's side, deep enough to reach the hole's center Carved with the stone-flake knife

    Operation: stand the shaft in a shallow depression; with the palms pressed together around the shaft, roll it back and forth while pressing down; speed about 120–200 rpm, combined palm pressure 3–6 kg (empirical values; must be field-calibrated); as the hands reach the shaft's lower end, quickly return them upward, keeping the shaft seated in its hole; drill continuously for 1–5 minutes. Criterion chain: white smoke appears → smoke thickens and holds → stop and inspect the powder pile — black powder pressed into a cake and, with the light blocked, a dark-red glow visible = the ember is established.

    Failure troubleshooting table:

    Symptom Cause Remedy
    Powder brown, board hole polished Insufficient pressure or board too hard Add pressure; switch to a softer board
    Shaft slips Hole worn oversize Move aside and open a new hole
    Powder black but no glow Powder damp or drilling stopped too early Switch to dry material; extend the drilling run
    Shaft scorched and breaks Pressure too high Reduce pressure, raise speed

    Fire plow method (backup for hand injury or cold weather): cut a longitudinal groove in a softwood board; push a hardwood plow head rapidly back and forth along the groove; the powder is pushed together at the groove's end into an ember. Laborious; moderate success rate.

    Fire saw method (in bamboo regions): saw a thin bamboo strip rapidly back and forth inside a kerf of a dry bamboo board; the powder falls into a carved notch below and forms an ember.

    Upgrade preview: the bow drill (Section 7.6) drives the shaft with a cord bowstring — less effort and stable speed, with a success rate several times that of the hand drill (empirical value). Until cord technology is established, the hand drill is the mainline; hand-drill training must not be omitted from the records.

    [Card 06-01 Hand-Drill Fire Making, Full Sequence] (eight fields)

    1. Input materials: drill shaft (40–70 cm long, 0.6–1.2 cm diameter, straight and round, dry straight-grained medium-hard wood such as willow or elder); fireboard (1.5–2.5 cm thick, about 5 cm wide, 30 cm or longer, dry soft wood); tinder passing the three tests in field 4; birch bark or a broad leaf to catch powder. Shaft and board must be equally dry — one dry and one damp grinds the hole smooth without producing black powder.
    2. Tools required: flake knife (Chapter 5, to cut the V-notch), a spare fireboard (for when the hole wears oversize), record tablet. Calibration source: speed and pressure cannot be instrumented — infer from the result: black powder means the parameters are right, brown powder means pressure is short.
    3. Sequence and control points: seat the shaft in a shallow dimple → clasp between the palms and rub while pressing down → just before the hands reach the bottom, snap them back up (the shaft must not leave the hole) → drill continuously 1–5 minutes → stop and check the powder pile. Control points: the V-notch must reach the center of the hole (too shallow and powder will not exit, too deep and the shaft wobbles); never release pressure during the hand reset.
    4. Empirical parameters and calibration: about 120–200 rpm; combined palm pressure 3–6 kg; interface reaches about 300–400 °C where black powder forms (empirical, calibrate on site); decision chain — white smoke appears → smoke thickens and holds → stop and inspect, black powder caked together with a dull red spot visible in shade = ember established. Speed and pressure constrain each other: too slow usually means too little pressure, too much pressure and it will not turn; use powder color as the arbiter.
    5. Observations to record: per attempt — drilling time / powder color (black / brown / white) / smoke color and density / whether a dull red spot appeared / failure-mode number (field 7) / humidity that day.
    6. Pass / rework / scrap criteria: pass = caked black powder with a dull red spot in shade; rework = brown powder or a glazed hole, add pressure or switch to a softer board and redrill; scrap = shaft scorches and snaps (reduce pressure, raise speed, replace shaft).
    7. Common failures and diagnosis order: (1) brown powder and glazed hole → pressure too light or board too hard, add pressure first, then change board; (2) shaft skids → hole worn oversize, move over and start a new hole (do not fight the old hole); (3) black powder but no glow → powder damp or drilling stopped too early, dry the material and drill longer; (4) shaft scorches and snaps → pressure too high, reduce and speed up.
    8. L1 / L2 / L3 evidence: L1 = one ember obtained plus record; L2 = five consecutive successes including at least one in damp weather, with the success rate entered in the combustion log; L3 = a second person succeeds from this card alone, and both success rates and times can be compared.

    6.4 Principle and Applicability of Percussion Fire Lighting¶

    Principle: when pyrite or marcasite (both FeS₂, Mohs 6–6.5, pale brass-yellow with metallic luster, nodular or with cubic crystal faces, greenish-black streak) is scraped briskly against a sharp flint edge, the tiny iron-sulfide flakes peeled off oxidize violently in air and release heat, spraying out as incandescent particles — essentially "hot particles carrying their own oxidation reaction"; landing on fluffy tinder, they become smolder points.

    Applicability conditions: (1) pyrite nodules are available (look in volcanic rock areas, coal-seam outcrops, and black shale; register them in the resource map, Chapter 2); (2) a sharp flint edge exists (Chapter 5); (3) the tinder is extremely dry and fluffy (tinder-fungus fluff is best).

    Operation: the left hand holds the tinder patty with birch bark underneath to catch ash; the right hand holds the pyrite and strikes its surface obliquely and quickly with the sharp flint edge; sparks spray into the fluff; when fine blue smoke appears, close the fluff around them and blow gently into open flame. Each scrape yields several sparks; success typically comes in 5–15 strikes (empirical value; must be field-calibrated).

    Boundaries and limitations (must be stated):

    • Mineral sources are extremely unevenly distributed. In regions without pyrite this route is zero and must not be the mainline.
    • In overcast or rainy weather the spark survival rate drops sharply.
    • Common misconception: flint struck against flint, or flint against ordinary rock, produces only cold spalls — no sparks, no fire.
    • The pyrite striking face dulls after repeated use; rotate to a corner or knock out a fresh edge.

    Combined strategy: mainline = friction fire lighting (feasible anywhere wood exists); percussion = an accelerated route in mineral-bearing regions. Run both in parallel and register success rates in the combustion log.

    6.5 From Campfire to Stable Heat Source¶

    Four standard fire configurations:

    Type Structure Use
    Cone Sticks leaned into a cone, fire nest at the cone's heart Fast ignition, lighting
    Log-cabin Layers of sticks crisscrossed in a square lattice Stable coal bed, supporting a grill
    Star Long sticks with ends toward the center, pushed in as each burns Overnight fire tending, wood saving
    Dakota fire pit Combustion well 25–30 cm in diameter, 40–50 cm deep; a 45° air tunnel dug slantwise from the windward side connects to the well bottom Wind-resistant, efficient, low smoke, concealed fire

    Dig the Dakota pit with a pointed stick and the hand axe (Chapter 5); the air-tunnel mouth must sit on the day's windward side — when the wind reverses, efficiency collapses, so re-dig or set wind-guiding stones at the tunnel mouth.

    Coal-bed management: after 30–60 minutes of open burning the wood turns to charcoal. Spreading or gathering the coals creates an adjustable heat surface. Three heat levels:

    • High fire: add sticks elevated off the ash (ample airflow).
    • Medium fire: gather the coals into a pile, lay sticks flat.
    • Low fire: spread the coals out, cover with a thin layer of ash.

    Cooking and baking rely on the coal bed, not on open flame.

    Banking the fire overnight: gather the coals into a pile → cover with 5–10 cm of dry ash → press thin soil around the edges → leave one finger-thick air hole. Success criterion: next morning the coal core is dark red and rekindles when blown. Failure causes: ash layer too thin or too damp; too little coal before banking (keep at least a bowl-mouth-sized coal pile).

    Turning the heat source into a hearth: build a stove from stone slabs. Slab selection: dense sedimentary or igneous rock; layered stone, vug-bearing stone, or water-absorbing stone explodes when heated. Trial-burn method: roast a candidate slab over a small fire for 10 minutes; if it does not burst, crackle, or crack, it is usable. This stone stove is the prototype of the Chapter 11 pottery kiln and the Chapter 12 furnace.

    [Card 06-04 Banking the Fire Overnight and the Ember Tube] (eight fields)

    1. Input materials: a charcoal bed after 30–60 minutes of open burning (at least a bowl-mouth-sized heap); dry ash, 5–10 cm (must be dry — damp ash smothers the fire); a thin layer of soil; an ember tube (bamboo section or small clay pot, lidded, with a pinhole in the lid).
    2. Tools required: a wooden or stone paddle (for raking coals and spreading ash), ember tube, a small bag of spare dry ash. Calibration source: no instrument — next morning's ember condition is the verdict on last night's banking.
    3. Sequence and control points: rake coals into a heap → cover with 5–10 cm dry ash → press a thin soil rim around it → leave a finger-width air hole. For the tube: put a small red coal plus charred tinder inside, lid on, pinhole open. Control points: ash depth and hole size — sealing the hole smothers it, opening it burns it out; one finger-width is the empirical middle.
    4. Empirical parameters and calibration: wood turns to charcoal after 30–60 minutes of open burning; ash cover 5–10 cm; air hole about one finger wide; heap no smaller than a bowl mouth (below that there is not enough thermal mass to last the night). A banked bed holds 8–12 hours (empirical, varies with ash depth and overnight temperature, calibrate locally).
    5. Observations to record: per night — heap size when banked / ash depth / hole condition / ember color next morning / whether it relit on the first try / overnight low temperature.
    6. Pass / rework / scrap criteria: pass = ember dull red next morning and relights on a breath; rework = warm but no glow, feed dry kindling to revive; scrap = cold ash — establish which of the three causes applied (ash too thin, ash damp, heap too small) and record it.
    7. Common failures and diagnosis order: (1) cold in the morning → check ash depth first, then whether the ash was damp, then whether the heap was big enough (in that order of frequency); (2) glow present but will not relight → air hole blocked or ash packed too deep; (3) coal dies inside the tube → tube not pre-warmed, or pinhole too large (convection carries the heat away).
    8. L1 / L2 / L3 evidence: L1 = one successful night plus record; L2 = seven consecutive nights including at least one cold or rainy night, with complete nightly records; L3 = a second person achieves it from this card alone, and the two seven-night records can be compared.

    6.6 Wood, Charcoal, and Combustion Conditions¶

    Three stages of combustion: below 150°C moisture evaporates (white smoke); 200–400°C pyrolysis releases combustible gas (a flame is essentially wood gas burning); above 400°C fixed carbon glows red without flame. Two engineering conclusions: (1) smoke = unburned combustible gas = heat loss + carbon monoxide risk; (2) the key to raising temperature is not piling on more wood but getting air to the root of the flame.

    Wood → charcoal conversion (earth-mound kiln):

    1. Build the mound: a cone-shaped wood pile 1–1.5 m in diameter; straight billets stand packed tight; leave a central vertical shaft as the ignition core.
    2. Cover: first with grass or leaves, then with 5–10 cm of earth; leave 1–2 fist-sized air inlets at the bottom and a bowl-mouth-sized smoke port at the top.
    3. Light: drop the ember into the central shaft and let the fire burn outward through the pile.
    4. Control the burn, using smoke color as the criterion sequence: white smoke (water vapor) → yellow-brown smoke (tar) → pale blue smoke (combustible gas nearly spent) → thin smoke. Each time the smoke color advances one stage, shrink one air opening with earth; after blue smoke, keep only the top port.
    5. Seal the kiln: close all openings and add more earth cover; smother-cool for at least 4 hours before opening. Opening early = it reignites into ash.

    Total time about 4–10 hours (empirical value; must be field-calibrated; larger mounds take longer).

    Charcoal acceptance criteria: a strike rings crisp like "ding"; fracture faces are jet black and glossy and the piece is light; no brown wood core (underburned); it does not crumble under finger pressure nor shed black powder (overburned charcoal turns whitish, is brittle, and sheds ash).

    Yield (empirical value; must be field-calibrated): mass yield about 20%–25%, i.e. 4–5 kg of dry wood yields 1 kg of charcoal; volume is roughly halved.

    Use registry: this chapter's charcoal serves roasting heat and drying; charred tinder fungus is the best fire-lighting medium; the large-scale consumers come in Chapter 12 (furnace and kiln fuel) and Chapter 18 (smelting reducer).

    6.7 How to Control Temperature, Airflow, and Burn Time¶

    Three airflow principles: low inlet, high outlet (air inlet low and facing the wind, smoke outlet high); the fire channel's cross-section must not be blocked by collapsed wood; rake out the ash when it accumulates to a finger's thickness (an ash layer insulates and blocks air, but a thin ash layer left in place protects the coals).

    Adjustment measures table:

    Goal Operation
    Raise temperature Add dry wood; elevate wood off the ash; gather coals together; open the air ports
    Lower temperature Stop adding wood; cover with ash; spread the coals; half-close the air ports
    Hold temperature Burn large hardwood billets slowly; add small amounts of wood at fixed intervals
    Concentrate heat Bank the coals into a nest; stand windbreak stones on all sides

    Judging temperature by fire color (auxiliary empirical method, not quantitative): dull red about 500–600°C; bright red 700–800°C; orange-yellow 900–1100°C; bright yellow-white above 1100°C (empirical values; must be field-calibrated). Limitations: strongly affected by ambient light, flame brightness, and viewing angle; use only for coarse grading and never as a quantitative basis (style rule 3).

    Standard-sample method (a reproducible substitute): place small clay pieces, dough, and rosin lumps of the same batch at the same position as the workpiece. The changes the samples show (drying out, scorching brown, softening) calibrate the temperature step of that fire position, and anyone following the record can reproduce the same criteria. Sample recipes and preparation are unified in Section 8.6. This method lays the groundwork for formal high-temperature measurement in Chapter 12.

    Burning schedule (empirical values; must be field-calibrated): finger-thick softwood sticks 2–4 minutes each; palm-wide split wood 15–25 minutes; wrist-thick hardwood billets 40–90 minutes whole; a banked coal bed 8–12 hours. Scheduling rules: overnight use fire banking, long cooking use a hardwood coal bed, quick heat use softwood open flame.

    6.9 Stone Boiling and Drinking-Water Disinfection (Delivering Chapter 2's Promise)¶

    Chapter 2 declares twice that "until boiling-disinfection capability is established in Chapter 6, do not drink still water" — this section delivers on it: stone boiling in the pre-pottery era. Method: hold the water in a wooden/leather container (never set it directly on the fire); drop in dense, non-spalling stones burned through in the stove (river pebbles; trial-burn method: only pebbles that survive one dry firing without cracking may be used — quartz-bearing pebbles tend to burst when heated, and flying stone fragments are this section's number-one hazard); add 3–5 stones per liter of water, stir, and once rolling bubbles appear, add another round; a full rolling boil sustained for one period (about 3–5 min) is the disinfection criterion; lift the stones out with a clean branch. Drink only after it has cooled completely. Batch disinfection and water storage upgrade to routine boiling once pottery (Chapter 11) and metal pots (Chapter 18) unlock.

    6.8 Fire Prevention, Smoke, and Carbon Monoxide Risk¶

    Carbon monoxide (CO) risk — this chapter's number-one safety item: CO is colorless and odorless, produced by oxygen-starved incomplete combustion (sealed shelters, smothered fires, green wood piled onto a fire). Poisoning progression: pressure pain in the forehead and temples → nausea → dizziness and weak legs → loss of consciousness. Mild poisoning response: move immediately to fresh air and rest lying down; after recovery, avoid smoke exposure for 24 hours.

    Four Iron Rules:

    1. Any indoor overnight fire must have both a low air inlet and a high smoke outlet (outlet diameter ≥20 cm, vertical separation ≥1 m; empirical value; must be field-calibrated). Ports at equal height = no draft.
    2. Banking is not sealing: always keep the flue open.
    3. A flame turning yellow, soft, and lazy, or indoor smoke accumulating and refusing to clear = poor combustion: open the door to ventilate and evacuate people immediately.
    4. Fires under eaves or inside shelters must be attended at all times; the attendant must not turn their back to the fire and must not fall asleep.

    Fire separation: keep the fire's outer edge ≥1.5 m from dry grass, woodpiles, and shelter walls; vertically, sparks can reach over 3 m (resinous wood throws them farther) — no drooping branches within 3 m above; clear the ground to mineral soil and ring it with stones; never light a fire directly over root mats, peat, or a thick humus layer — smolder can creep along roots for days and reappear far away.

    Extinguishment acceptance: pour water → stir apart → pour again → hold the back of a hand against the ash for 60 seconds with no sensation of warmth before leaving the site. When water is short, bury with wet soil and tamp it firm; the hand-back test applies equally.

    Burn treatment: flush immediately with cold water, continuing to cool for at least 15 minutes; never apply mud, ash, or grease; do not puncture blisters.

    Capability Boundaries¶

    Can do: at least two of the four fire-lighting methods (hand drill / fire plow / fire saw / percussion) reproducible in all weather; three-tier tinder preparation and charring preparation; ember storage overnight; controlled coal bed and fire banking. Explicitly cannot do: bow drill (unlocked by Chapter 7 cord — see the three-material mismatch preview in this chapter's prerequisites); gunpowder-grade ignition; maintenance-free embers in humid rainforest conditions (weighted further in the rainforest edition, Chapter 50). External dependencies: dry tinder — fire-lighting success rates in the humid season are subject to this team's own measurements; no promises.

    Safety Boundaries¶

    • The four CO Iron Rules (6.8) are absolute; no "warmth first" excuse holds.
    • Opening the charcoal kiln during the smother-cooling period is strictly forbidden: reignition not only ruins the charge, kiln CO can kill.
    • Never bring the ember tube into a closed overnight shelter.
    • In thunderstorms, do not light or tend fire under lone trees, on ridges, or on open high ground.
    • Fire-lighting training takes place on cleared mineral ground, far from woodpiles.

    Acceptance Criteria¶

    • L1 proof-of-principle: complete one successful friction fire lighting and raise a campfire; complete one pyrite percussion fire lighting (in mineral-bearing areas).
    • L2 repeatable manufacture: complete friction fire lighting to flame in 3 weather conditions (sunny, overcast, after rain); the Dakota pit burns for 30+ minutes without dying; the banked fire rekindles next morning with ≥2 successes; one kiln batch of charcoal with a pass rate (jet black, hard, no wood core) ≥60% (empirical value; must be field-calibrated).
    • L3 sustainable production: standing tinder stock from ≥3 sources; fuel reserves >7 days' use, raised off the ground against damp; 3 consecutive kiln batches pass; the combustion log and attendance regime are on the books and in force; two or more members independently pass the L2 assessment.
