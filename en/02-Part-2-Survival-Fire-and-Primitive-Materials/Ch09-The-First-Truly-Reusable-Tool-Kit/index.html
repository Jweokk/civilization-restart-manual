    Chapter 9: The First Truly Reusable Tool Kit¶

    Part 2 · Survival, Fire, and Primitive Materials (final chapter of this part). The previous four chapters accumulated stone tools, fire, wood and cordage, clay and glue; this chapter combines them into a tool system that is reusable, repairable, and replicable: composite tools, tooling (drills, saws, grinding, clamping), workbenches and tool kits, plus multi-person collaboration and throughput systems. This part's acceptance goal: the basic capabilities of gathering materials, making tools, controlling fire, working wood, and recording processes.

    Chapter Objectives¶

    • Complete the upgrade from "placeholder tools" to "proper composite tools": the four-way joint of hafting standard + glue + wedge lock + lashing.
    • Establish the first tooling: sand-abrasive drilling (stone/bone/wood), bow saw and cord saw, grinding platforms.
    • Establish the first set of fixtures and workbench (clamping is the precondition of repeatable precision).
    • Establish the "tools that make tools" bootstrap route, standard parts, and a repair system.
    • Establish multi-person task breakdown and the first basic tool kit.
    • Deliverables: one qualified tool kit per workstation + ledger + repair records.

    Heat source fire pit, charcoal bed, charcoal Chapter 6
    Materials tar glue, rawhide strips, sinew, tempered clay, pigments Chapter 8, 7.3
    Records angle board, scribed rule, numbering system Chapters 3, 8

    9.1 Combining Stone and Wood¶

    Complete composite-tool specification table:

    Tool Edge angle Handle length Haft angle Use boundary
    Stone axe 60°–80° 60–90 cm edge line perpendicular to the handle axis felling, splitting
    Stone adze 45°–60° 30–45 cm edge at 60°–75° to the handle smoothing, gouging
    Stone knife 20°–40° 15–25 cm straight handle or side slot cutting
    Stone chisel 30°–50° 15–20 cm coaxial gouging, prying; hammering forbidden
    Scraper 45°–70° 10–15 cm coaxial scraping smooth

    Wooden caliper (completed in v2: the first "reading" measuring tool — a size comparator for the pre-iron age): nail or scribe a fixed straight stop (reference jaw) at one end of a hardwood strip; on the strip, scribe lines at the standard rod's "finger-width" graduations to position the sliding jaw — after the sliding jaw (a small straight-stop block) is pressed against the workpiece, press-scribe its edge line onto the strip and read the nearest graduation. Zero calibration: with both jaws closed, the sliding jaw's edge should press on the zero line — if it is off, shim thin wood slivers behind the back of the jaw to correct, then fix with a scorched brand on the hardwood strip + a dot of tar (wire waits for Chapters 17–18). Precision: finger-width graduations + eyeballed half-finger (about the 5–8 mm grade, rule of thumb) — stronger than "looks about right," one grade weaker than the Chapter 20 snap gauge; it is made precisely so that "the same size" has evidence before a snap gauge exists. Re-check zero weekly (wood swells and shrinks; tighten the schedule in the wet season).

    [Card 09-03 Wooden Caliper Making and Zero Calibration] (eight fields)

    1. Input materials: one hardwood strip (the beam); two straight-stop blocks (reference jaw and sliding jaw); thin wood slivers (for shimming the zero); tar (for fixing the setting).
    2. Tools required: marking tool (to scribe the standard rod's "finger" graduations), charcoal for branding the hardwood beam (scorch marks fix the lines), grinding stone (truing the jaw faces). Calibration source: the graduations are taken from the standard rod's finger-level sub-units (Chapter 3); graduations are never self-invented.
    3. Sequence and control points: nail or scribe the fixed straight stop at one end (reference jaw) → scribe finger graduations along the beam → press the sliding jaw against the workpiece and press-scribe its edge line onto the beam, reading the nearest graduation → zero calibration (with both jaws closed the sliding jaw's edge should press the zero line) → if off, shim thin slivers behind the back of the jaw → fix with a scorch brand plus a dot of tar → re-check weekly. Control points: no use before the zero is calibrated; tighten the re-check schedule in the wet season.
    4. Empirical parameters and calibration: precision = finger-width graduations + eyeballed half-finger (about the 5–8 mm grade, empirical value — must be calibrated to local conditions); zero re-checked weekly, tighter in the wet season; readings are comparative only (same size / half a finger off), never absolute.
    5. Observations to record: caliper number / beam wet-dry state / zero re-check date and deviation (in half-finger units) / workpiece number and reading / comparison against the template.
    6. Pass / rework / scrap criteria: pass = the zero sits on the zero line and two readings of the same piece agree; rework = the zero drifts — re-shim and re-fix; scrap = the beam cracks or the graduations wear flat (remake from dry stock; old readings may not be carried over).
    7. Common failures and diagnosis order: (1) readings drift → check the zero first (moisture swelling is the first cause), then whether the jaw faces are rounded (they must be trued flat), last whether the measurement was taken on a sloped face; (2) two readings of the same piece differ by more than half a finger → first check whether the sliding jaw is pressed home (release it and it backs off), then whether the graduations are worn flat; (3) the zero will not stay calibrated → first check whether the shim is too thick (slivers, not a thick shim), then whether the jaw back is crushed (change the jaw); (4) it is always one grade off against the template → first check whether the graduations came from the standard rod (self-invented graduations always differ), then whether the measurement points match.
    8. L1 / L2 / L3 evidence: L1 = one caliper that can read "half a finger off" plus a zero re-check record; L2 = weekly re-checks for two consecutive months, with the readings used for hole-diameter judgments; L3 = a second person re-checks the same caliper from this card alone and the zero agrees.

    Anti-vibration handle wedge (completed in v2): driven wedge locks loosen under long-term impact; use all three anti-loosening methods together — (1) double-wedge cross: drive one wedge with the grain and one cross-grain, interleaved (their expansion locks each other); (2) dip the wedges in glue (tar glue) before driving to fill the gaps; (3) one wrap of wire/cord as a collar at the handle end to hold down the wedge mouth. Weekly inspection: the moment a gap shows at the wedge mouth, re-drive it (clean grit out of the gap first — sand crushes wood fibers).

    [Card 09-02 Anti-Vibration Double-Wedge Method] (eight fields)

    1. Input materials: two hardwood wedges (one with the grain, one cross-grain; 7.5 specification: face angle 8°–12°, 2–3 mm blunt head); tar glue; cord (or wire after Chapters 17–18) for the end collar.
    2. Tools required: wooden mallet (driving), scraper (cleaning the gap), angle board (checking the wedge face angle), marking tool. Calibration source: wedge face angle checked against the angle board (the 45/60/90 hard grades plus the approximate grade); wedge dimensions per 7.5.
    3. Sequence and control points: clean the gap (grit must go — sand crushes wood fibers) → drive the first wedge with the grain → drive the second cross-grain, interleaved (their expansion locks each other) → dip the wedges in glue to fill the gaps → one collar wrap at the handle end to hold the wedge mouth down → weekly check. Control points: the two wedges must be one with and one across the grain (two in the same direction is just a single wedge).
    4. Empirical parameters and calibration: wedge face angle 8°–12° (empirical value — must be calibrated to local conditions); blunt head 2–3 mm; wedges dipped in glue; one collar wrap at the handle end; inspection every week; drive a dry wedge into a wet hole (it locks as the hole dries and shrinks).
    5. Observations to record: tool number / directions of the two wedges (with grain, cross-grain) / wedge face angle (angle-board check) / dipped or not / collar turns / gap at the wedge mouth at each weekly check (present or not, width eyeballed).
    6. Pass / rework / scrap criteria: pass = both wedges interleaved and seated, no gap at the mouth, collar holding the mouth down; rework = a gap shows — clean it and re-drive (never just hammer it tighter without cleaning); scrap = the handle end splits at the mouth (fibers already crushed) — change the handle or shorten it.
    7. Common failures and diagnosis order: (1) the wedge backs out within days → first check whether only one wedge was driven (a single wedge always backs out), then whether both run the same way (one must be cross-grain); (2) it still backs out after re-driving → first check whether grit was cleaned from the gap, then whether the wedges were dipped in glue; (3) the wedge splits the handle end → first check whether the face angle left the 8°–12° range, then whether the blunt head has mushroomed; (4) the end collar loosens → re-wrap at the weekly check (the collar holds the wedge mouth down; it is not decoration).
    8. L1 / L2 / L3 evidence: L1 = one tool completed with double-wedge cross, glue dip, and collar; L2 = an unbroken run of weekly check records with no gap at the wedge mouth; L3 = a second person re-wedges the same handle from this card alone with the same wedge directions and face angles, and the weekly check shows no gap.

    Standard hafting procedure (four-way joint):

    1. Cut the slot: fire-carving sets the slot lines (6.5) → deepen with the stone chisel → dress the walls with the scraper. Slot width = blade thickness with 0.5–1 mm interference; slot depth = 2/3 of the blade height.
    2. Apply glue: tar glue (8.5 recipe), warmed, coating the full blade-slot mating faces.
    3. Seat the blade: set the blade and align the force line — the striking-force direction must pass through the midpoint of the cutting edge and the handle axis (verify with lines drawn on the bench top).
    4. Lash: with wet rawhide strip or sinew (7.3), wrap the slot mouth in a cross pattern, 5 turns above and 5 below; it self-locks as it dries and shrinks.
    5. Wedge lock: drive a hardwood wedge with the grain into the slot-mouth end face until it is fully expanded.
    6. Cure: rest 24 hours before issue.

    Acceptance (three escalating stages): 20 air swings → 20 light chops into a wood block → 1 day of normal use. Pass criteria: lashing displacement <2 mm; blade does not shift, no glue-crack sounds; no continuous cracking in the glue line. Failures go back for rework: heat to soften the glue (tar re-melts and re-bonds) → re-lash.

    Tool anatomy essentials: handle diameter 3–3.5 cm (palm-fit rule; standard part in 9.4); the axe's balance point near the head, the adze's near the edge; straight-grained handles, no knots on load-bearing faces. Ranked main causes of hafting failure: force line off > glue layer too thick > uneven lashing > slot too wide.

    [Card 09-01 Four-Way-Joint Hafting] (eight fields)

    1. Input materials: blade (stone axe / adze / knife / chisel / scraper, per the 9.1 table); handle stock (straight-grained hardwood, handle diameter 3–3.5 cm); tar glue (8.5 recipe); wet rawhide strip or sinew (7.3); hardwood wedge (7.5 specification).
    2. Tools required: fire chisel and stone chisel (slot), scraper (dress the walls), wooden mallet (driving the wedge), bench scribing (aligning the force line), angle board (checking the haft angle). Calibration source: slot interference referenced to blade thickness; the haft angle per the 9.1 table (axe edge line perpendicular to the handle axis, adze 60°–75°) checked with the angle board.
    3. Sequence and control points: (1) cut the slot (width = blade thickness with 0.5–1 mm interference, depth = 2/3 of blade height) → (2) apply glue (warm, coating the full blade-slot mating faces) → (3) seat the blade on the force line (striking force through the edge midpoint and the handle axis, verified with bench lines) → (4) lash (cross pattern, 5 turns above and 5 below the slot mouth) → (5) wedge lock (drive a hardwood wedge with the grain until fully expanded) → (6) rest 24 hours. Control points: force-line alignment; the glue layer must not be thick.
    4. Empirical parameters and calibration: slot interference 0.5–1 mm; slot depth = 2/3 of blade height; 5 lashing turns above and below; 24 h cure; three-stage acceptance = 20 air swings → 20 light chops into a wood block → 1 day of normal use; pass criteria: lashing displacement <2 mm, blade does not shift, no continuous glue-line cracking; handle diameter 3–3.5 cm (palm-fit rule).
    5. Observations to record: tool number / edge angle and handle length (against the 9.1 table) / measured slot width / glue-layer thickness (eyeball) / lashing turn count / force-line check result / cure time / result of each acceptance stage.
    6. Pass / rework / scrap criteria: pass = all three acceptance stages pass and lashing displacement <2 mm; rework = glue line cracked or lashing loose — heat to soften the glue (tar re-melts and re-bonds) and re-lash; scrap = the handle cracks or the slot is too wide to re-glue (shorten the handle for other work, recycle the blade blank).
    7. Common failures and diagnosis order (ranked by the text's main causes): (1) force line off (the first cause of blades leaving the handle and of handles snapping) → re-align on the bench so the striking force passes the edge midpoint and the handle axis; (2) glue layer too thick (the glue holds the mating faces apart) → heat, scrape thin, re-coat; (3) uneven lashing → re-lash 5 turns above and below in the cross pattern; (4) slot too wide (interference beyond 0.5–1 mm) → shorten the handle for other work or change the handle; (5) a gap at the wedge mouth → clean the grit out and re-drive.
    8. L1 / L2 / L3 evidence: L1 = one four-way-joint composite stone axe passing air swings and light chops; L2 = 3 axes in a row passing all of the 9.1 acceptance; L3 = a second person hafts from this card alone, passing all three stages with lashing displacement <2 mm.

    9.2 Simple Drills, Saws, and Grinding Tools¶

    Sand-abrasive drilling (this chapter's core new process):

    • Wood: bow drill (7.6) + stone drill point, direct cutting. 5–15 minutes per hole in 2 cm hardwood (rule of thumb, must be field-calibrated).
    • Stone and bone: cutting is not feasible; switch to abrasion grinding — hollow drill (bone tube, bamboo tube) + wet quartz-sand abrasive: press the tube drill against the workpiece, drive rotation with the bow drill, feed slurry every few turns, and lift every 20–30 seconds to clear the cuttings. Piercing a 1 cm stone slab takes about 2–6 hours (rule of thumb, must be field-calibrated); when the hole breaks through, a ring-shaped boss appears on the back face of the workpiece — a light tap knocks it free, leaving a clean round hole.
    • Drill wear goes on the books: change the bone tube's end every 2–3 holes; quartz-sand consumption counted by the bowl.

    Two sawing routes:

    Tool Making Use and efficiency
    Bow saw toothed stone flake (5.5 blank, tooth pitch 2–3 mm) lashed into a bow-shaped wooden frame and tensioned reciprocating sawing of wood: a 5 cm wooden pole in 20–40 minutes (rule of thumb, must be field-calibrated); cuts flatter than axe chopping
    Cord saw cord + wet quartz sand slicing stone slabs, shell, bone: the cord with abrasive slurry saws back and forth; slow but the kerf is flat and straight — can blank stone preforms for grinding

    Grinding platform and workflow:

    1. Abrasive stone grades: coarse (scratches visible to the eye) — medium (felt by a fingernail) — fine (no grain feel); fix the coarse and medium stones between stop blocks on the workbench (9.3) to form the "grinding bench."
    2. Flat-grinding sequence: scribe with allowance → coarse-grind off the allowance → medium-grind to shape → fine sand on the slab + leather polish.
    3. Flatness self-check: mate the two workpieces' faces against each other — coat with charcoal dust and press; high spots show black; or sight against the light for a transmission gap; a gap <1 mm passes (checked against the Chapter 3 reference straightedge).
    4. Angle control: check against the wooden angle board (45°/60°/90°) as you grind.

    Grinding a complete stone axe takes 3–8 hours (rule of thumb, must be field-calibrated); grinding is a slow process — throughput is maintained by division of labor (9.5).

    [Card 09-04 Grinding-Bench Work and Angle-Board Checks] (eight fields)

    1. Input materials: three abrasive-stone grades (coarse: scratches visible to the eye; medium: felt by a fingernail; fine: no grain feel); water and quartz sand (fine sand sprinkled on the slab); leather (polishing); the stone blank to be ground.
    2. Tools required: stop blocks (clamping the coarse and medium stones between the bench stops to form the grinding bench), wooden angle board (45°/60°/90°, Chapter 3 bootstrap), charcoal dust (flatness mating check), reference straightedge (Chapter 3). Calibration source: angle by fitting against the angle board and sighting the light gap; flatness by charcoal-dust mating of two workpieces or by light-gap sighting.
    3. Sequence and control points: scribe with allowance → coarse-grind off the allowance → medium-grind to shape → fine sand on the slab plus leather polish; check the edge angle against the angle board between every pass. Control points: grinding striations consistent in direction and parallel to the edge (the evidence the angle has not changed); keep the original edge angle throughout.
    4. Empirical parameters and calibration: flatness criterion = light gap <1 mm; the three hard angle grades 45/60/90 (approximate grade ±2–3°); grinding one stone axe takes 3–8 hours (empirical value — must be calibrated to local conditions); edge angles per the 9.1 table (axe 60°–80°, adze 45°–60°, chisel 30°–50°, scraper 45°–70°).
    5. Observations to record: workpiece number / stone grade used / angle-board check after each pass / striation direction / light-gap width / hours per piece / stone wear depth.
    6. Pass / rework / scrap criteria: pass = light gap <1 mm, edge angle matching the angle board, striations parallel and same-direction; rework = the edge angle wanders — go back to coarse grinding, re-open the edge, and check pass by pass; scrap = over-ground (blade too thin or under template size) — demote to practice stock or make a smaller piece.
    7. Common failures and diagnosis order: (1) the ground edge angle wanders → first check whether the angle board was used between passes (no angle-board check is the first cause), then whether the stone face has hollowed (stop blocks not clamped), last whether the hand wobbles; (2) the ground face is not flat and the light gap varies → first check the stone face flatness (charcoal-dust mating of two workpieces), then whether the stop blocks are loose; (3) slow going, only scratches and no stock removed → first check whether the wrong stone grade is in use (coarse grinding needs the coarse grade), then whether the water was forgotten (coarse sandstone is ground wet); (4) edge chipping → first check whether grinding runs against the edge (it must follow the edge), then whether downward pressure is excessive.
    8. L1 / L2 / L3 evidence: L1 = one ground piece with an angle-board check and a light-gap record; L2 = one piece completed on each of the three stone grades, with hours and wear on the books; L3 = a second person grinds a piece to the same edge angle from this card alone (the angle-board fit shows the same light gap).

    9.3 How to Make a Simple Workbench and Fixtures¶

    Workbench: low post table — top slab 5–8 cm thick (riven board, 7.2), height 50–70 cm seated / 75–90 cm standing, four legs mortise-joined (7.5) + wedge locks, with cross rails between the legs against wobble. Plane one long edge of the top straight as this workstation's first straight-line reference (the physical extension of the Chapter 3 baseline). Drive stop blocks and stop pins into the top (into drilled holes) as planing stops.

    Fixture lineage (clamping is the precondition of repeatable precision):

    Fixture Structure Applies to
    Wedge-clamp groove V-groove cut into the benchtop; workpiece sits in the groove, wedges driven in on both sides round stock, handle shaping
    Rope-windlass clamp Π-shaped wooden frame + rope wound and twisted tight (windlass rod inserted into the rope loop and twisted; the spring-back self-locks) planing boards — the primitive vise
    Forked-branch clamp forked branch fixed to the bench edge or a ground anchor holding round work for drilling
    Ground anchor + foothold bent stake driven slantwise into the ground; workpiece held underfoot rough work on large pieces
    Hot-tar spot glue small parts spot-glued to a stone slab; heat to release drilling small parts, edge grinding (keeps hands away from the drill point)

    Layout and positioning: bench reference edge + square (Chapter 3) for cross layout lines; for batch parts use templates: trace a qualified part's outline onto thin board to make the template, then work new parts against it; a light gap <1 mm passes — this stage's realization of the template-replication method (Iron Law 2: precision must have a source).

    Workstation layout: light from the side (frontal light casts misleading shadows); keep the grinding bench and the drilling station apart (slurry and sawdust foul each other); posture: work surface near elbow height; leave two-person space at the bow-drill station (7.6).

    9.4 How to Make the Tools That Make Tools¶

    Bootstrap principle: every tool must be re-makeable from "other verified tools + recorded processes"; once a tool can only be repaired and not re-made, the system has a single point of dependency (the Chapter 1 three-tier definition).

    Tool mother-machine prototypes (three bootstrap lines):

    1. Bow drill + bench fork clamp = rotary power head: bits are interchangeable (stone point for cutting / bone tube for abrasive grinding) — the drill-press prototype.
    2. Grinding-stone platform + stop blocks = flat-surface machining prototype: the stop blocks set the angle; grind the bench's reference edge; the reference edge then re-verifies the grinding stones — grinding and measuring calibrate each other.
    3. Template + slot location = replication template: the template is generated from a qualified part, and new parts are made to the template, realizing "replicate one size" (Iron Law 2).

    Standard parts (the first step toward interchangeability):

    Standard part Unified spec Use
    Handle diameter 3–3.5 cm (palm-fit rule) any tool handle swaps with the same grip feel
    Axe handle length 75 cm spare-part interchange
    Drill shaft 25×2.2 cm shared by bow drill and tube drill
    Cord diameter grades 3/6/10 mm lashing and hoisting tiers

    Repair system (every tool registered):

    Fault Remedy Process source
    Dull edge regrind, angle unchanged 7.8
    Small chip (<3 mm) pressure-flake and redress 5.5
    Large chip recover the blade blank and re-flake 5.5
    Loose lashing re-lash per the standard lashing diagram 7.4
    Glue failure hot re-melt and re-bond (a tar property) 8.5

    Spare-parts stock (rule of thumb; calibrated against weekly consumption): stone-flake knives ≥5, stone drill points ≥3, bone-tube bits ≥2, cord coils ≥20 m, tar glue ≥2 lumps, rawhide strips ≥10, one spare each of coarse and fine grinding stones. Stock below the floor triggers replacement manufacture — never wait for run-out.

    9.5 From Solo Handwork to Multi-Person Collaboration¶

    Task-breakdown example (axe making, 7 stations): quarry → rough flaking → finish flaking → grinding → handle-slot cutting → hafting (glue + lashing + wedge) → inspection. One person end-to-end: 1–2 days per piece; a flow line of 3–5 people makes several pieces per day (rule of thumb, must be field-calibrated — backfill the ledger with this team's measured figures).

    Two-person operating rules:

    • Large-piece flaking: one steadies the core (wood clamp + side stance), one strikes; the steadier stands 45° behind the striker's flank.
    • Bow-drill deep holes: one presses the cap and holds alignment, one draws the bow for power.
    • Carrying: unified commands ("lift — walk — set"); for long stock, call ahead and answer behind.

    Quality responsibility system:

    • Every tool is marked with the maker's number (8.4 pigment marking) → traceable, repairable.
    • The inspector is independent of the maker; acceptance follows the 9.1 table and is signed (carved mark).
    • Rework rate goes on the books: a station whose rework rate stays above 20% (empirical criterion) goes back for retraining.

    Master–apprentice transfer (landing the information lineage):

    1. Turn procedures into mnemonics: compress each key procedure into a chant (select — flake — dress — grind — glue — lash — wedge).
    2. Demonstrate + reproduce: the apprentice independently makes 3 pieces; all must pass 9.1 acceptance to graduate (L3 personnel criterion).
    3. Archive-reproduction examination: periodically draw one person to reproduce a specified process from the 8.7 records; deviation beyond limits triggers a revision of the record.

    Safety collaboration iron rules: a 3 m exclusion zone around flaking splash; before swinging tools (axe, hammer), call out to anyone behind you; no hands in the bow-drill cord zone; clear slurry off the grinding bench promptly against slips.

    [Card 09-05 Seven-Station Flow Line and Workstation Layout] (eight fields)

    1. Input materials: blanks (stone and bone, sorted into the 9.7 three-way material rack); per-station consumables (tar glue, rawhide strips, sinew, quartz sand); archive cards (9.10, one card per tool).
    2. Tools required: station kits (flaking: hard hammer, soft hammer, pressure flaker; grinding: grinding bench; slotting: fire chisel and stone chisel; hafting: glue, wedge, lashing stock; inspection: template, angle board, wooden caliper). Calibration source: inspection tooling is the 9.4 standard parts, and the criteria are the 9.8 three-grade system.
    3. Sequence and control points: lay out the seven stations — quarry → rough flaking → finish flaking → grinding → handle-slot cutting → hafting (glue + lashing + wedge) → inspection → every piece travels with its card → the inspector, independent of the maker, signs (carved mark) against the 9.1 acceptance table. Control points: the inspector must not also be the maker; every piece carries the maker's number.
    4. Empirical parameters and calibration: one person end-to-end 1–2 days per piece; a flow line of 3–5 people makes several pieces per day (empirical value — must be calibrated to local conditions, backfilled with this team's measured figures); a rework rate staying above 20% (empirical criterion) means retraining; graduation = 3 pieces made independently, all passing 9.1 acceptance.
    5. Observations to record: station number / operator number / time each piece clears each station / rework cause per station / daily output / rework rate / material batch number.
    6. Pass / rework / scrap criteria: pass = every piece clears all seven stations, passes the three-stage 9.1 acceptance, and has a complete card; rework = a station's rework rate above 20% — stop that station for retraining before rescheduling; scrap = the card is missing or the maker number untraceable (not issuable — send it back to the first station to be re-marked).
    7. Common failures and diagnosis order: (1) output will not rise → find the bottleneck station first (longest clearing time), then check whether one person is covering two stations (two-person rules: large-piece flaking, bow-drill deep holes, and carrying each have their division); (2) rework clusters at one station → first check the maker numbers (clustering means that station needs review), then whether material batches cluster (a source problem), last whether the specification itself is unsuitable (revise the 9.1 table); (3) pieces and cards do not match → first check whether station sign-offs were skipped, then whether the maker number was ground off; (4) cross-traffic injuries → first check whether the travel path crosses the drill station's cord zone (the "6"-shaped circuit of 9.7), then whether anyone swung before calling out.
    8. L1 / L2 / L3 evidence: L1 = one piece carried through all seven stations with its card on file; L2 = 3 pieces in a row passing all 9.1 acceptance with the rework rate on the books; L3 = two or more members graduated (3 pieces each passing acceptance) plus rework-rate and daily-output data used for scheduling.

Never conclude an inventory with "mostly complete" — an inventory has only three possible outputs: complete, missing (list the missing items), broken (list the broken items).

    Workstation floor plan (text version): four zones and one circulation line around the workbench:

    • Workbench at the center, reference edge facing the side light (9.3 lighting rule).
    • Grinding bench about an arm-and-a-half to the workbench's front left (within 2 m), wet-sand work, stone slabs on the floor against mud; between it and the workbench stand the shavings bucket and the water bowl — slurry does not cross zones.
    • Drilling station to the workbench's front right, with room for two (cap-presser + bow-puller); nothing suspended overhead, no foot traffic in the cord rebound zone.
    • Stock rack against the rear wall: blank stock stored horizontally in three divisions (stone/bone/wood); marked-out blanks laid flat in a single layer, never stacked.
    • Finished-goods rack by the door: inspected pieces and pieces awaiting repair in separate sections, with a wooden tag standing between them (carve the character for "awaiting" to mark repairs).
    • Circulation line: stock rack → workbench → grinding bench → drilling station → workbench → finished-goods rack, looping in a "6"; never crossing, never cutting through the drilling station's cord zone; when two people work simultaneously, each keeps to one line (one bench, one line), and swapping positions requires setting down the workpiece first.

    Fixed-position tool management (shadow board): hang a thin board inside the tool-chest lid or on the workstation wall; after placing every tool in its storage spot, trace its outline (charcoal pencil); a tool's return must land inside its silhouette. Triple payoff: missing tools visible at a glance (an empty silhouette is a missing tool — no need to dig through the ledger); wrong-tool returns exposed instantly (a round head does not fit a square silhouette); newcomers know every tool's home without memorizing. Rule: the shadow board is checked once at each weekly inspection; any silhouette-object mismatch is logged as a missing item; a borrowed tool must leave a wooden tag on its silhouette spot (carved with the borrower's number). This is the prototype of fixed-position management — the ancestor of the later machine-tool rule "tool in position means work in position."

    9.8 Fine Grinding of Stone Tools¶

    9.2 gave the grinding framework; this section completes the criteria of the three finishing procedures up to the third tier.

    Hole-diameter and perpendicularity criteria for tube drilling:

    • Diameter control: the tube's outer diameter is the hole's lower bound — with the sand layer wrapped around the tube, the finished hole comes out 0.5–1 mm larger than the tube (rule of thumb; field-calibrate: drill three holes with the same tube, measure the diameters with the wooden caliper, take the average, and transcribe "tube diameter + increment" into the ledger). For a hole of diameter d, pick a tube of outer diameter about d−1 mm.
    • Perpendicularity criterion: after breakthrough, insert a test rod (a thin stick whittled from 9.4 standard drill-shaft stock, diameter = hole diameter −1 mm); if the difference in its wobble between the two sides of the hole mouth is <1 mm, count it perpendicular (empirical criterion); or scribe a cross on the workpiece's bottom face beforehand and sight the offset of the hole's center from the cross intersection against the light — an offset <1 mm passes — this is the workstation-level internal control (stricter than final acceptance, leaving margin) for the L2 acceptance item "hole-position deviation <2 mm."
    • Three anti-drift methods: (1) first lightly chip a positioning dimple on the start face with the stone point (1–2 mm deep); (2) the cap-presser applies vertical pressure only, no lateral force (two-person division, 9.5); (3) stop every 5 minutes and re-check against the layout lines — if drift is found while the hole is still shallow, you can grind the hole oval and then bring it back round to correct; once past half thickness, discovery means scrapping or demotion to a small part.
    • Slurry: sand:water about 2:1 by volume (thick paste that coats the wall without running); double-sieve the sand — coarse-sieve grade (grains ≤1 mm) to break through, fine-sieve grade (grains ≤0.3 mm) to finish; switch to fine sand just before breakthrough.

    Slot cutting (progressive-width method): slots ground into stone blanks (lashing slots, handle slots, decorative slots) must not be ground to full depth in one pass — three progressive passes:

    1. First pass: width to half of full width, depth to a third of full depth; press a charcoal-dusted wood strip the width of the slot against the slot floor — an even print of charcoal dots means the floor is flat and straight.
    2. Second pass: widen to 3/4, deepen to 2/3; gauge the width with the slot gauge (a hardwood strip of the same width + scribed lines on both sides).
    3. Third pass: to size; dress the slot walls with the fine grinding stone.

    Rationale: grind the full width in one pass and the walls have no retreat — over-grinding cannot be undone. Check between every pass; the checking pieces are the wood strip and slot gauge above (the Chapter 3 self-made gauge method).

    Polishing (three abrasive tiers + leather strip):

    1. Coarse tier: wet quartz sand (fine-sieve grade) with water, circular strokes, removing every medium-grind scratch — criterion: no directional scratches under side light.
    2. Medium tier: fine settled silt (from the still bottoms of river bends; slick and grit-free when rubbed) with water — criterion: a continuous reflective band seen at 45° against the light.
    3. Fine tier: dry leather strip (rawhide from 7.3, cut into 10 cm lengths) charged with tallow, pulled rapidly — criterion: the strip leaves no mark, and a fingertip drawn across meets no drag.

    Ceiling criterion: stop when the polished face shows a blurred reflection of your finger outline. Beyond that, returns approach zero while hours double — a high-cost, low-return procedure — so polish to grade by use (edges fine tier, bodies medium tier, blanks unpolished); never polish for polishing's sake.

    Finished stone-tool grading (three tiers): every piece is graded at completion, the tier number carved at the handle end (8.4 pigment marking):

    Tier Criteria Use
    Tier 1 (service) all dimensions pass the template (light gap <1 mm), no visible edge chips, hafting passed the 9.1 three-stage acceptance issued for use
    Tier 2 (reserve) dimensional deviation 1–2 mm, or edge chips <1 mm shallow without affecting loading into the spare-parts box; replaces Tier 1 on retirement
    Tier 3 (practice stock) pieces failed mid-work: chips >2 mm, slots off-line, holes skewed teaching and hand-feel practice only; barred from the ledger tool roster

    Promotion/demotion rules: a Tier 2 piece that after regrinding and re-inspection meets the Tier 1 criteria may be promoted to service; a Tier 1 piece damaged in use goes through the 9.4 repair table, then re-inspection and re-grading. Never let Tier 3 pieces mix into the Tier 2 box — practice stock leaking into the store is the number-one source of "unreliable spares."

    9.9 Bone and Antler Workshop¶

    Bone and antler are this stage's only tough materials — stone chips, wood splits; bone can bend, be ground, and be drilled. They are the exclusive source of needles, awls, hooks, and handles, and worth a dedicated workshop (workstation layout per the 9.7 four-zone system; the "bone" section of the stock rack is its store).

    Bone degreasing by simmering: fresh bone carries fat; worked directly it turns rank and slippery, and glued faces never hold (fat blocks glue — 8.5 tar glue has no adhesion to oily surfaces).

    1. Saw into short lengths (method below) into a pottery pot (Chapter 8) or a pit lined with stone slabs as a boiler; cover the bone with water; after boiling, hold at a bare simmer 2–3 hours (rule of thumb; field-calibrate: lift a section and press the fracture face with a fingertip — an oily sheen means another hour; a dry, grabby feel means done).
    2. Change the water once midway (at the 1-hour-after-boil mark) to keep fat from re-coating the bone.
    3. Lift out and shade-dry 3–5 days (rule of thumb; criterion: the knock changes from dull to crisp) — fire-drying always warps and cracks: forbidden.
    4. Final degrease verdict: no oily sheen on the fracture face; after a 15-minute soak, no oil film on the water. Degrease quality directly determines later glue-bond and lashing life; record the stock batch number and let it travel with the record card (9.10).

    Sawing (stone-knife groove + snapping): blanking bone does not use the cord saw for full cuts (slow and jittery); use "stress breaking":

    1. With the stone-flake knife (edge angle 20°–40°, the 9.1 table) score a ring groove around the break position, to 1/3–1/2 of the bone-wall thickness (empirical starting point; see the practice method).
    2. Line the groove with double-sieved fine sand to speed the scoring (the dry-use version of the 9.2 slurry principle).
    3. Bridge both ends, align the groove with the bench edge, and tap the bone lightly with a wooden mallet face or press it — it snaps cleanly along the groove.

    Criterion: the fracture follows the groove (if not, the groove depth was uneven); diagonal cracking running >5 mm into the bone body demotes that piece to Tier 3. ◎ Hand-feel: the measure of a "light tap" is the hardest thing to put into words — practice method: take 10 scrap bone sections, start at 1/4 wall-thickness groove depth and deepen piece by piece, recording each fracture quality in the practice ledger, and find the critical groove depth for this team's usual stock (most animal bone falls between 2/5 and 1/2 of wall thickness, an empirical band); misjudgment compensation: rather score too deep than strike too hard — scoring deep costs a few minutes; striking hard costs the whole section.

    Point and edge grinding (bone needle/awl/fishhook specification table):

    Item Spec Grinding method Pass criterion
    Bone needle length 6–8 cm, diameter 1.5–2 mm, eye diameter 0.8–1 mm (rule of thumb) fine tier of the grinding bench, reciprocating to a point; the eye spun with a fine bone tube (a scaled-down 9.2 tube drill, half-depth from each face to prevent breakout) no cracks around the eye; the point thin enough not to split leather thread
    Awl length 10–15 cm, point section 3–4 cm coarse-grind to shape, fine-grind the edges, taper from four faces trial stab into hardwood: 2 mm penetration without snapping
    Fishhook inner bend diameter 5–8 mm, barb length 2–3 mm cut the blank from a bone plate (groove-and-snap) → grind the curve → grind the barb → sharpen the tip hang a stone weight of about 1× the target fish's weight, hang still for 10 seconds: no deformation, no springing straight
    Scraper blade bone plate length 8–12 cm, edge angle 45° bevel one face test scraping a hide: no curling, no scored grooves

    The bone needle is this workshop's difficulty ceiling: a practiced hand makes 5–10 per day at about 50% yield (rule of thumb, must be field-calibrated and entered in the ledger) — schedule production from measured capacity, not from "how many we want" (9.5).

    Antler tools (handle stock and mallet faces):

    • Handle stock: the straight main beam of antler (length ≥15 cm, diameter 2.5–3.5 cm) is a natural handle, the swollen burr at the base serving as the grip — eliminating slot-cutting and hafting; after drilling the edged piece, fix it with a fine bone pin dipped in glue (8.5), for small knives, awls, and scrapers. Criterion: no longitudinal cracks in the beam; fracture face dense, no honeycombing.
    • Mallet face: a cross-section of the antler base (4–6 cm thick) serves as a soft mallet, dedicated to pressure flaking (5.5) and to seating hafting wedges — it is softer than stone and will not shatter stone edges. After the face is ground flat (the 9.8 coarse polish tier suffices) it goes into the clamping/striking class of the ledger.
    • Heat bending: antler softened in hot water can be bent into hook shapes and crank handles (large fishhooks, crank-drill handles); after bending, bind with cord to hold shape and shade-dry 3 days to set (rule of thumb).

    9.10 Tool-Life Ledger, Detailed Rules¶

    9.4 gave the repair table, 9.6 the inventory fields; this section closes the loop on "the life of one tool" — the ledger is not a recording hobby; it is the only data source for replacement planning and process review.

    One record per tool (record card): a tool gets its file the moment it enters service — one card per tool (wooden tablet, hung beside its silhouette spot on the shadow board):

    Field What to record Example
    Number/name ledger number (Chapter 8 system) + tool name T-041 stone axe
    Made/batch manufacture date + stock batch number (stone/bone batch number) Autumn Day 3 / stone batch B-7
    Grading 9.8 three tiers (recorded at entry to service and at every change) Tier 1
    Regrind count each regrind/re-lash/re-bond counts once, with the date 3rd, Winter 1
    Damage record symptom + cause judged on the spot (fill in a row against the failure-mode table) 2 mm edge chip / stock too wet and tough
    Status in use / spare / awaiting repair / retired in use
    Retirement date + reason (one of five: ground out / shattered / handle lost beyond re-bonding / snapped / lost) —

    Record discipline: regrinds left unrecorded break the life curve, and replacement planning will systematically undercount; card and tool stay together; retired cards are bundled monthly into the archive box and must not be destroyed.

    Monthly loss roll-up → replacement plan: on the last day of each month every workstation submits card copies, rolled up into the monthly loss table:

    Roll-up item Source Use
    Retired/in-service counts by class card retirement column monthly loss rate = retired ÷ in-service at month start
    High-loss item ranking retirement causes classified sets replacement priority; revises the 9.4 spare-part floors
    Batch correlation card batch numbers one batch losing abnormally → a stock problem (trigger line)

    Replacement plan, three lines: N retired this month → replace with N next month (same spec) → if N would take stock below the 9.4 floor, top up to "floor − on hand"; take the larger of the two. Replacement pieces enter the 9.5 flow-line schedule — never "make it when there's time."

    Process-review trigger line for high-loss tools: when a tool type's monthly loss rate exceeds 1/3 of its in-service count (empirical trigger; revise from measured data after 3 months of running), a process review triggers — not simply making a few more:

    1. Check stock: are the retired pieces' batch numbers concentrated? — if so, change the stock source or revise the selection criteria;
    2. Check workmanship: are the retired pieces' maker numbers concentrated? — if so, review that station's rework flow (linking to the 9.5 rework-rate >20% clause);
    3. Check spec: losses uniform → the spec itself does not fit the station; go back and revise the 9.1 specification table.

    Review conclusions go into the ledger's appendix; triggering without reviewing counts as the ledger system failing — data produced and not used is worse than no data.     C. Unresolved Dependencies¶

    • Edged-tool material ceiling: the regrind life and cutting speed of stone edges are far below metal — solved in Chapters 17–19; until then, cutting parameters must not be pushed to "rush the work."
    • Precision ceiling: flat surfaces rely on grinding + templates, with no rigid ways and no gauges; precision machining needs the Chapter 20 machine-tool lineage.
    • Hard abrasives: quartz sand works on stone/bone but will be insufficient for future metals; garnet sand, corundum sand, and the like should be registered for later use (Chapter 19 formalizes abrasives).
    • Power: all rotation and reciprocation is human-powered; sustained power waits for the water wheel (Chapter 21).

    Safety Boundary¶

    • No standing in the rebound zone of a broken bow-saw frame cord; the cord saw is worked by two people pulling from both sides — no one straddles the rope.
    • Wet-sand work at the grinding bench guarded against slips; a cracked grinding stone (struck) is scrapped and replaced immediately.
    • Hot-tar spot-glued pieces are released with wooden tongs; bare hands never touch glue dots.
    • In the tool chest, edged tools are always stowed edge-inward; in transport, edged tools are wrapped (grass bundle + cord wrap).
    • No swinging-class tool may be handed to another person for use before passing the 9.1 three-stage acceptance.

    Acceptance Criteria¶

    • L1 proof-of-principle: complete one four-way-joint composite stone axe passing the air-swing + light-chop tests; one abrasive-drilled hole (bone or stone).
    • L2 repeatable manufacture: 3 composite axes in a row passing all of the 9.1 acceptance; bow saw, cord saw, grinding bench, and rope-windlass clamp all built, each having completed one formal job; abrasive-drilled hole-position deviation (cross lines) <2 mm; template-replicated piece light gap <1 mm.
    • L3 sustainable production: standard-workstation tool kit complete and on the ledger; spare-part floors enforced ≥1 month; repair records continuous; two or more members graduated (3 consecutive pieces passing acceptance); rework-rate and daily-output data on the books and used for scheduling.
