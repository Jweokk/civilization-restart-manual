    Chapter 43: Archives, Ledgers, Negative Results, and Master–Apprentice Transmission¶

    Link with Section 4.10: this chapter is the full version of the Section 4.10 minimal archive (numbering / duplicates / borrowing); the three-tier archives, the negative-results store, and the transcription institution all grow out of those three things.

    This chapter closes Part 8. Chapters 37–40 supplied the settlement's shift roster, ledgers, spot inspection, and traceability; Chapters 41–42 supplied paper, ink, binding, and drawing versions. This chapter packs them into a container that can outlive a generation: archives (what to keep), ledgers (how to look things up), negative results (a wrong road is still a road), and the master–apprentice system (the part paper cannot write down). It answers two structural criticisms from the review head-on: first, "treating failure records as worthless information, so every generation repeats the same trial and error"; second, "key skills resting in single hands, where injury or death snaps the whole capability chain." The positioning must be stated first: this chapter is an institutions chapter, not a new-process chapter—the Chapter 39 five-step failure analysis, the batch-number system, and the failed-sample library are cited here, not rewritten; what this chapter adds is the filing and classification upstream of them and the transmission and teaching downstream. The whole chapter in one sentence: let the information of every trial and error outlive the people who made it, and let no craft's survival hang on one person.

    Chapter Objectives¶

    Having read this chapter, a settlement should have four institutions that mesh with one another:

    Output Specification Purpose
    Three-tier archive system active / technical / historical tiers, one master catalog volume, borrowing register, off-site duplicates cards, books, drawings, and evidence produced by the chapters stored by tier, retrievable on demand
    Parameter ledger system six-column format (parameter name / manual default / local field-calibrated value / calibration date / calibrated by / re-verification interval), three core ledgers each chapter's "empirical value, must be field-calibrated" results land as ledger pages that can be consulted
    Negative-results institution failed-sample library expansion (continuing Chapter 39) + a one-page, five-column "negative results record" failure conditions, raw-material differences, and next-time fixes kept alongside the physical items
    Master–apprentice and certification system four job grades, three assessment formats, certification for four classes of high-hazard processes, ≥2-person redundancy at key posts, handover year operations split into five steps—demonstrate, practice, assess, authorize, document—continuously producing replacements

    Capability Boundaries¶

    Can do:

    • Establish the three institutions—archives, ledger, apprenticeship—and make them cross-check and cross-confirm against the Chapter 37 shift roster, the Chapter 38 ledger, and Chapter 39 spot inspection and traceability.
    • Organize the book's ◎ techniques into a teaching list of "practice method + measurable compensating evidence," making hand-feel skills teachable and assessable.
    • Institutionalize the results of "empirical value, must be field-calibrated" calibration: transcribed into the ledger, re-verified when due, changes kept as an unbroken chain.

    Cannot do:

    • Fully commit tacit knowledge to paper. The scraper's entry stroke, the judgment of forging heat, the sheet-forming dip-and-lift—their substance is the coordination of muscle and senses. Paper can carry the practice method and the criteria, but not the hand-feel itself—this is a boundary of principle, not a paper-and-ink quality problem. Precisely because it cannot, the master–apprentice system is mandatory: paper governs the part that can be looked up, people govern the part that can be practiced; lose either carrier and the chain breaks.
    • Printed reproduction. Chapter 41 already declared movable type an upgrade route; every act of reproduction in this chapter is hand transcription, and cost grows linearly with volume, so archives stop at "a sole original plus one transcribed copy," with no multi-copy divergence.
    • Managing against forgetting. This chapter can guarantee "what should be recorded is on paper"; it cannot guarantee "what is recorded gets read." The dead-archive problem is fought with institutions of use (study sessions, monthly checks, drills), not with cabinets.

    External dependencies (counted in the C list):

    1. A literate population—depends on the output of the Chapter 4 teaching system; a non-literate settlement can only run degraded with physical samples + oral transmission (see the 43.7 degraded entry).
    2. A second building ≥50 m away in a different fire compartment (Chapter 40 resilience package)—the physical prerequisite of off-site duplicates.
    3. Teaching staff—≥1 craftsman at grade 3 (independent) or above on the roster; without teachers, this chapter's institutions can file but cannot transmit.

    43.1 Why Build Archives for a Furnace of Scrap Iron¶

    43.1.1 The time scale: this book's institutions must answer to three generations¶

    The reader of this book has twenty years of work ahead: starting from a clay pot and reaching the point of smelting iron, generating electricity, and treating one's own illnesses. In twenty years a settlement turns over two generations of people. A craftsman needs 3–5 years to go from apprentice to independent (empirical value, depending on process complexity), and another 2–3 years from independent to able to teach—one craft, from zero to "ready to be handed on," takes 5–8 years. So the acceptance scale for every institution in this chapter is not "this quarter" but three generations: the generation that built the institutions, the generation that grew up under them, and the generation that has only ever seen them. The first generation personally remembers why each rule was set; the second generation read the archives; the third has only the institutions themselves—and the institutions must remain self-consistent for people who never lived their making: the catalog can be searched, the ledger reconciles, the sample library holds the physical items.

    43.1.2 The decay of oral transmission, worked through¶

    Oral transmission is reliable on a 5-year scale: master and apprentice share the floor, and a mistake is corrected on the spot. It fails at the 20-year, cross-generation scale—the corrector has died, leaving only retelling. Run a checkable deduction. Take the Chapter 12 hand-feel criterion for judging furnace temperature from the slag, whose original wording was complete:

    Link Retold content What was lost
    Master (eyewitness) "Yellow-white like buckwheat husk on the slag face and not glaring—stop the blast; on an overcast day the color reads darker, wait another half quarter-hour" —criterion complete: color + brightness + weather correction
    Apprentice A (heard it a few times) "Stop the blast when it's yellow-white" lost the brightness reference and the weather correction
    Apprentice B (heard from Apprentice A) "Stop the blast when it yellows" lost the "yellow-white" band; only the hue remains
    Apprentice C (heard from Apprentice B) "The fire? the old masters just know" criterion entirely gone; only the legend "it's a feel, cannot be told" remains

    Three retellings, and the criterion has decayed from "color + brightness + correction term" to "one noun + one piece of mystique." In year five Apprentice C is on shift; the moment of stopping the blast rests entirely on nerve. Numeric decay is just as lethal: the carburizing hold, "six shichen (double-hours, wet-kiln charcoal)," becomes "six shichen," then "half a day," then—following dry-charcoal practice and holding the full six shichen—the steel over-carburizes and the edge shatters on its first strike. The pattern: qualifiers are lost first, numbers next, and last even the memory that a criterion ever existed. Oral transmission is not unreliable; its reliable span is only those years when the corrector is present.

    43.1.3 The archive account of one furnace of scrap iron¶

    Suppose a furnace in Chapter 18 over-reduces and fails—iron and slag inseparable, the whole heat condemned. Two ways to handle it; twenty years decide the outcome:

    • As garbage: the scrap is remelted, the story goes oral. Five years on, all that remains is "the year we lost a heat"; ten years on, "early days were hard." When the rainy season comes again, the next crew charges wet ore just as before and loses a second heat—the reviewers' "every generation repeats the same trial and error," reproduced intact.
    • As archive: one piece of the scrap is kept as a sample in the library (Chapter 39 four-field tag), and a one-page negative-results record states that the ore batch was rain-season stockpiled wet ore, along with the charcoal batch, the blast-pressure records, and a physical slag sample; conclusion: "wet ore must be sun-dried before charging." Next time wet ore arrives in the rainy season, half an hour with the archives saves a furnace of iron and three person-days of work.

    One furnace of scrap is worth a few work-days; its archive may save tens of work-days over twenty years—the scrap's archive is worth more than the scrap. That is where the verdict of Iron Law Six lands: "a pass without evidence is not a pass; it is an impression." By the same token, a failure without evidence is not experience either; it is a story. Sections 43.2–43.4 build "evidence" out of a slogan into cabinets, ledger pages, and sample racks; 43.5–43.7 hand the half that paper cannot hold to living people.

    43.2 The Archive Classification Institution¶

    43.2.1 Three tiers: shelve by frequency of use, not by importance¶

    The first principle of archive classification: classify by "how often it gets opened," not by "how important it sounds." A card needed every day, buried deep in a cabinet, is no archive at all. The three tiers:

    Tier Contents Storage Frequency of use Check interval Keeper
    Active archive copies of current-version process cards, daily ledger pages of each workshop, shift rosters, current-period spot-inspection sheets active-archive compartment inside each workshop (copies of the master file; if oil-stained or damaged, re-transcribe from the master file) daily monthly check: fill missing pages and replace soiled pages the same day as the monthly stocktake workshop master
    Technical archive master-file originals: the master process-card volume, drawings and formulations (Chapter 42), parameter-ledger annual volumes, life-table measured columns (Chapter 39), the equipment inventory, Chapter 38 ledger-page annual volumes moisture-absorbing cabinet in the archive room monthly monthly check + quarterly pest inspection (Chapter 41 intervals) archive custodian
    Historical archive closed failure-analysis casebooks (Chapter 39), negative-results records (43.4), retired old-version cards, acceptance-evidence indexes (test-bar/coupon numbers), bound annual volumes, experience entries of deceased craftsmen sealed compartment in the archive room (locked compartment or sealed box, numbered seals) annual unsealed and counted once a year (annual inspection day) archive custodian

    The annual sealing institution: on each annual inspection day, the year's technical-archive annual volumes and active-archive monthly pages are bound into volumes, given a year-volume number (e.g., H26 Vol. 3), moved into the historical-archive compartment, and sealed with a registered seal strip. Thereafter they may not be unsealed without the dual signatures of custodian and workshop master—the value of the historical archive lies precisely in "not being casually flipped through and amended": it is the final evidence for reconciliation twenty years on.

    Numbering rule (keyed to the Chapter 39 batch numbers): every accession gets one catalog line, numbered "Class-Year-Sequence"—A (active) / T (technical) / H (historical) + two-digit year + three-digit sequence, e.g., T26-014. Anything originating from a particular furnace or batch also records the furnace/batch/piece numbers in its catalog line (the Chapter 39 three-level batch numbers), so that a sheet of paper can be found both "by name" and "traced back from the piece number."

    43.2.2 Protection, duplicates, and the catalog: the physical parts of the institution¶

    • Cabinet protection: the full set carries over from Chapter 41—≥300 mm off the floor, out of daylight and kept closed, in a separate fire compartment ≥15 m away, ten-day moisture check (a paper strip inside the cabinet that bends crisply and snaps = pass), quarterly pest inspection (check spines and page corners for frass, galleries, and egg clusters), one-of-three insect-proofing treatment. This chapter does not repeat its procedures, only fixes ownership: every protection action in the archive room is entered in the archive inspection log, and the log itself is an active archive—the insect-proofing institution must keep records of its own.
    • Off-site duplicates: every technical-archive and historical-archive original gets one transcribed copy; original and copy are stored in two buildings (≥50 m apart, different fire compartments—a Chapter 41 Iron-Law-grade rule). Active archives are not copied—they are copies; if one is ruined, re-transcribe from the master file. Copies are checked against originals every half year: check the catalog, fill missing pages, dual signatures (Chapter 41 institution carried over). A fire may burn the paper of half a settlement; it must not be allowed to burn a single criterion.
    • Master catalog and spot-check criterion: one master catalog volume (quarto), one six-column line per accession—number / name / source (chapter-card number or furnace-batch-piece number) / archive date / storage compartment / original-and-copy locations. Its acceptance is isomorphic to the Chapter 39 traceability drill: point to any catalog line, and the custodian must produce the physical item within 10 min; producing an empty compartment means the catalog has drifted, and that segment of the catalog is rebuilt within the month.

    43.2.3 The borrowing register and the archive custodian¶

    Borrowing carries over Chapter 41's three columns (date taken and returned / handler / purpose), plus two rules: originals never leave the archive room (Chapter 41 standing rule); copies are lent for at most 7 days, overdue items are chased by the custodian and noted in the borrowing column—a copy overdue and unreturned is treated as missing, which triggers re-transcription.

    The archive custodian post: no dedicated position. The master file is kept by the bookkeeper concurrently (the Chapter 37 bookkeeper already handles the monthly stocktake and the ledger pages; archives are an extension of the books); each workshop's active archive is kept by its workshop master. Conditions of appointment: literate, grade-2 skill or above; the handover is supervised by someone who is not the current workshop master of the same workshop. Four duties: register accessions in the catalog, keep the borrowing register, run the periodic checks (ten-day moisture, quarterly pests, monthly page-filling), annual sealing and annual unsealing. Handover checklist (eight items, dual-signed item by item when the custodian changes):

    # Handover item Criterion
    1 Master catalog volume 10 catalog lines drawn at random all match physical items
    2 Original–copy check records the latest half-yearly check's dual signatures on file
    3 Outstanding-borrowings list overdue items at zero, or disposition noted
    4 Sealed-compartment keys and seal register historical-archive annual sealings match the register
    5 Failed-sample library index volume index lines match the tagged samples (Chapter 39)
    6 Skill matrix volume and assessment stubs roster count matches the certificate wall (43.6)
    7 Unresolved items unclosed failure analyses and unfilled missing-page lists, handed over in writing item by item
    8 Archive inspection log the latest round of ten-day and quarterly check records continuous, no gaps

    The consequence of an unclear handover is fixed in writing: the incoming custodian bears no responsibility for items left unchecked on the handover list; the outgoing custodian remains responsible for checked items until the next handover takes over—without this rule, the archives rot in a fog of "it isn't mine."

    43.3 The Ledger System: The Transcription Institution for Empirical Parameters¶

    43.3.1 The transcription institution: the form where Iron Law Six lands¶

    Every parameter in the book marked "empirical value, must be field-calibrated" (Chapter 41 paper and ink, the Chapter 39 life table, the Chapters 18–20 smelting and forging parameters... every chapter has them) has exactly one destination after calibration: transcription into the ledger. This is the transmission rule already fixed in the General Constitution; this chapter makes it a form: within 3 days of completing calibration, the calibrator personally transcribes it into the process parameter ledger; from then on, the next batch of people may not work from memory—they may only consult the ledger. A lost ledger, or a row long un-updated, demotes that process back to tier one (able to recite the step names, unable to reproduce the parameters)—demotion is not punishment; it is an honest declaration: with the books gone, "repeatable manufacture" is empty talk.

    Three disciplines of transcription, each aimed at one form of rot:

    1. Single-line strike-through: when a value changes, the old value is struck through with a single line and kept legible; the new value is written beside it, with the date and the dual-signature number noted (per Chapter 39 change control). Blotting the old value out with a blob of ink is forbidden—that struck-through old value is the only clue, twenty years later, to "why was this changed back then."
    2. Review countersignature: every transcribed row must be checked by a second person (grade 2 or above) against its source (the calibration record page) and countersigned. Single-person transcription demotes the ledger into a private notebook.
    3. Card–ledger cross-reference: once a parameter enters the ledger, a "see ledger" stamp is applied at that parameter on the corresponding process card (the workstation copy)—the workstation card carries the manual default; the local current value is governed by the ledger; wherever the two conflict, the ledger prevails, without exception.

    43.3.2 The ledger format: six columns¶

    Uniform six columns; one quarto page holds about 12–16 rows; one volume per ledger type, independently paginated:

    Parameter name (chapter-section) Manual default Local field-calibrated value Calibration date Calibrated by Re-verification interval
    Example: soda-lime glass annealing point (Chapter 14) 500–540 ℃ 520 ℃ (this kiln) 26-03-12 Zhang San every season / with each new sand batch

    Notes column (page footer): for any row on the page that has been changed, record the change's dual-signature number and reason, per Chapter 39 Section 39.7. The re-verification interval is not "whenever someone happens to remember to look"—it is a hard deadline: when it falls due, recalibration is mandatory. The defaults, sorted by the parameter's stability class:

    Parameter class Examples Default re-verification interval Basis
    Raw-material-batch-bound ore-batch reducibility, charcoal-batch strength, sand-batch composition, lye concentration every batch raw materials change batch to batch (Chapter 39 batch-number system)
    Season-bound retting days, drying duration, corrosion rate, sheet-forming water temperature every season Chapter 41 seasonal windows, Chapter 39 climate records
    Equipment-wear-bound bearing clearance, furnace-lining erosion, crucible wall thickness with spot inspection / semi-annually Chapter 39 spot-inspection intervals
    Inherent process cycle annealing point, sizing ratio, soot-to-glue ratio annually (annual inspection day) the parameter itself is stable

    A row that falls due without re-verification is flagged red at the monthly check; flagged red for two consecutive cycles, the parameter reverts to the manual default and the reversion is entered in the inspection log—better a conservative value with a stated source than a "seemed fine last time" of unknown provenance.

    43.3.3 The three core ledgers (interfaces only; content not repeated)¶

    Ledger What it records Chapter interface Where it is archived
    Materials ledger five-column materials in/out/stock, batches and sources, monthly stocktake Chapter 38 material-and-energy ledger (format and institution entirely in that chapter) monthly pages bound with the monthly stocktake; annual volumes into the technical archive
    Process parameter ledger six-column format (43.3.2), collecting every "empirical value, must be field-calibrated" item across the chapters newly established in this chapter; every row points back to its source chapter-section the current volume is an active archive; annual volumes into the technical archive
    Equipment spot-inspection ledger daily/weekly/monthly/annual four-tier spot-inspection records, quantified values Chapter 39 Card 39-01 (format and intervals entirely in that chapter) monthly pages bound with the monthly check; annual volumes into the technical archive

    The three ledgers in one sentence: the materials ledger governs "what comes in," the process parameter ledger governs "how you use it," and the spot-inspection ledger governs "what you use to do it"—the three are interlocked by Chapter 39 batch numbers: one failure analysis (that chapter's five-step method) opens all three books at once. The physical protection of the ledger volumes themselves follows the 43.2 technical-archive standard, with current volumes kept in the active-archive compartment.

    How the ledger is used matters more than its format: a newcomer's first lesson is not the craft; it is learning to look things up—hand them a parameter name, and within 10 min they must get from the catalog to the ledger row and read out the local current value and the re-verification date. Assess "looking up," not "memorizing": pick 3 people and ask each for one parameter of their post; the correct action is to open the ledger and point to it for the examiner. One who blurts the answer from memory gets the follow-up question: "is that the number in the books?" Not remembering is fine, so long as the books exist; being confident while the books are gone—that is where accidents begin.

    43.4 The Negative-Results Preservation Institution¶

    43.4.1 Expanding the failed-sample library (continuing Chapter 39; only three additions)¶

    Chapter 39 Section 39.6.2 already built the failed-sample library's four zones (fracture / corrosion / wear / rejects) and the four-field tag; this chapter does not repeat its operation, only fills three gaps between it and the archive institution and the master–apprentice system:

    1. Paper-record copy: the tag content of every item entering the library is transcribed onto a one-page "negative results record" (format in 43.4.2), cross-referenced with the sample number—sample tagged H26-031, the record page's header reads H26-031. Samples rust, break, and get disposed of at quarterly counts (untagged items are remelted per Chapter 39); the page does not. Once the physical item is gone, the page is the last surviving evidence of that failure.
    2. Open for teaching: the sample library is open to masters and apprentices for its Chapter 39 intended purpose—each quarterly count day doubles as an apprenticeship walk-through day, lecturing along the shelves from the index volume (item name / date / batch number / one-line conclusion). One real fracture beats ten verbal explanations; this is the cheapest teaching equipment in the book.
    3. Cross-check against scattering: at the annual unsealing count, the paper-page count is checked against the in-library sample count, hunting for gaps in both directions—samples without pages: backfill the page within a deadline (transcribed from the tag fields from memory, marked "supplementary entry"); pages without samples: note "physical item lost / disposed of" and the date at the page head.

    43.4.2 The one-page "negative results record" format (five columns)¶

    Whenever there is a first-article rejection of a batch, an abnormally retired item (Chapter 39 Section 39.5.1), a failed experiment, or a near miss (no one hurt—but it came close), the on-duty person drafts within 24 h and a craftsman of grade 2 or above closes the case within 5 days; one page, five columns, quarto:

    Column What goes in it Counter-example (unacceptable entries)
    Goal what you were trying to achieve, against which card and which criterion "wanted to smelt good iron" (no criterion to work from = no goal)
    Method full transcription of the actual parameters: furnace/batch numbers; every deviation from the card listed item by item copying the card without recording the deviations (the deviations are the information)
    Observations observation + physical items: what the failure looked like; sample number, sketch, weighed and measured values "the results were poor"
    Cause analysis if the five-step method (Chapter 39 Card 39-02) was run, copy the three-line conclusion (root cause / evidence / fix); if not, write "pending analysis" and the responsible person "the material was bad" (the lazy answer Chapter 39 expressly bans)
    What to change next time land it as a process card revision number, or write "maintain, no change" with the reasons blank (a failure with no fix will recur exactly the same)

    A page that cannot fill the five columns is not gray-filed; it is returned for rework—if it cannot be filled, the failure has not yet been thought through; what would be archived is confusion, not knowledge. Near-miss pages add a further line, "had it happened, who would have been hurt," and are copied to the Chapter 37 safety entry.

    43.4.3 Why failure records are worth more than successful recipes¶

    Intuition says the thing to transmit is the successful recipe; the arithmetic says otherwise:

    1. Success may owe to luck; failure always has a cause. A good heat of iron may mean the ore happened to be dry, the blast happened to be steady, the veteran happened to be on duty—success records often omit their conditions; every failure, by contrast, really does have a cause, and finding it yields one piece of certain knowledge.
    2. Success teaches one road; failure fences off a whole field of wrong roads. A recipe that fails nine times in ten replications shows the success record omitted a key condition—that alone deserves a negative-results page ("reproducibility of this recipe unverified"). A negative result is searched-and-cleared territory: the next generation need not walk the same dead end again, and what is saved is exactly the reviewers' "repeating the same trial and error."
    3. Failure records carry their own correction loop. In the five columns, "what to change next time" must land on a card revision number (Chapter 39), and recurrence of the same failure type triggers a review of the earlier closing (that chapter's institution)—successful recipes have no such loop; when they are wrong, the error merely gets read as "declining craftsmanship."

    From this, one counterintuitive archiving priority: if only one page of an event can be kept, keep the failure page. The success page records "it worked this time"; the failure page preserves "why, how far off, what to do next time"—the reader twenty years on needs the latter.

    43.5 The Tacit-Knowledge List and the Teaching Method for ◎ Techniques¶

    43.5.1 The principle: the hand-feel cannot be written on paper, but the practice method and the criteria can¶

    Throughout the book, steps that cannot be fully put into words are marked ◎. Their hand-feel substance—the muscles' sense of measure, the senses' calibration—cannot be written onto any page; this is not a defect of paper but the shape of the knowledge itself: tacit knowledge exists only in the person who has it, and transmission must be person to person. But every ◎ technique can be split into three writable things: which variable it controls, how many practice repetitions establish it, and what measurable compensating evidence verifies acceptance. This section gathers the ◎ techniques scattered across the chapters into one master table—the table itself is a technical archive (Class T), the course register of the master–apprentice system (43.6), and the direct antidote to the reviewers' criticism that "key skills rest in single hands": for every technique on this table, the settlement must keep at least two people who can practice it and one who can teach it.

    43.5.3 Three steps of teaching: demonstrate—imitate—blind test¶

    Every ◎ technique is taught in the same fixed three steps, written into the daily routine of both master and apprentice:

    1. Demonstration: the master works while calling out the key points—not adjectives, but variable names and criterion values ("the resistance has gone soft now; the small sample should be showing fuzz"). Every demonstration must leave physical evidence: the point count after this stroke, the slag sample from this heat.
    2. Imitation: the apprentice repeats the prescribed number of times on scrap / practice pieces per the practice method, leaving physical evidence every time (test bars, small samples, point-count records); the master corrects but never does the work. Doing it once for the apprentice steals one chance to err—and the hand-feel grows precisely out of corrected errors.
    3. Blind test: the apprentice works alone; acceptance is by the physical-evidence criteria; the master does not watch the process, only examines the evidence left behind—are the points enough, the fracture right, the deviation band inside limits? Only passing the blind test counts as "knowing it"; doing it right under the master's eye counts only as "knowing it while watched."

    One sentence to sum up this section, and the teaching philosophy of the master–apprentice system: the hand-feel cannot be written on paper, but the practice method and the criteria can; the master will die someday, but dismantled into "practice method + criteria + physical evidence," his hand-feel can be replayed in every new apprentice's hands.

    43.6 The Master–Apprentice System and Skill Certification¶

    43.6.1 Four job grades: splitting "can do it" into verifiable steps¶

    This section refines the levels of the Chapter 37 Section 37.5 skill matrix into four named grades, each with criteria and authority—a grade is not seniority; it is "what physical evidence has been examined":

    Grade Name Criteria (what you can do, what evidence is examined) Authority
    1 Apprentice operates under grade-2 supervision; can read cards, transcribe cards, consult the ledger (passed the 43.3 query assessment) forbidden to operate the four high-hazard process classes alone; ledger entries require a grade-2 reviewer's signature
    2 Proficient completes all routine operations of the post independently, self-checked against physical-evidence criteria; holds at least one post certificate operates routine processes alone; authority to countersign ledger reviews; no change authority
    3 Independent includes abnormal-condition handling: diagnose from the failure-mode table, run the first two steps of the five-step method (secure the scene, collect all parameters, Chapter 39), take charge of 1 apprentice condemnation authority (Chapter 39 Section 39.5.1), one party of the change dual-signature, chairs the weekly check
    4 Can teach able to teach: has passed the Card 47-03 teaching assessment + has independently brought 1 apprentice to grade 2 the other party of the change dual-signature (with the workshop master), chairs the annual inspection, issues certificates

    Promotion assessment follows Card 39-03, with one iron rule against the master–apprentice system turning into exploitation: the examiner may not be the examinee's own teaching master—master and apprentice see each other daily, and favor and habit both distort assessment. The assessment panel is two people, at least one of whom has no teacher–student lineage with the examinee. An apprentice who spends three years under a master without a single promotion-assessment record counts as a breach by the settlement (not the apprentice): the master's teaching qualification is suspended, and the apprentice transfers to another workshop under another master.

    43.6.2 Assessment formats: live operation + blind interpretation + troubleshooting drill¶

    Grade-3 and grade-4 assessment consists of three formats, each testing one face:

    Format How it is tested What it verifies
    Live operation draw 1 card from the examinee's own post's process cards, complete it within a time limit, accept physical evidence only (test bars, point counts, fracture, deviation band), never the look of the motions can the hands do it
    Blind interpretation interpret samples without being told their origin: slag-color series readings (43.5 table #5), the three fracture morphologies (beach marks / coarse grain / fibrous, Chapter 39), grading on the tempering-color plates, judging clearance from the three sound states—at least 10 items per set, read aloud and recorded on the spot are the eyes and ears accurate
    Troubleshooting drill a preset fault, walked through its search sequence within a time limit: the generator "spins but no power" search table (Chapter 31), the three checks for bearings over 60 ℃ (Chapter 20), "ink feathers—recheck the washing" (Chapter 41), two conflicting ledger values (43.3)—answered in the verification order of "one symptom, many causes" is the head clear

    Blind-test pass line ≥8/10 (empirical value; calibrate to the distribution after the first assessment round); the drill's criterion is the order is right—the value of a search table is common causes first, rare causes after; skipping steps and leaping straight to "strange material" is a fail (the discipline of step three of the Chapter 39 five-step method).

    43.6.3 The authorization institution: certification for four classes of high-hazard processes¶

    For any process involving high temperature, high pressure, acutely toxic media, or electricity, being able to do it is not the same as being permitted to do it. Four certificate classes; without the certificate, do not touch; certificates are posted on the wall (the certificate wall is the public face of the skill register):

    Certificate Processes covered Assessment focus Retraining interval (empirical)
    Smelting certificate tending the fire, tapping iron and slag, slag-color reading (Chapters 12/16/18) live operation + slag-color blind test + burn treatment 1 year
    Steam certificate hydrostatic test, startup and paralleling, boiler watch (Chapter 28) live operation + overpressure emergency drill 1 year
    Quench certificate quenching, tempering, taking the piece at the tempering color (Chapter 19) tempering-color blind test + fracture interpretation 1 year
    Electrical certificate wiring, energizing, proving de-energized (Chapter 31) troubleshooting drill + oral exam on the safety rules 6 months

    A certificate lapses into suspension automatically when retraining is overdue; touching that process while suspended is logged as a violation; anyone off work more than three months for injury or illness must re-take the original certificate before returning—a certificate's validity belongs to the hands of today, not to a name from the past.

    43.6.4 Key-post redundancy and emergency takeover¶

    This translates the Chapter 37 labor-organization principle into the following iron rule: for every key post at grade 3 (independent) or above, the settlement must keep ≥2 people on the roster—above all the four posts of furnace front, steam watch, quenching, and power distribution. Three supporting measures:

    1. Single-point knowledge list: every skill of the "only one person knows it" kind (mostly ◎ techniques and implicit handling experience) is inventoried quarterly onto a risk list (active archive), each item marked with "second-person training progress." Any item on the list whose second-person count is zero is deemed to be in a countdown.
    2. Emergency takeover plan: if any key person is injured or falls ill, the rostered second person takes over within 24 h; the plan is written into the shift roster, including "which processes run degraded after takeover" (any process short of two certified people stops outright—better a stopped furnace than a violation).
    3. Annual takeover drill: each year one day is drawn at random, and each key post's second person runs the post all day while the incumbent only observes and records—chain breaks found in the drill (oral experience that cannot be handed over, ledger rows that cannot be found) are registered on the spot and remedied by a deadline. The drill's purpose is not to prove that takeover works; it is to find, while nothing is wrong, the places where it would not.

    A fixed half-day each week (suggested: rainy days or slack-farm-season slots, Chapter 37 scheduling rules), all personnel trained in rotation, three contents rotating:

    43.7.4 The handover year¶

    In a veteran craftsman's late years (or the year before a planned departure), they work a full round of four seasons side by side with the designated successor: spring (material preparation, retting, sowing, Chapters 7/10), summer (rush harvest and drying, rainy-season protection), autumn (the smelting high season, Chapter 18), winter (overhaul, annual inspection, annual sealing, Chapter 39). Three rules: the successor is the main operator; the elder only corrects (the operation-signature records show it at a glance); all operations through the year leave physical evidence; at year's end the elder signs an "independent" recommendation and hands over their personal experience entries. After the elder's death, their unrecorded oral experience is verified entry by entry into the ledger by the successor within three years; what cannot be verified is marked "unverified, doubtful"—never taken on faith: sparing the dead's face is burying accidents for posterity.

    Degraded entry for non-literate settlements: where literacy is not yet universal, transmission degrades to "physical samples + oral transmission + two-person operation witnessing"—every process must have two people at the post witnessing each other; study sessions become oral teaching + demonstration; card transcription is deferred; the archives are backfilled once the Chapter 4 teaching system produces literate craftsmen. This is degraded operation and must be declared on the master catalog's first page.

    Safety Boundaries¶

    This chapter is mostly paper institutions, but the following items are part of executing the institution; failing to execute counts as failing this chapter (Iron Law Seven):

    1. Archive-room fire prevention: carried over from Chapter 41—no open flame around the cabinets, minimal single-flame lamp with the rule "person leaves, lamp goes out," ≥15 m from furnaces and kilns; if fire destroys the originals, the off-site copies are the only fallback, so the duplicate system's checks rank equal with fire prevention.
    2. Lime-slaking injuries: replacing the lime cloth bags follows the full Chapter 13 protection set (face veil, oiled gloves, upwind position, added to water in batches), two-person work—the highest-risk routine operation in the archive room (Chapter 41 standing rule).
    3. Failed-sample library: fracture edges are sharp; wear heavy leather gloves for carrying; acid-etched coupons, lead-bearing slag samples, and green vitriol samples are stored in separate compartments with red tags (Chapter 8 hazardous-materials rules), never in the same cabinet as paper records; wash hands after handling.
    4. Teaching sites: uncertified apprentices are forbidden inside the operating radius of the four high-hazard process classes (furnace front, boiler, quench tank, power distribution board); observation zones are cordoned off with lines; during demonstrations apprentices watch but do not touch; slag-color and fracture samples for blind tests are prepared under Chapter 12 and Chapter 19 protection respectively (eye protection; burn treatment on hand at the post).
    5. Airing and quarterly checks: the mold and dust of aired archives harm the respiratory tract; wear a mask (Chapter 15 workwear); volumes found with mold spots are isolated and sun-dried, never beaten with bare hands.
    6. Labor protection: the handover year must not work injured or ailing veteran craftsmen beyond their strength under the banner of "mentoring"—the elder's role is to correct, not to fill a labor shortfall (Chapter 37 labor protection); study-session hours are work hours, not rest.
