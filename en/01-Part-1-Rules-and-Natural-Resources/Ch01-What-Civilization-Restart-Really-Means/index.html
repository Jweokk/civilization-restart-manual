    Chapter 1 What Civilization Restart Really Means¶

    Chapter Objectives¶

    • Establish three judgment capabilities:

    • Determine the restart starting point you are at (A/B/C);

    • Determine which level of L0–L3 any given technology is at;

    • For any target technology, list its dependency chain and find the critical path and the bottleneck.

    • This chapter builds no physical artifacts. Its deliverables are four "paper tools" (the starting-point determination checklist, the level determination table, the dependency-tracing four-question card, and the blank dependency graph template), which every later chapter will invoke.

    • This chapter also defines the book's lowest-layer boundary rules: what counts as "direct use of natural objects" and what counts as a "tool".

    Item Requirement Source
    Knowledge The seven Iron Laws (no crossing, precision has a source, materials have chains, acceptance is provable, technology has a fallback, every process must have failure criteria and evidence, labor is the first asset) General Constitution G01
    Knowledge The three-level acceptance system and the chapter template General Constitution G02

    1.1 The Three Restart Starting Points¶

    The three starting points are the fork for every technology route in the book. They must be determined on day one and written up as a "starting-point checklist".

    Starting point Initial conditions Main challenge Role of this manual
    A: Pure natural starting point No tools, containers, clothing, or any artifacts Surviving and manufacturing from natural resources Baseline verification line (Part 2 is written to this standard)
    B: Few leftover items starting point Clothing, a few metal tools, and containers, but no way to replenish them Using the existing tools to build a sustainable production system Accelerated route (chapters flag "leftover-item shortcuts")
    C: Knowledge-retained starting point Modern scientific knowledge remembered, but no modern industrial goods Converting knowledge into actual production capability Main line

    Determination steps (complete on day one):

    1. Inventory the artifacts available within 24 hours: clothing, metal tools, containers, cordage.
    2. Rule: ≥1 metal tool present and not replenishable → B; no artifacts at all → A; modern knowledge in the group's memory and no industrial goods → C.
    3. Record the determination result together with the inventory details in the starting-point checklist. Mixed states (e.g., clothing but no tools) are handled at the lowest tier.

    Starting-point resource inventory — every starting point holds three classes of assets:

    1. The body: hands, fingernails, teeth, body temperature, physical strength, stride, heartbeat. These are the first "measuring instruments" (formally put to use in Chapter 3).
    2. Natural objects: stones, sticks, vines, shells, and animal bones that can be picked up and used as-is. **Boundary rule (valid book-wide): an unprocessed natural object picked up and used directly in the hand does not constitute tool crossing; once it has been chipped, ground, or carved into shape, it counts as a tool and belongs to the deliverables of Chapter 5 and later.** Wherever later chapters write "striking with a stone held in the hand", they mean the usage this rule permits.
    3. Knowledge and record-keeping capability: the core asset of starting point C. But knowledge must first pass the "memory reliability" check — a wrong memory is more dangerous than no memory (see Section C of this chapter).

    Failure modes:

    • Misjudging the starting point. The most common error at starting point B: treating a leftover steel knife as a sustainable capability, using it until it wears out and fails, and never building the capability to remake it (usable iron stock does not arrive until Chapter 18). Rule: leftover items may only be used to accelerate the making of "lower-level tools that can be self-sustained" (example: use the steel knife to carve the first standard rod, then immediately switch to a route that can be sustained without the steel knife).

    • Treating the knowledge of starting point C as the whole of your assets. Knowledge is a route map, not production capacity. See 1.2.

    Three rules for leftover-item management at starting point B (to prevent "eating the mountain bare"):

    1. Register and catalog: give every leftover item an archive entry (item name, material, current condition, date placed in service), incorporated into the Chapter 3 archive system.
    2. Tier the usage: use them only to accelerate the making of "lower-level tools that can be self-sustained" or for one-time reference-standard making; everyday consumptive use is forbidden. Example: a leftover steel knife may not serve as a kitchen knife; it may only carve the first batch of standard rods.
    3. Failure contingency: annotate next to every leftover item "after damage, replaced by the deliverables of Chapter N"; items for which no replacement plan can be written are treated as "end-of-life resources" — the replacement route must be built before they are used.

    1.2 What Counts as Truly Rebuilding a Technology¶

    The book splits "possessing a technology" into four levels; levels must never be mixed in reporting:

    Level Name Criterion
    L0 Knowledge comprehensible Without the manual, can write out the principle and the main process steps
    L1 Proof of principle Done once successfully, with records of date and observed behavior, but unstable
    L2 Repeatable manufacture Following the recorded process, produce conforming pieces 3 times in a row (the conformity criteria must be written down first)
    L3 Sustainable production Damaged tools can be repaired or remade; raw-material supply interruptions can be bridged; another person following the records can produce

    Level-up conditions (interrogate level by level):

    • L0→L1: what is missing is one hands-on run. Schedule the time and the materials.

    • L1→L2: what is missing are parameters and records. Write out the conditions of every run in full (material batches, parameters, weather) and identify what distinguished successes from failures.

    • L2→L3: what is missing is the system. Answer: what is the tool attrition rate? What are the raw-material resupply routes? Who knows the second way of doing it? Where are the spares?

    Three telltale signals of "treating L1 as L3" (self-check):

    1. No failure records — only successes and no failures means you have not tried often enough.
    2. No second product — something made only once is not a technology; it is an event.
    3. No consumables replenishment plan — if you cannot answer "what happens when the raw materials run out", you have not reached L3.

    False-claim phrasing cross-reference (for self-check):

    Common claim Actual level Corrective action
    "I studied it and watched someone do it" At most L0 Schedule one hands-on run
    "I made one once" L1 Make 3 conforming pieces in a row, with records throughout
    "Following the process, it basically works" Below L2 Add the parameter window and the failure records
    "I can make it whenever I want anyway" Unverified Aid-interruption test + reproduction by a different person (see 1.4)

    Running example (a preview only; the artifact is established in Chapter 5): an edged stone tool. Being able to state "flaking stone requires homogeneous rock with a conchoidal fracture" = L0; having flaked one stone flake that can cut a grass stem = L1; flaking 3 flakes in a row, each able to cut 20 dry grass stems without edge chipping = L2; the stone source recorded on the resource map, the flaking hammer replaceable by re-picking and re-dressing after wear, and another person able to flake by following the records = L3.

    1.3 Why Modern Technology Cannot Be Copied Directly¶

    Behind every modern product stand four layers of hidden dependencies, and none of them exists in the early restart phase:

    Hidden dependency Default condition of the modern world Early-restart reality
    Energy density Power grid and liquid fuel, available on demand Only muscle, fuel wood, and fire (from Chapter 6)
    Material purity Reagent-grade chemicals, specification steel Only minerals, clay, and biomass (from Chapter 2)
    Standardization Tolerances, interchangeable parts, thread standards Not even a unified unit of length (from Chapter 3)
    Supply chain Thousands of factories behind a single device Everything within a one-day walking radius (Chapter 2 resource map)

    The "chase three layers" exercise (to be done once in this chapter): pick any modern object and interrogate three rounds in a row: "What is it made of? → Where do those things come from? → What, in turn, are the processing tools for those things?"

    • If you hit bottom within three layers (landing on natural objects such as stone, wood, clay, and fire) → the object can be attempted relatively early.

    • If you still have not reached bottom after three layers (example: stainless steel knife → steel mill → iron ore + coke + refractory bricks → chase each of those one more layer) → the upstream must be built first; a direct attempt is not allowed.

    Conclusion: the true asset of starting point C is "knowing where to go and knowing which roads are dead ends", not "already owning". The manual's task is to exchange "knowing" for L1, L2, and L3, chapter by chapter.

    Four common illusions of knowledge retainers (check one by one):

    1. "Understanding the principle means knowing how to make it" — the principle is only L0; between it and L2 lie the parameter window and the failure library (3.7).
    2. "Skip the old technology and go straight to the better solution" — the preconditions of the better solution (materials, measuring tools, energy) are established precisely by the old technology.
    3. "Modern numbers can be taken over as they are" — without standard gauges, numbers such as "a few millimeters" or "so many degrees" have no measurement source (Iron Law 2) and can only be expressed through the self-built references of Chapter 3.
    4. "One person can be all-capable" — the depth of division of labor in modern technology dictates that rebuilding must be divided too (1.4); the realistic ceiling of a solo route ends at Part 2.

    1.4 From Making One Piece to a Production System¶

    Making one part ≠ establishing a system that can keep producing that part. The six minimal elements of a system (checked item by item at every chapter's L3 acceptance):

    Element Interrogation Record medium
    Raw-material stock Current stock? Resupply cycle? Resource map (Chapter 2)
    Energy consumption rate How much firewood burned / how many labor-hours per unit of product? Process card (3.7)
    Tool attrition rate Which tools will fail? How often? What happens when they do? Tool archive (Chapter 3 archive system)
    Process records Are the parameters and the failure cases written down? Clay-tablet archive (3.6)
    Quality inspection What are the conformity criteria? Who inspects? Acceptance criteria (each chapter)
    Manpower and succession How many people can do it? Does it still work when the person changes? Mentoring records (3.7)

    Two quantitative criteria:

    1. Output rate = number of conforming pieces ÷ total labor-hours invested. Start tracking only after L2.
    2. Sustainability criterion: resource consumption rate ≤ resource regeneration/resupply rate. Example (previewing Chapter 11): the wood consumed by one kiln load of pottery must fall within the annual regeneration of that forest stand; otherwise pottery production will cut off its own supply within a few years.

    The blunt test of whether a system exists: the aid-interruption test — imagine (or actually) stop all external inputs for 30 days: can this technology's output still be sustained? Only what can be sustained counts as "possessed" by the settlement.

    **Example (the numbers are fictitious; the format is for imitation):** A settlement produces 30 pottery jars per month, 24 of them conforming, investing 60 working days → output rate 0.4 jars/labor-day; fuel consumption is 3 dan of firewood per kiln load, 4 kiln loads per month; measured against the forest stand's annual regeneration of 200 dan → about 70 percent taken up. Record both figures once each month; the trend matters more than any single value: when the output rate falls for three consecutive months, check tool attrition and raw-material batches first instead of pushing for more labor-hours.

    Failure mode: treating "a certain artisan can make it" as "the settlement can produce it". The artisan falls ill and production stops = the system lacks element 6. The countermeasure is in 3.7 (from personal experience to replicable processes).

    1.5 The Technology Tree, Bottlenecks, and Dependencies¶

    Backward dependency tracing (the four-question card): for any target technology, answer in order:

    1. What does making it require? (tools, materials, energy, knowledge — the four dependencies, expanded in Chapter 4)
    2. How are those things themselves made? (repeat the question for each item until you land on the natural resources of Chapter 2 or on deliverables already established in earlier chapters)
    3. Which tools in the manufacturing process will be damaged, used up, or need replacement?
    4. If one of them is missing, can another route be taken? (alternative routes must state the performance loss, the resource cost, the applicability boundaries, and the final bottleneck — Iron Law 5)

    Example (quoted from the outline, for imitation): iron chisel ← usable iron stock ← reduction of iron ore and forging ← furnaces/kilns and charcoal ← refractory materials and temperature-control capability ← pottery kilns, fuel processing, and basic measurement. Side branches: abrasives, hand hammers, clamping devices, edge-quality judgment methods, re-heat-treatment capability. The artifacts of the links are established in Chapters 18, 12, 11, 6, 3, and 2 respectively.

    Second example (the reader should practice once in this format): pottery jar ← clay purification and batching ← pottery kiln and fuel ← refractory materials ← controlled combustion ← tinder and dry firewood ← resource map. Side branches: shrinkage-rate records (Chapter 3 gauges), firing cycle (Chapter 3 time anchor), acceptance criteria (watertightness and resistance to thermal cycling — Iron Law 4). The artifacts fall in Chapters 11, 12, 6, 3, and 2 respectively. The common node of the two chains is "controlled combustion and the resource map" — it blocks both chains at once, and that is the bottleneck: manpower goes to bottlenecks first, because once one is cleared, several chains advance at the same time.

    Definitions of critical path and bottleneck:

    • Critical path = the longest prerequisite chain from the starting point to the target. It determines the earliest completion time; the other chains can run in parallel.

    • Bottleneck = a node on which multiple downstream items depend at the same time (examples: a controlled fire source is the common prerequisite of pottery, charcoal, lime, and metallurgy; the measurement system is the common prerequisite of almost all L2 acceptances). Bottlenecks should receive manpower first.

    Priority decision (quoted from General Constitution G04): priority = survival contribution × resource availability × repeatable-manufacture capability × supporting value for subsequent technologies. This is a discussion framework, not a computable formula. The accompanying five questions: if we do not build it, what gets stuck? Is there a cheaper substitute? How much nonrenewable resource does it consume? Can it raise the efficiency of other links? When is it actually worth adding people?

    Deliverable: the blank dependency graph template. Node fields: name / source chapter number / current level (L0–L3) / side-branch dependencies / alternative routes. Maintain it on inscribed clay tablets or a ground-inscription area (medium in 3.6). Rule: until any node reaches its labeled chapter, its status on the drawing is always "not yet built", no matter how sure you are that you could make it.

    C. Unresolved Dependencies¶

    • There is no way to verify whether the reader's memory of modern knowledge is accurate. Wrong memories contaminate the entire dependency chain. Mitigation: key numbers and criteria are cross-checked against the independent recall of ≥2 people; where they conflict, physical experiments prevail; the quantitative verification method is established in Chapter 25.

    • No physical production capability of any kind yet — this depends on every manufacturing chapter from Chapter 5 onward.

    • The problems of manpower scale and division of labor can only be raised here, not solved (handled by the per-environment versions of Part 10).

    Failure Modes and Diagnosis [methods chapter]¶

    This chapter has no processes; its failure modes are method misuse: (1) treating leftover items as sustainable capability (bootstrap-line preview in Chapter 18); (2) treating previews as existing (criteria in Chapter 4); (3) memory contamination (key numbers cross-checked by ≥2 people; quantitative verification awaits Chapter 25).

    Process Card Index [methods chapter]¶

    This chapter sets no process cards — its deliverables are method pieces (checklists/tables/cycles), see inventory B; the cards of every process chapter are used with the 4.7 cycle of this chapter.

    Safety Boundaries¶

    • No physical operations in this chapter.

    • The single clause: no one may cite "knowing the principle" as a reason to skip verification and go straight to hazardous processes (high pressure, high temperature, toxic substances). The relevant hard prohibitions are in each chapter's "safety boundaries" block; in Part 6 (steam, electricity, chemicals) especially, proceeding on knowledge alone is absolutely not allowed.

    Acceptance Criteria¶

    • L1 proof of principle: for a given technology (example: pottery jar), can state the L0–L3 differences and one's own current level, and can say whether one is caught by any of the three "treating L1 as L3" signals.

    • L2 repeatable manufacture: complete the starting-point checklist for this environment + at least 1 complete backward dependency graph (with side branches and alternative routes), independently reviewed by a second person with the four-question card, and no tool crossing found.

    • L3 sustainable production: ≥2 people in the settlement can independently use this chapter's tools to run dependency analysis on new targets; the deviations between the analysis conclusions and the actual subsequent progress are recorded and used to revise the analysis method itself.

    Parameter Notes¶

    This chapter is a position and methods chapter; its capability boundary is the boundary of the book itself (natural objects may be used directly, every tool must be traceable); it contains no process parameters and no master parameter table; the only quantitative content of this chapter is one capability-level determination (the three-level L0–L3 table, see 1.2). The parameters and criteria of every process chapter fall in the chapters after this one — this chapter pre-issues no numbers, which is in itself a demonstration of the ban on tool crossing.
