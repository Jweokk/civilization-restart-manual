    Appendix G · Civilization-Level Pitfalls, a Methods Curriculum, and Open Questions¶

    Where this appendix sits, and one declaration: the charter of the main text does not write history and does not speculate—that does not change. So this appendix is not counted among the main text: it is a register of "never again," admitting only those things whose cost is large enough to be worth writing down on its own, and written strictly in the three columns temptation–cost–defence line, with no stories told and no prophecies made. Keep it clearly apart from the "common failures" on the process cards: a process failure ruins one batch; a civilization failure ruins a generation.

    Part A — Pitfall Map: Eight Civilization-Level Mistakes¶

    Pitfall 1 Tetraethyl lead: one anti-knock agent traded away a generation's intelligence¶

    • The temptation: tetraethyl lead raised engine power sharply and the profits were enormous; and its safety was "self-certified" by the inventor and the company promoting it—the same firm.
    • The cost: from its spread in the 1920s to the global phase-out in 2021, some seventy years. Over that span lead exposure across the population rose, and the global cost to children's intelligence and to cardiovascular outcomes was measured again and again. The inventor himself (Thomas Midgley Jr.) was later harmed by another of his own inventions.
    • The defence line: (1) any new substance to be added to a public medium (air, water, the food chain) must answer before production begins: where does it finally go, and who metabolizes it; (2) an interested party may not certify its own safety—safety assessment is separated from sales, written into the Chapter 37 rules of order; (3) for the three heavy metals mercury, lead and cadmium, no wording of "a small amount is harmless" may enter any process card; they are managed as cumulative poisons without exception (the Chapter 49 standard).

    Pitfall 2 The romance of radium: luminous paint and "health water"¶

    • The temptation: a newly discovered radioactive element—"the unknown is mysterious, and the mysterious is universal"—radium water, radium toothpaste, glow-in-the-dark watch dials.
    • The cost: the radium dial painters died in a body (necrosis of the jaw), and the lawsuits established the precedent for claiming occupational injury; the doctrine of "a trace is beneficial" stayed in circulation for decades.
    • The defence line: (1) swap the default from "unknown = unlimited possibility" to "unknown = unlimited danger"; (2) for radiation phenomena, in an age with no means of detection, the related crafts (luminescence, radioactive mineral handling) are all listed as C-class and not to be done; (3) clustering of illness among workers is a process problem, not a problem of the workers' constitutions (the Chapter 49 principle that clustering is itself a conviction).

    Pitfall 3 Thalidomide: a "safe drug" with no pregnancy test¶

    • The temptation: a powerful sedative and anti-emetic; the animal studies "passed."
    • The cost: about ten thousand cases of phocomelia; the lesson gave rise to modern drug scheduling and the system of contraindications in pregnancy.
    • The defence line: (1) Chapter 46 already has the rudiment—for a pregnant woman, every drug still under trial is forbidden by default; (2) cross-species extrapolation must be discounted: an animal "pass" is not human safety; (3) a cluster of birth defects is an epidemic alarm, and the reporting channel (Chapter 47) must stay open at all times. Whoever reports is a hero; whoever conceals is a butcher.

    Pitfall 4 Chlorofluorocarbons: perfect for the user, a disaster for the whole¶

    • The temptation: not flammable, not toxic, not corrosive—"safer" than every substitute available at the time.
    • The cost: depletion of stratospheric ozone, and another dozen-plus years from the paper to the global phase-out. "Safe for the user" does not mean "safe for the whole."
    • The defence line: (1) "safe" must be given boundaries: safe for whom, for how long, spreading to where; (2) once a volatile substance is to be made at scale, answer first what happens when it enters the atmosphere; this generation cannot run the chemical tests, so refrigeration and aerosol volatile chemicals are all listed as long-term, and the medium term cools by the evaporation of ammonia, ice or water—ammonia is toxic, but its smell is the alarm, and a leak is known at once, and that property is itself a defence line.

    Pitfall 5 Minamata disease: the factory said "there is no evidence"¶

    • The temptation: dumping mercury-bearing wastewater straight out was the cheapest option; the victims were fishermen with no voice.
    • The cost: thousands of people with permanent central nervous system damage, and more than a decade dragged out between the discharge and the admission.
    • The defence line: (1) the destination of every discharge must be registered (the Chapter 43 ledger plus the water-source map), with downstream intake points marked at the same time; (2) a cluster of cases convicts the discharge (the generalization of the Chapter 45 water-source-to-case mapping method); (3) "no evidence that it is harmful" is not "evidence that it is harmless"—the burden of proof lies with the discharger.

    Pitfall 6 Institution-level: killed by the ruler we built ourselves¶

    • The temptation: output, speed, pass rate—countable indicators are easy to manage.
    • The cost: history repeats "indicators met, substance rotten": scrapped goods hidden from the books, safety steps cut to make schedule, "no adverse reactions" reported falsely. The narrower the indicator, the more thorough the distortion.
    • The defence line: (1) every indicator is paired with a counter-indicator (output × rework rate, speed × accident rate, number of subjects × follow-up rate); (2) negative results and bad news are rewarded (the governance version of the Chapter 43 negative-results archive); (3) changing an indicator = opening a new version, and swapping the ruler quietly is forbidden (the governance version of the Chapter 42 change discipline).

    Pitfall 7 The two ways knowledge dies¶

    • Death one: the carrier breaks—one book, one person, one library. The defence line = the 3×3×3 of Appendix F.
    • Death two: canonization—knowledge is sealed as sacred and testing is cancelled. The defence line is a single sentence, worth printing on the title page: when any clause conflicts with a reproducible measurement, the clause yields; the highest authority of this book is "the next batch," not the last edition.

    Pitfall 8 "Wait until conditions are better"¶

    • The temptation: build the plant, raise the output, go to high pressure—get it running first, and add safety and environmental protection "in phase two."
    • The cost: in the post-mortem of nearly every major accident there is one line reading "we knew at the time, and said to wait."
    • The defence line: the General Outline's list of "what we explicitly cannot do" is not a schedule, it is a contract—if the four conditions are not all met, high-pressure steam is not to be lit; any "special circumstance" is handled under the hard gate of Chapter 44: cross the boundary and you stop.

    Part B — The Generator: Scientific Method as an Institution (a Twelve-Lesson Curriculum)¶

    Goal: make "conjecture → disproof → record → reproduce → transmit" the settlement's default motion. Students from age twelve up, half a day per lesson, the course finished in one year; instructors teach by the apprenticeship system of Chapter 43. This part is not popular science, it is the curriculum version of an institution: all twelve lessons land on devices the main text already has, and no new tools are added.

    Lesson Topic Where it lands in the book Exercise
    1 Observation and questions — Record one thing a day that "I did not understand" (phenomena register)
    2 Measurement first Chapters 3, 25 Three groups measure the same object independently; compare the spread
    3 Conjecture and prediction — Put the conjecture into falsifiable words: if A holds, then B should be measured
    4 Control and single variable Chapter 25 Two plots, two furnace fires, two groups of patients
    5 Error and repetition Chapter 25 Take the median of three runs; if the repeat spread is >5%, measure again
    6 Records and archives Chapter 43 Negative and positive results enter the register with equal weight
    7 Reproduction Chapter 43 A second person redoes it independently from the record—a finding that does not reproduce is not a finding
    8 Failure case day Chapter 43 Once a month, tell "which batch I ruined this month, and why"
    9 The boundaries of a model Chapter 29 and others Every rule carries its range of applicability (the waterwheel formula fails in the freezing season)
    10 Grading sources Part C of this appendix Who said this, how did he know, and would it still hold with someone else doing it
    11 Arbitration of disputes Chapter 19 Use the test as final arbiter: if it cannot be settled, make it do one job
    12 Transmission Chapter 43 Teach one lesson you learned this year to one other person

    Completion criterion: the student independently completes one full loop of "problem → conjecture → controlled experiment → record → reproduction → explanation" (any topic in agronomy, craft or medicine, using the existing systems of Chapters 25 and 46).

    Part C — Four Source Tiers: a Label for Every Number¶

    Iron rule two of the General Outline requires that "every figure must have a source." This section is its operating standard, for later revisions to backfill card by card—this edition has not yet applied it book-wide; the rules are being fixed in place first.

    Tier Name Definition Treatment of confidence
    S1 Textbooks and technical history University textbooks, authoritative technical histories May serve as acceptance evidence
    S2 Experimental reports First-hand experimental data (e.g. bloomery furnace run records, experimental archaeology datasets) May serve as acceptance evidence, with the sample size stated
    S3 Practice records First-hand records from workshops and amateurs Reference only; does not support an acceptance criterion on its own
    E Locally self-devised No literature correspondence; extrapolated from the author's experience Must retain the wording "empirical value, to be calibrated to local conditions"

    Labelling format (one entry per card):

    Source: [S2] one furnace charge of 94 kg charcoal yielded a 14 kg iron bloom,
        iron yield about 60% (experimental furnace-run record)
        [E] layer thickness 80–100 mm and the sounding interval are self-devised,
        to be calibrated per furnace

    Where the label goes: 286 cards, and a paragraph repeated 286 times is noise. So this tier is not written out as prose — every card carries its tier in the header ((eight fields · source tier E)), where whoever transcribes it sees it on the first line; Appendix A's master table still recognises card numbers only. Where several sources genuinely meet inside the text (the two-tier production passage in Chapter 18, for instance), the label is written into the sentence in the format above.

    Tie-break rule (several tiers in one card): where a card mixes textbook constants with self-derived ranges, tag the lowest tier — conservatively. The reason is plain: a transcriber carries the whole card, and its criteria are only as trustworthy as the weakest number in it, not the strongest.

    Result of this edition's sweep: all 286 cards were tagged one by one, and the answer is E × 286. This is not laziness; it is the book's actual state. There is no page-by-page literature anchoring anywhere in it: every process range, criterion and calibration step is self-derived and handed to the field to calibrate. The one first-hand experimental anchor (the Sauder & Williams 2002 run record quoted in Chapter 18's two-tier production passage) sits in the body text to show that 5–14 kg per run is reachable with practice; it does not by itself underpin any single card's accept criteria. Tiers S1 and S3 went unused this round — an empty tier is not a missing item; it is the slot left for whoever measures the numbers in a card, one at a time, and fills it in.

    Relation to measurement: for a parameter marked [E], the long-term answer is not to go looking for literature but to measure it against the card—the Chapter 18 L2 criterion (a second person reproduces from the card) already demands this step, and the source label only makes it explicit. A number rises from [E] to [S2] by one reproducible measurement, not by one more persuasive citation.

    Part D — Open Questions: This Book Is a Floor, Not a Ceiling¶

    The book has no answers to the following questions. Each one notes "where things currently stand stuck," and is left to those who come after:

    # Question Current state
    1 Long-term failure rate of the best safety-valve type for low-pressure boilers (deadweight direct-loaded vs lever) Requires more than a year of operating data
    2 Quantitative relation between bloomery slag basicity and iron yield Chapter 18 gives an empirical value
    3 Critical conditions for the loss of writing in a gap period: how many generations of oral transmission, how much parameter drift The basis for launching the Chapter 43 archive system; no measurements
    4 Without antibiotics, the best interval between open drainage and delayed suture Chapter 44's 3–5 days is an empirical value, awaiting backfill from infection-rate data
    5 At the end of the scavenging period, the optimal handover point between local scrap-steel stock and smelting capacity The modelling problem of Appendix E, Section 10
    6 How much readability a multilingual key retains after three generations The basis for Appendix F's ten-year verification
    7 The critical curve between settlement size and the number of specialized work stations The quantitative version of the Chapter 37 population question
    8 Standardizing the local method for measuring the epidemic generation interval Chapter 45 currently gives an empirical value
    9 The transition criterion for a centrifugal governor between manual closed loop and machine closed loop Follow-on to the Chapter 29 preliminary study
    10 Whether the economy of a base-3 notation civilization really has a lower threshold A thought experiment, with no data

    This list is revised once every ten years, together with the "hand-in day" (Appendix F): entries that are cleared move into the main text, and new unknowns are added. A book that writes "we do not know" as a list lives longer than a book that claims to know everything.
