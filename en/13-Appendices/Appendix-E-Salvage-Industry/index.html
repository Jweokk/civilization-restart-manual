    Appendix E · Salvage Industry: The First Two Decades Belong to Scavenging¶

    Where this appendix sits: the main text walks the "start from a stone" route (Starting point C: a purely natural starting point). But the most common starting point of a real break is not open ground — it is urban rubble: industry destroyed outright, buildings still standing, steel still lying in the ruins. That is the stretch this appendix fills in. For the first twenty years, scavenging is the main industry and smelting is its downstream. It is not Part 12, it takes no chapter number, it sets no process card, and the main text and the charter are not changed by a single word. Criteria and processes all carry over from what came before: scrap-steel grading keeps to the four-color library of Chapter 19, dismantling keeps to the safety red lines of Chapter 44, and accounts keep to Chapters 38 and 43. Anywhere marked "empirical value", it must be calibrated to local conditions.

    1. Ruins Are Ore, Only Higher Grade¶

    One economic account is enough to explain why this comes before any smelting: smelting 1 kg of new iron takes 20–35 kg of charcoal (Chapter 18), while 1 kg of already-smelted steel in the ruins costs only hauling and sorting. The steel frame of a medium-sized building runs about 20–60 tonnes — roughly equal to four hundred years of output from one bloomery furnace (estimated at 1.5–3 kg per run and 30 runs a year, per the Chapter 18 novice line).

    So scavenging answers three questions first, and every salvage site must answer all three before work begins:

    Question Content Consequence of not answering
    ① What is in there Resource identification (metal, glass, cement, chemicals, instruments, books) A wasted trip, or a pile of unusable material hauled back
    ② What does taking it cost Structural risk, chemical risk, manpower and injury One person under a collapsed floor slab equals a loss to the whole settlement
    ③ Where does it go Whether the processing route is unlocked (without machine tools, rail is scrap iron) It occupies warehouse space, inflates the accounts, and ends up back in the furnace anyway

    Question ③ is the one most easily missed: the value of scavenging does not depend on what you pick up, it depends on whether what you pick up has a matching processing capability at this point in time. In the years before machine tools are unlocked, heaps of structural steel are nothing but a burden sitting in the warehouse.

    2. What Each Kind of Ruin Hides (Resource Map)¶

    Ruin type Main resources Key points for recovery
    Collapsed buildings and bridges Structural steel, rebar, sheet piling; copper (grounding electrodes, cable) Large pieces need prying and cutting; check the corrosion grade first — anything flaking in layers is remelt stock only
    Electrical installations Copper wire (motor windings, transformers, cable) The highest-grade copper source; transformer oil handled as hazardous material (see Section 3)
    Vehicle wrecks Steel sheet, aluminium parts, lead (batteries), copper (harnesses) Batteries are the main lead source; identify aluminium by its non-magnetic response and a fingernail scratch
    Machine shops and repair shops Finished tools, measuring instruments, machine tools, cutters, bearings The highest-priority rescue point — finished tools are two generations ahead of anything you can make
    Pipe networks Cast-iron pipe, copper valves, lead (old supply lines) Cast iron becomes furnace-front fittings; lead pipe is melted and cast, with food segregated throughout
    Railways (if any) Rail, spikes Rail runs about 0.6–0.8% carbon, the best edge-tool steel blank (empirical value, cross-checked by spark and fracture)

    One order of priority: finished tools and measuring instruments > motor copper wire > structural steel > rebar > castings > corroded scrap. The order is not set by feel — it is ranked by "how much usable capability the same hour of hauling buys back".

    3. Hazard Screening: The First Lesson of Scavenging Is "Don't Die"¶

    Hazard Identification Procedure
    Asbestos (roofing, insulation, old gaskets) White fibrous fracture on corrugated sheet, fibre-rope gaskets Wet working, double-bagged, sealed and buried deep; no entry to damaged dust-raising zones — a mask does not stop asbestos fibres, only wet working does
    Lead paint (old buildings) Brightly coloured old paint layers, especially window frames and railings No children or pregnant women; no dry scraping that raises dust — wet scraping only
    Chemical residues (laboratories, pharmacies, workshops) Unlabelled containers are all treated as the most toxic No smelling, no tasting; oxidizers and organics must never share a room (they can ignite or explode); haul the vessel out sealed, bury it deep and register the location
    Transformer and capacitor oil The oil in old electrical equipment No burning, no skin contact; bury the whole unit and register it (PCB-type oils go on the Chapter 49 poison ledger and are never used as fuel)
    Pressurized gas cylinders Partly charged cylinders, valves rusted shut Bleed the pressure before moving — a charged cylinder heated is a shell; a cylinder whose valve is rusted shut is buried whole, never dragged
    Collapsed structures Cantilevered slabs, broken beams, cracked columns, displaced bearings Look before you enter; shore before you enter; a stepladder is never used alone (falls in Chapter 44 are the leading killer in ruins)

    These six are not empty "be careful" talk; each one corresponds to a death or a lifelong injury that does not reverse. A salvage crew's rules should be harder than a smelting section's, because in a ruin there is no second chance.

    4. Tools and Minimum Equipment¶

    Crowbar (made from scrap rebar), sledgehammer, steel chisel, one set of wrenches (taken straight from the plant site), two block-and-tackle sets, wire rope, hand saw and grindstone, bolt cutters, wheelbarrow or sled. A gantry-type tripod plus a hand chain hoist is the only safe way to take down large pieces — with no lifting machinery, the right move is not to lift harder but to break the piece down first: divide it into sizes two people can carry, then move it. Protection per Chapter 15 (leather apron, gloves) and Chapter 44 (eye protection, CO-zone discipline).

    5. Dismantling: From Component to Stock Block¶

    1. Priority system: finished tools and measuring instruments > motor copper wire > structural steel > rebar > castings. Measuring instruments and edged tools are filed the moment they reach the store (the Chapter 22 system transplanted) — instruments in a ruin are "a ready-made library of standards"; one serviceable caliper is worth more than ten bars of rebar;
    2. Method of breaking down: bolts first — whatever can be turned is not cut: cutting one piece ruins one finished article; with no gas-cutting route, use "cold chisel plus sledgehammer" to drive along weld seams and rivet seams; a large beam gets two-point support before its middle section is cut, against the piece springing up and injuring someone;
    3. The hauling account: one person lifts ≤30 kg, two people ≤70 kg; anything over the limit must be broken down (the human-power account goes into Chapter 37);
    4. Taking copper: saw the motor stator open and take the windings out, recovering the strand whole. Burning insulation off for copper is forbidden — an open flame on a coil oxidizes the copper and loses weight, and the smoke is toxic.

    6. Scrap-Steel Grading and Storage (the Chapter 19 Four-Color Library Carried Over)¶

    Grade Identification Destination
    Finished-tool grade Lathe bits, files, measuring instruments, corrosion on the surface only Straight into the tool store, filed and registered
    Rail and spring-steel grade Rail section, leaf springs (high carbon, subdivided by spark test) Edge-tool blanks (the same cell as Grade B in Chapter 19)
    Structural and rebar grade Low-carbon structural steel Structural stock, carburizing blanks (Chapter 18 stock)
    Casting grade Grey iron (grey fracture, file slips) No forging; the melt-and-cast route (the Chapter 19 puddling condition)
    Corroded scrap grade Flaking in layers, fails to enter the store Remelt stock

    Identification tools: magnet, file check, fracture; the spark test comes into use once the grinding wheel is unlocked (same table, Chapter 19).

    7. Dedicated Recovery of Copper, Lead, and Aluminium¶

    • Copper: motor windings > cable > pipe fittings. Insulation comes off by hand stripping (fine wire) or by "squeeze-drawing" (heavy cable through a sizing die); no burning. Recovered copper feeds straight into the Chapter 31 wire drawing;
    • Lead: batteries (strip the grids, neutralize the electrolyte by the acid procedure) and old water pipe. Melt and cast in a dedicated crucible; lead work is physically segregated from food preparation; exposures go on the Chapter 49 register;
    • Aluminium: engine pistons, wheel hubs, housings (identify: non-magnetic, a fingernail scratches it, silver-white fine-grained fracture). Scrap aluminium is the only aluminium source before item C of Chapter 35 (electrolytic aluminium) is unlocked — registered and sealed, barred from daily use, consistent with the Chapter 35 line.

    8. Glass, Cement, and Non-Metals¶

    • Flat glass in sound large panes: cut (the Chapter 24 scratch method) for windows, mirror blanks, mask substrates (Chapter 35);
    • Cement: unopened bagged material is usable (once it has caked it has already hydrated; crush it for aggregate); concrete already set, once broken up, serves as recycled aggregate, proportioned down per Chapter 13;
    • Ceramic sanitary ware: pieces with the glaze intact serve as substitutes for pickling tanks (Chapter 26) — glaze resists acid better than bare earthenware, one of the rare "upgrades" to be found among waste.

    9. Knowledge Remains: The Most Precious Salvage¶

    Remains Priority What to rescue, and how
    Libraries, school book stores Highest Reference works, textbooks, medical books, farming manuals. Water-damaged books (soaked in muddy water) go at once to freezing or to shade-drying with the pages separated; never sun-dried (one day in the sun and they fuse into a brick)
    Pharmacies, hospitals High Leftover medicines registered under the Chapter 46 "consume only, never replenish" rule; instruments boiled and entered into the Chapter 44 instrument kit
    Seed stores, farm-supply shops High Germination sampled (10 seeds on wet paper) and then entered into the Chapter 10 seed system
    Telephone exchanges, broadcast stations Medium Wire, insulators, permanent magnets
    Filing cabinets and document cabinets Medium Good locks, thin steel sheet (a filing cabinet is quality cold-rolled sheet)

    Permanent magnets are the invisible jackpot: neodymium or ferrite magnets in a ruin skip straight past the hardest first step of the Chapter 31 "magnet bootstrap chain" (prospecting for natural lodestone). One working permanent-magnet speaker equals a whole prospecting line saved.

    10. The Salvage Ledger and Decline Management (continuing Chapter 38)¶

    • Every salvage site gets a file: resource list, risk list, quantity taken, quantity remaining, expected years to scrap (the corrosion rate in the open);
    • Priority = rate of decay: things that corrode in the open (thin sheet, wire) come out first, corrosion-resistant material (cast iron, concrete) later; pharmacy chemicals are the most urgent (shortest packaging life);
    • Scavenging is not unlimited: convert the local "steel reserve per head" by population and enter it on the resource map (the Chapter 16 backfill) — what it decides is not "how much can be picked up" but "how many years of scavenging are left, and by when at the latest the smelting line must be up". The scavenging period is a countdown, not a constant.

    11. Acceptance Criteria¶

    • L1: one ruin completes the three-question assessment, producing a resource list and a risk list, with no injuries;
    • L2: one accident-free dismantling completed (including a full record of hazard screening and disposal), producing ≥100 kg of graded metal into the store;
    • L3: several ruin ledgers running, the switch between the scavenging line and the smelting line decided on accounts (Section 10), and a second salvage crew brought out.

    One reminder: the biggest trap of the scavenging period is not failing to find things, it is picking up too much and using too little. The warehouse holds enough steel for fifty years and the smelting line is slow to come up — fifty years on, the steel has rusted through and people have forgotten how to smelt. The point of the countdown is this: it forces you to build the next stage's capability while the resources still suffice.
