    Ch 53 Island Edition

    Chapter Objectives¶

    Presents the complete restart execution plan for island conditions: island-type determination → five-stage roadmap → special-risk sections. This chapter introduces no new technologies; it only performs reordering and island adaptation; its core thesis is to recognize "limited local industry + external exchange" as a legitimate finished state.

    Capability Boundaries¶

    What can be done: three freshwater sources with lens-water abstraction-limit management; the fish-salt drying-and-storage system; catamarans and star navigation for inter-island voyaging; the shell-lime–seaweed-ash–volcanic-ash chemical line; salt-spray-resistant electrics and the island-group communication network; "limited local industry + external exchange" as an official route (each of the three island types sets its metallurgical depth by its resources). Explicitly cannot be done: local metallurgy on coral islands (strategic non-metallurgy); wave/tidal power (registered as a research target, no promises); eliminating typhoons (only early warning and contingency plans). External dependencies: stability of trading partners (chain-break contingency plans kept ready at all times); boat-timber plantations (a century-scale project — today's boat timber is a tree planted by our forebears).

    Environmental Assumptions (Assessment-Table Conclusions)¶

    Item Typical island answer Route consequence
    Fresh water rainwater / lens water / streams, variable in quantity the water ledger sets the population ceiling
    Food abundant marine protein the fish-and-salt system carries high weight
    Minerals volcanic islands (obsidian / sulfur / iron); coral islands (almost none) three types, three routes
    Alkali shell lime + seaweed ash (twin specialties) a unique chemical line
    Power wind, small temperature-difference flows, waves (long-term prospect) wind is primary
    Corrosion salt spray — the most severe corrosion environment in this book electrical anticorrosion tightened

    Minimum Prerequisites¶

    The Parts 1–6 technology library + island-type determination (volcanic / coral / continental island — the island version of the Chapter 2 geological survey).

    53.1 Stage One: Water, Food, and Transport¶

    • Three freshwater sources: rainwater harvesting (the roof-to-urn system, Chapter 11 large urns); the island freshwater lens (the fresh water floating atop seawater on coral islands — only small, slow withdrawals, over-abstraction causes salinization; dispersed well siting + registered daily withdrawal quotas); streams (volcanic islands often hold bulk fresh water — this directly unlocks the population ceiling);
    • Food: fish (fish spears / nets / traps) + shellfish (filter-feeding shellfish are forbidden during red tides — toxin accumulation; register sea-state precursors) + coconut starch; salted-dried fish (salt = sun-dried seawater; the Chapter 52 salt-drying method costs nothing on an island) — a protein storage system = lean-season insurance;
    • Transport is the lifeline: dugout canoe → outrigger canoe → catamaran (stability leaps upward, cargo becomes possible). Navigation: star navigation (Chapter 3 time + Chapter 25 angles — self-made rising/setting azimuth tables for stars; a preserved-knowledge version of the Austronesian millennium system) + coastal landmarks + monsoon windows (the sailing window = the monsoon calendar; register the no-sail periods);
    • Coastal work zone: slipway (launch–recovery ramp) + net-drying ground + salt pans — laid out along the tide lines (a combination of Chapter 2 hydrology + Chapter 13 building).

    [Card 53-01 Lens-Water Abstraction Limit] (eight fields)

    1. Input materials: the lens-water wells (well sites spread apart), each well's daily-withdrawal register and ten-day salinity records, colour-coded tags (marked in different colours from the rainwater jars).
    2. Tools required: a measuring vessel (Chapter 25 volumetric / water-weighing method), a dedicated tasting spoon (salinity, one person's own). Calibration source: withdrawal is registered by the volumetric method, not estimated in bucket counts.
    3. Operation sequence and control points: register each well's first-month daily withdrawal to fix its baseline → draw water within the ceiling (sites spread apart, small amounts drawn slowly) → taste salinity every ten days into the ledger → two consecutive salty ten-day periods close the well and the backup well opens → colour-code against the rainwater jars and keep a separate account for each source. Control points: the ceiling is per well, not per island (concentrating on one well = local over-abstraction); two consecutive salty periods means stop at once, do not wait for a third (salinization is irreversible).
    4. Experiential parameters and calibration method: per-well daily ceiling = the registered baseline; water quality tasted every ten days, every lens well on the books; two consecutive salty periods close the well (master-table red line); lens water may only be drawn in small amounts and slowly — over-abstraction means salinization, and a salinized freshwater lens is a death sentence for the island.
    5. In-process observation items: per well record daily withdrawal / ten-day salinity (taste judgment) / dates of closure and reopening / the well's converted days of supply.
    6. Accept / rework / scrap criteria: accept = no well exceeding its baseline all year and a steady salinity curve; rework = a single salty ten-day period (cut withdrawal at once, tighten tasting, see whether it freshens); scrap = salinity in two consecutive periods (that well is closed, irreversibly; open the backup and re-set the baseline).
    7. Common failures and troubleshooting order: ① well water turning salty period by period → over-abstraction (withdrawal above baseline, or sites too close together); ② the whole island salty at once → several wells over-drawn together (re-arrange sites and the water ledger); ③ withdrawal pushed up in the dry season → the rationing regime was not accounted per well; ④ disagreement over taste judgment → dedicated spoon and taster, compared every ten days; when in doubt treat it as salty.
    8. L1 / L2 / L3 evidence: L1 = one year of per-well registers; L2 = a steady salinity curve plus at least one voluntary closure and reopening; L3 = a second person independently making one closure / reopening decision per this card with the same conclusion.

    53.2 Stage Two: Woodwork, Fiber, and Marine Materials¶

    • Hull timber (straight-grained lightweight wood, selectively bred — Chapter 7 material selection + the long horizon of Chapter 10 plantations: today's boat timber is a tree planted by our forebears; boat-timber plantations are listed as a century-scale project). Canvas and rope (coconut fiber / sisal — the marine-fiber version of Chapter 15, salt-resistant and rot-resistant);
    • Obsidian (a volcanic-island birthright): natural glass-grade stone-tool material (Chapter 5 — its sharpness crowns all stone tools); edge tools and scrapers go straight to top grade. But it is brittle (forbidden for impact tools) and non-renewable (registered sites, quota-limited extraction);
    • Anticorrosion treatment: anti-fouling for hull bottoms (burned dried-oyster ash mixed into paint / multiple coats of tung oil); monthly inspection of ironware for salt-spray corrosion (a strengthened version of the Chapter 50 system — island ironware rusts at a "respiration rate"; wherever wood / stone / ceramics can substitute, iron is not used).

    [Card 53-02 Salt-Spray Anticorrosion Inspection] (eight fields)

    1. Input materials: the three-tier exposure-surface checklist (tier one = hull hardware; tier two = outdoor structures; tier three = indoors), fresh water (to wash salt frost), anticorrosion grease and tung oil, corrosion coupons (test pieces).
    2. Tools required: brushes, grease gun, soft cloth, coupons and a monthly scale (Chapter 3). Calibration source: corrosion rate is logged by monthly coupon weighing, not judged by eye.
    3. Operation sequence and control points: weekly rounds over the three-tier checklist (tier-one parts inspected after every use) → on white frost: fresh water wash → dry → oil → contacts grease-sealed (monthly + after rain) → coupons weighed monthly to log the rate → review the graded material choice (where wood / stone / ceramic can replace iron, use them; hull bottoms get dried-oyster lime paint or multiple coats of tung oil). Control points: patrol immediately after rain; wash before oiling (oiling without washing seals the salt under the paint).
    4. Experiential parameters and calibration method: weekly rounds + rounds immediately after rain; tier-one parts inspected after each use; electrical contacts monthly + after rain; coupons weighed monthly; island iron rusts at a "respiration rate"; insulators get extra skirts + silicone sealing coat + sealed distribution boards.
    5. In-process observation items: per round record date / tier patrolled / location of white frost / treatment (wash–dry–oil) / contact condition / monthly coupon weight / parts replaced.
    6. Accept / rework / scrap criteria: accept = weekly and post-rain rounds with no gaps, no green contacts, steady coupon weight loss; rework = white frost recurring (tighten to a round after every voyage and recoat); scrap = green, powdery contacts or corroded terminals (replace, switching to non-iron parts or sealing).
    7. Common failures and troubleshooting order: ① contacts failing in batches → no post-rain round (make it up + grease-seal + seal the board); ② iron life halved → the three-tier material choice was not followed (switch to substitutes, re-baseline the coupons); ③ blistering under the paint → oil applied before washing (salt sealed under the paint); ④ pitting on hull hardware → tier-one parts not inspected after use.
    8. L1 / L2 / L3 evidence: L1 = one season of weekly-round records + monthly coupon weights; L2 = a full year with no batch contact failures and a coupon-rate curve; L3 = a second person independently running one round per this card with the same white-frost judgment.

    53.3 Stage Three: Pottery, Lime, and Glass¶

    • Pottery: clay or none (coral islands usually lack soil — sea-shipped clay is listed as a regular trade item, or transition with lime-mortar wooden vessels); on volcanic islands, volcanic ash + lime give a natural cement formula (pozzolanic cement, Chapter 13) — the mainstay material for island construction and slipways;
    • Shell lime (the mother of island chemistry): kiln-burning of shells (the Chapter 13 lime process) — one kiln, two products: lime (construction / mortar) and lye (after causticizing: soap, glass, and the whole Chapter 23 line);
    • Seaweed ash: burn seaweed to get ash — rich in iodine and alkali (iodine is a strategic material for wound disinfection and thyroid health; registered extraction: ash-water leaching with graded crystallization). Soda supplies the glass line;
    • Glass: the coral island's white sand + seaweed soda = a complete glass batch — glassware and lenses (Chapters 14 and 24) actually proceed smoothly on islands (coral islands are an "optically rich zone" — a registered paradox).

    53.4 Stage Four: Metallurgy and Machinery¶

    • Three routes by island type:
    • Volcanic island: basalt (building material / millstones) + magnetite sand (black sand = an iron source; the island form of the Chapter 16 heavy-mineral-concentrate method — smelting sand-iron has heavy fuel costs, so scale is decided by the fuel ledger) + sulfur (crater vents, Chapter 23);
    • Continental island: conventional ore deposits (follow the main route of Parts 1–6);
    • Coral island: essentially no minerals — local metal = the recycling loop (Chapter 17) + driftwood iron fittings + trading for metal (strategic non-metallurgy);
    • Guano (where rookeries exist): phosphorus-nitrogen fertilizer (the Chapter 10 agriculture leap) and raw material for phosphate dopants (Chapter 35) — a remote island may in fact be one of the environments closest to semiconductors in this whole book (the odd combination of guano + shell-lime alkali + seaweed iodine + white-sand glass + wind-generated electricity); registered as a "long-line opportunity";
    • Machinery: ship-repair capability outranks machine building — the woodworking and rigging system for masts / rudders / pumps is the body of the seafaring industry.

    53.5 Stage Five: Electricity and Electronics¶

    • Power: wind as the mainstay (sea winds are steady) + small hydropower (volcanic-island streams); waves and tides (registered as a research target, no promises);
    • Salt-spray-resistant electrics: skirting added to porcelain insulators + silicone-oil sealing coats + sealed distribution boards (the island version of the Chapter 32 three-piece set). The grounding-electrode trap on islands: coral sand conducts poorly — backfill a saltwater pit + multiple electrodes in parallel (measure the grounding resistance; do not close the switch until it meets spec);
    • Electronics and communication (the greatest value of the island edition): telegraph / radio stations (Chapters 32–33) link the island group — fishery warnings, rescue calls, trade inquiries. Navigation lights (harbor wind lamps + focused beams) — a public good of a seafaring society;
    • Packaging: the Chapter 36 harsh-environment grade + salt-spray accelerated-aging qualification (the first island entry in the failure atlas = electrochemical migration).

    53.6 Special Sections of the Island Edition¶

    1. Seawater cannot directly replace fresh water (all process ledgers are still kept in fresh water); a seawater-use register (cooling / salt drying / rinsing — wherever seawater is used, anticorrosion design is mandatory);
    2. The general corrosion strategy: grade the metal exposure surfaces (Tier-1 exposure = hull hardware, use copper / tin fittings; Tier-2 = outdoor construction; Tier-3 = indoors) — graded material selection replaces "iron everywhere";
    3. The economic trade-off between sea freight and local manufacture: goods whose value per unit weight is below the freight cost (stone, wood, coarse ceramics) are made locally; high-value low-weight goods (salt, iodine, glassware, dried fish, guano fertilizer) are exchanged for metal, fuel, and seed — the trade list is the technology roadmap (the nautical version of Chapter 29 full-cost accounting);
    4. The ship–harbor–rope-and-net system is the island's "first industry": repairing one ship is as comprehensive as a joint examination in metallurgy + woodworking + textiles + chemistry — ship-repair capability is the acceptance touchstone of island industry;
    5. The typhoon/storm protocol: warning (register cloud forms and swell precursors) → boats hauled onto the slipway → supplies stored → personnel sheltered (in bone-built structures); after a disaster, the "communication network + repair fleet" is the first recovery sequence.

    [Card 53-03 Storm Contingency] (eight fields)

    1. Input materials: the sky-watch post's daily register (swell / cloud forms / hand-feel barometric grade), boats awaiting haul-out, supplies awaiting storage (including the three-tier water storage), the storm-shelter bone-frame building, the radio set.
    2. Tools required: the register, the slipway and keel blocks (Card 53-08), mooring lines, the storage checklist, a hot-standby radio set. Calibration source: precursors are read against the daily register, not by last-minute feel.
    3. Operation sequence and control points: register the precursors daily (swell direction shift / cloud forms / sudden sky-glow) → grade and issue the warning → boats hauled out and secured → supplies stored (three-tier water storage full, sail-and-rigging spares doubled) → personnel sheltered (bone-frame building) → post-disaster recovery order: communications → ship repair → power → production. Control points: the recovery order must never be inverted (without communications first, the repair crews cannot be dispatched); once warned, boats come ashore regardless of how the sky looks.
    4. Experiential parameters and calibration method: three tiers of precursors; the sky-watch post registers 15 min daily; recovery order communications → ship repair → power → production (master-table iron rule); before the typhoon season rigging spares doubled and three-tier water storage full; slipway gradient re-measured once before and after each typhoon.
    5. In-process observation items: daily three precursors / warning level and issue time / number of boats hauled out and how secured / items stored / number of people sheltered / damage and the time of each recovery step.
    6. Accept / rework / scrap criteria: accept = warning issued on time, all boats ashore and secured, recovery run in order; rework = a late warning or a missed boat (sky-watch post held to account + whole-crew drill); scrap = an inverted recovery order causing second-round losses (register and review, retrain).
    7. Common failures and troubleshooting order: ① typhoon losses concentrated → late warning or boats not hauled out; ② slow recovery → communications not restored first (repair crews cannot be dispatched); ③ boats damaged on the slipway → hauled out but not blocked up and secured; ④ water run out during the storm → three-tier storage not full before the season.
    8. L1 / L2 / L3 evidence: L1 = one typhoon season of daily registers + one drill record; L2 = an actual storm run through in order with losses contained; L3 = a second person independently running one drill with the same sequence and checklist.

    53.7 Failure: Which Line Breaks First on an Island (Second of the Five Review Angles)¶

    No. Failure line Precursor Consequence chain
    ① Salt-spray corrosion (the strongest in this book) white salt frost creeping onto hardware, contacts turning green, wire terminals powdering ship lost → communications cut → electrical accidents — every island failure is layered on top of this one
    ② Lens-water over-abstraction salinization well water tasting saltier with each ten-day period (registration system) irreversible salinization of the freshwater lens = a death sentence for the island
    ③ Storm damage (typhoon / cyclone) swell direction shifts, cloud forms, the barometric hand-feel notch (bird-behavior and swell-precursor registration) fleet wiped out, houses destroyed, sailing halted for several ten-day periods
    ④ Boat-timber generational gap (the shipbuilding forest not renewed) harvestable boat-timber diameters shrinking year by year no boats = trade and fishing both severed — boat timber is a century-scale project; its failure arrives a generation later
    ⑤ Red tide and fish poisoning sea-surface discoloration, noctiluca glow, dead fish drifting ashore mass poisoning (the Section 10.1 red line)
    ⑥ External-dependency chain break (trading partner defaults / route severed) anomalies in the quarterly trade-list carryover metal / seed supply cut — the unique weak point of a trade-based economy

    53.11 Catamaran Construction Card (Card 53-04)¶

    The catamaran is the island's "heavy truck" — two hulls clamping a platform, with stability and payload both multiplied:

    • Material selection: hull timber is straight-grained lightweight wood (the weigh test: heft equal-volume blocks and take the lighter — light wood draws less water and carries more); the keel and the crossbeams use heavy hardwood (load-bearing parts do not mind weight);
    • Hull spacing: 1.5–2 times the width of one hull (too narrow and the hulls strike each other in waves; too wide and the crossbeams take heavy bending — an empirical starting point, to be calibrated against the wave conditions);
    • Crossbeams: at least 3 spanning both hulls (bow / midship / stern), with the triple connection of mortise-and-tenon + lashing + wooden pins (the island version of the Chapter 7/9 system). The beam-to-hull joints are where all stress concentrates — visually inspect the three beam mortises before and after every voyage (replace at the first hairline crack; a broken beam at sea = a capsize);
    • Platform: planks laid over the beams form the cargo deck; the cargo deck must not rise above the gunwales of the two hulls (center-of-gravity discipline);
    • Launch-test protocol: three stages for the maiden voyage — calm-water empty-load test → nearshore test at 1/3 load → inter-island test fully loaded; each stage's voyage log is archived (roll angle, water-ingress points, speed);
    • Load line: the fully loaded waterline is scribed in paint on both hulls (the maritime version of the Section 15.7 load-calibration idea — no sailing past the overload line, non-negotiable).

    [Card 53-04 Catamaran Construction] (eight fields)

    1. Input materials: straight-grained lightweight wood (hulls; the weigh test: heft equal-volume blocks and take the lighter), heavy hardwood (keel and crossbeams), beam pins and lashing cord, deck planking.
    2. Tools required: woodworking tools (Chapter 7), equal-volume blocks and a scale (for the weigh test), a scribing line for the load line in paint. Calibration source: hull spacing starts from the empirical figure, then is calibrated against the wave conditions.
    3. Operation sequence and control points: select materials (light wood for hulls / heavy hardwood for load-bearing parts) → set the hull spacing (1.5–2 times the width of one hull, an empirical starting point) → fit at least 3 through-crossbeams (bow / midship / stern, with the triple connection of mortise-and-tenon + lashing + wooden pins) → lay the platform (not above the gunwales) → scribe the load line in paint → three-stage maiden-voyage test. Control points: the three beam mortises are inspected before and after every voyage (replace at the first hairline crack; a broken beam at sea = a capsize); no sailing past the overload line, non-negotiable.
    4. Experiential parameters and calibration method: hull spacing 1.5–2 times one hull's width (empirical starting point, calibrated against wave conditions); at least 3 through-crossbeams; the three-stage maiden voyage = calm water empty → nearshore at 1/3 load → inter-island fully loaded, each stage logging roll angle / water-ingress points / speed; the load line scribed in paint on both hulls.
    5. In-process observation items: per voyage record the three beam-mortise inspections / roll angle / water-ingress points / speed / load and where it sits against the load line.
    6. Accept / rework / scrap criteria: accept = all three maiden-voyage stages passed, no cracked mortises, platform not above the gunwales; rework = hulls striking each other (spacing too narrow) or heavy beam bending (too wide) — reset the spacing and refit the beams; scrap = a cracked beam mortise (replace the beam; a boat that has broken a beam may not carry cargo again).
    7. Common failures and troubleshooting order: ① hulls striking each other in waves → spacing too narrow; ② crossbeams bending and cracking → spacing too wide or overloaded; ③ platform shipping water → platform above the gunwales or the load line exceeded; ④ capsizing on the maiden voyage → one of the triple connections was skipped (mortise, lashing, or pin).
    8. L1 / L2 / L3 evidence: L1 = one catamaran's three-stage maiden-voyage log; L2 = a full season of scheduled inter-island runs with no gap in mortise inspections; L3 = a second person independently building one per this card and passing the three-stage test.

    53.12 Star-Navigation Azimuth Table Card (Card 53-05)¶

    Inter-island voyaging in the age before the compass (the magnetic needle comes only after Chapter 30) relies on the stars — a local reconstruction of the Austronesian millennium system:

    • Selecting the reference-star set: choose 8–12 bright stars of stable brightness and easily recognized position (selected by local night-sky observation; register the star names by imagery — "Paddle Star", "Shark Star", and the like);
    • Azimuth calibration: plant two stakes ashore and sight along the line between them (with the Section 3.2.1 true-north line as the reference); night after night, record the azimuth on the horizon of each reference star's rising point and setting point (using the stake line as the reference, record "so many fists left, so many fists right") — each star's pair of azimuths = one "star path";
    • Rising intervals: record the time differences between the stars' risings (water clock / beat counting, Chapter 3) — the stars rise in sequence, so counting the stars as they pass is counting the bearings as you go;
    • Making the table: draw each star's star path into an azimuth chart (a paddle-blade radial diagram: the boat at the center, radial lines for each star's rising/setting azimuth, each annotated with the island direction it corresponds to) — going to a given island = relaying along the several star paths that point toward it;
    • Verification protocol: for a new route, first follow an experienced boat three times while checking against the star table; any case of "the star table says we should have arrived but the island is not there" is always first attributed to wind drift (currents and wind push the track off course — record a "wind correction angle", accumulated line by line);
    • No-sail red line: no putting to sea under overcast skies with neither stars nor shore marks — windows already banned on the monsoon calendar (53.1) are enforced doubly.

    53.13 Shell-Lime Kiln and Seaweed-Ash Iodine Extraction (Card 53-06)¶

    The shell-lime kiln (one kiln, two products): - Charging the kiln: shells / coral blocks and firewood loaded in alternating layers (isomorphic to the Chapter 13 lime kiln); shell pieces are thin, so burn-through takes less time than limestone (about 70 percent, empirical value); - The two products: half of the kiln-run shell lime goes to the slaking line (the building-mortar line); half goes to the causticizing line — boil shell lime together with ash lye → caustic lye (the Chapter 23 saponification alkali; the local alkali source for the island soap industry); - Criterion: vigorous slaking and a high milk-of-lime yield mean a proper burn (the Chapter 13 three-state judgment table applies).

    Seaweed-ash iodine and alkali extraction: - Raw material: seaweed piled up after storms (a free bulk supply), sun-dried and burned (the ash-to-seaweed ratio is about 20:1, empirical value); - The alkali line: leach the ash in water → evaporate and crystallize to get soda (sodium carbonate — the local alkali for the Chapter 14 glass line; the other half of the puzzle of why coral islands are glass-rich); - The iodine line: further concentrate the leached mother liquor; iodine enriches in the dark crude crystals that precipitate last (registration-grade process: confirm the presence of iodine with a starch test paper turning blue-black — quantitative purification is listed as long-term; for now, "crude iodine crystals + alcohol tincture" serve wound disinfection (within the Chapter 46 boundary) and the thyroid-health food line (Section 48.6)); - Red line: concentrated iodine solution is toxic; never take the undiluted liquid internally. Burning seaweed gives off irritating smoke; work upwind.

    53.14 The Island-Group Communication Network (the greatest value of the island edition)¶

    • Network structure: a star network with the main island as the hub — one telegraph station per island (Chapter 32), the main island on double shifts (Chapter 37 scheduling), outlying island stations keeping watch according to the sailing schedule;
    • Traffic discipline: weather reports (each island broadcasts wind and sea state every morning) carry top priority — fishery warnings save lives directly; next, rescue calls (dedicated code words); trade inquiries are lowest;
    • Relay and repeater: where inter-island line-of-sight falls short, unmanned relay islands (carrying only antennas and automatic repeaters — Chapter 33 relay components) bridge the gap;
    • Salt-spray protection: sealed stations + weekly contact inspections (Card 53-02) + hot-standby spare sets (a failed set is hot-swapped without dropping the network);
    • Drills: quarterly network-wide broadcast drills (stations take turns transmitting — the communication network is the first post-disaster recovery sequence; untrained in peacetime, it will be mute in wartime).

    53.15 The Fish Salting-and-Drying Process (the protein storage system)¶

    • Splitting and sun-drying: split large fish into fillets and remove the skull (the skull is fat-rich and turns rancid first); thin slices with light salting (salt at 10–15 percent by weight, empirical value), pressed under stones for half a day and drained, then onto the drying racks (the Chapter 10 four dryness criteria apply);
    • Whole-fish drying: small fish salted whole for a full day and night, then strung on cord and hung to shade-dry in a ventilated place (shade-drying beats direct sun — surface case-hardening that locks in moisture is the main cause of re-softening and rot);
    • Storage criterion: a dried fish that snaps with a "crack" when bent passes; one that bends without breaking = moisture still high, return it for further drying — store on high racks + smoke to prevent maggots (interlocked with the Chapter 45 vector-control line);
    • Red-tide red line restated: for fish and shellfish caught during any sea-discoloration period, take muscle only and discard the viscera, and halve the first meal (the Section 10.1 rule) — filter-feeding shellfish are banned as an entire class;
    • The ledger: dried-fish output is the main body of the rainy-season protein reserve (the protein column of the Chapter 37 ration table); the annual storage line = island population × daily protein need × rainy-season days × 1.3 redundancy.

    53.16 The Fishing Line in Depth: Three Fishing Methods, the Seasonal Fishing Calendar, and the Catch-Handling Line (Card 53-07)¶

    Section 53.1 gave only the names of the gear; here "how many fish come out per day, which fish come out in which season, and which line the catch follows once ashore" is filled in as a schedulable system.

    Division of labor among the three fishing methods (choose by water depth and conditions, not personal preference):

    • Nets (surface to mid-water, the mainstay for bulk catch): mesh size is set by the target fish's dimensions — the principle is "release the small, catch the large": mesh side length ≈ half the body depth of the target fish (empirical starting point); small fish pass through the mesh and the fishery stays sustainable. Field calibration: three test nets of different mesh sizes are set side by side on the same beach for one tide; compare the catch length distributions and the fish-damage rate (the proportion of small fish injured by gilling) — a high damage rate means the mesh is too large; drop one size and re-verify. Net tying ◎: the mesh must be even, and this rests entirely on hand-feel — practice method: tie twenty 10 cm × 10 cm test pieces from scrap fiber; overlay the pieces mesh against mesh; any piece whose mesh holes are offset by more than one-third is untied and re-tied. Misjudgment compensation: check mesh by mesh with a standard mesh gauge (a board carrying two bamboo pegs spaced at the mesh side length); a wrong mesh is visible at a glance;
    • Traps (bottom-dwelling crabs, shrimp, burrowing fish): fit the trap mouth with an inward-barb funnel — a funnel-shaped ring of points shaved from bamboo strips or stiff twigs; fish and crabs squeeze in through the cone mouth and are blocked by the sharp tips when they try to turn back; the funnel cone length ≈ one-third of the trap diameter (empirical value). Calibration: sink baited traps and empty traps in equal numbers at the same spot; fish entering the empty traps means the barbs have failed or the mouth is too large — repair the funnel and re-verify. Retrieval cycle: haul once per day-and-night — beyond two days, dead fish in the trap attract predators, ruining the trap and drawing sharks;
    • Spearing (visible fish in the shallows; selective take that spares small fish): shallow water + dawn or dusk — both conditions are indispensable: at dawn and dusk the light is weak, surface glare small, fish come onto the flats to feed, and shadows are sharp-edged. ◎ Aiming rule "spear low, not high": refraction lifts the fish's image; the true fish is below the image, so aim the spear tip at the lower edge of the shadow and then about one fist lower (empirical value, at depths of 30–50 cm). Practice method: plant 30 sticks on the flats as "shadow targets"; each morning spear at the targets 20 times and record hits; only after five consecutive days above 15/20 may one enter the water to spear live fish. Misjudgment compensation: read the depth with a graduated stake touching bottom; if the depth exceeds one arm span (spear + arm length), abandon the spear and switch to nets.

    The seasonal fishing calendar (an annual cycle of fish runs, wind seasons, and closed seasons) — fishing's "agricultural calendar":

    • Monthly registration items (kept by the sky-watch post on the side, fixed format): catch of the main species (converted to trap hauls / net hauls); water color and hand-feel water temperature (red-tide precursors, 53.7 ⑤); migratory-bird passage and nearshore swell direction (markers of wind-season turns); the proportion of gravid females (above 30 percent registers that species as a closure candidate);
    • The calendar-making protocol: only three consecutive years of data make a "calendar" — year one is a running log; year two makes the first cross-year comparison to find repeating patterns (does a given fish run arrive in the same ten-day period each year); year three pre-schedules the fishing seasons on the year-two pattern and back-verifies the hit rate; only above 70 percent may it be issued as the "calendar" to guide next year's scheduling — boat rotations, drying-rack capacity, and salt-pan start dates are all scheduled by the calendar;
    • Closed-fishing windows: two fixed kinds on the calendar — the spawning closure (one to two ten-day periods of the egg-carrying phase, enforced per the calendar) and the red-tide emergency closure (temporary; lifted after sea conditions recover and three consecutive tides of test fishing pass inspection). The catch drop during closures is underwritten by dried-fish stores (the 53.15 annual storage line) — calendar, storage, and salt are one chain; break one link and the whole chain is downgraded.

    The catch-handling line (sorting the moment the catch lands — three routes: salt / dry / fresh):

    • Discipline: sorting is completed within one hour of landing (under the tropical sun fresh fish begins to turn within two shichen (≈4 hours) — the sorting line is set up right on the beach; the catch is not carried into the village to be sorted);
    • Criteria for the three routes: the same-day consumption share (per the ration table's head count) → fresh; thin-bodied, firm-fleshed fish → split and sun-dried on the drying line (53.15); thick-bodied, evenly fatty fish → the salting line (salt at 10–15 percent, same parameters as 53.15) — salting and drying can be chained (salt first, then semi-dry; the long-storage champion);
    • Beach sorting table: a stone or plank table + a sunshade; the salt pans and drying racks sit within thirty paces — the fish does not move, the people do: the three workstations of sorting, salting, and racking are laid out in one line.

    53.17 Shipyard Layout: Slipway, Dry Dock, Sail-and-Rigging Store, Timber Yard (Card 53-08)¶

    Ship-repair capability is the acceptance touchstone of island industry (53.6), and a touchstone needs a workshop with a fixed layout — scattered temporary beach repair points cannot produce "all-round" capability:

    • Slipway (the launch–recovery ramp): two parallel hardwood rails (straight-grained, length ≈ 1.5 times the boat length), gauge slightly wider than the boat's beam (empirical starting point: the boat's centerline aligned to the rail centerline, each rail carrying half the boat's weight). Ramp slope 1:8 to 1:12 (empirical range); calibration: grease the rails and push the empty boat — "moves when pushed, stops when released" is the pass standard (too shallow and it cannot be pushed; too steep and letting go lets the boat run away and hurt someone; both cases are rework). The lower end of the ramp extends one boat-length past the lowest tide line — so launching and recovery work even at ebb tide, doubling the tidal work window. Keel blocks are placed along the line, spaced 1.5–2 m (empirical value; calibration: with a fully loaded boat on the ways for three days, drive and re-tighten the wedges block by block once — a spacing is qualified if it still takes half a wedge, and sunken spots are re-blocked at closer spacing); a layer of sacking on the block tops keeps the hull planks from bruising;
    • Dry dock (for overhauls): dig a dock pit ashore + a dock gate + drainage — open the gate at ebb tide and it drains itself; the remaining water is bailed out by hand. Build a rain shelter over the dock mouth (rainwater pooling in the dock = bailing in vain). Line the dock walls and floor with pozzolanic cement (53.3). Coral islands offer no digging conditions: substitute "haul up onto the high beach + block repair under a shelter" for the dry dock, registered as a legitimate substitute (the 53.9 substitution-matrix logic); the performance loss = no dock repair in strong-wind weather;
    • Sail-and-rigging store (sails / rope / anchors on separate racks): three racks kept apart — the sail rack (canvas hung on poles, spread out for storage; folded and stacked it will certainly mildew); the rope rack (rope coiled at a coil diameter ≥ 20 times the rope diameter, hung to prevent kinks, the rack off the floor against damp); the anchor rack (anchors and chain on separate racks, chain hung and oiled). Anticorrosion inside the store follows the Card 53-02 weekly rounds. The sail-and-rigging store doubles as the repair fleet's spare-parts store — one spare sail, one spare set of rigging, one spare set of rudder and oars, all doubled before typhoon season (interlocked with Card 53-03);
    • Timber yard (the boat-timber shade-drying area): enforce the Chapter 7 seasoned-timber system — off-the-ground drying racks, sunshade, ventilation channels facing the prevailing wind, the four dryness criteria as usual. The yard is zoned by stock size: the keel zone (long stock on flat racks), the planking zone (thin stock on upright racks), the mast zone (whole sticks hung to prevent bowing). Newly felled boat timber may go onto the slipway only after the full seasoning period — a wet-wood boat launched will leak and warp within a year; this is a hard gate, and no "urgent need, launch early" is permitted.

    53.18 The Rainwater-Harvesting Network and the Water Ledger: Storage Balancing, Three-Tier Storage, and Dry-Season Rationing¶

    Section 53.1 listed rainwater as one of the three freshwater sources; here it is expanded into a ledger — the environmental-assumptions table states plainly that "the water ledger sets the population ceiling"; an unclear ledger is a slow death sentence:

    • Roof catchment area–storage capacity balancing worked example: the ledger formula is storage capacity ≥ dry-season days × total daily demand ÷ collection efficiency. Example: dry season 60 days, island population 20, per-person daily need 4 L (3 for drinking + 1 for minimum cooking, the stricter Chapter 10 standard), collection efficiency 0.7 (roof runoff losses: dust fouling, splash loss, first-flush diversion) — required capacity = 60 × 20 × 4 ÷ 0.7 ≈ 6860 L ≈ 7000 L (counting the Chapter 11 large urn at 200 L each, about 35 urns). Order-of-magnitude sanity check: 35 large urns for a 20-person village — the footprint and the urn-making labor are both considerable; household urns alone are unrealistic, so they must be merged into the communal cistern and the hidden storage (below), with household urns handling only turnover. The other side of the supply ledger: collected volume = roof area × rainfall per event × efficiency — a 40 m² roof catching one 25 mm rain (1 mm of rain on 1 m² = 1 L) × 0.7 = 700 L, enough for 175 person-days. When the two ledgers do not match: a shortfall within 20 percent is bridged by rationing (below); beyond 20 percent the roof or the storage must be expanded — no gambling that "this year's rains will be generous";
    • Three-tier water storage: first, household ceramic-urn storage (Chapter 11 large urns; household turnover; must be lidded — an open water urn is a mosquito breeding site, see the failure-modes table). Second, the communal cistern (a stone-built pool lined with pozzolanic cement, 53.3; holds the big rains of the wet season; covered with a lid plate + a communal long-handled dipper). Third, hidden underground storage (clusters of ceramic urns buried in the sand layer — protection against typhoon-smashed urns and against squandering; locations are registered in the elders' water ledger, not public to all). Full storage at all three tiers before typhoon season is the water item of the storm contingency plan's (Card 53-03) "supplies stored" clause;
    • Tiered water-quality management: the four tiers of drinking / cooking / washing / livestock are kept separate in vessel and in source — the drinking urn has its dedicated vessel and dipper (the island advance version of the Chapter 45 hygiene line; the separation discipline is established here first); lens-water wells and rainwater urns carry color-coded marks (the lens-water daily abstraction ceiling is governed separately by Card 53-01; the two sources keep separate ledgers); communal-cistern water is for washing and construction only — cistern water must be boiled or passed through solar distillation before drinking (interlocked with the distillation item of the 53.9 substitution matrix);
    • The dry-season rationing system (isomorphic to the Chapter 50 dry-season discipline): the criterion for entering "rationing state" = days of support computed from storage < estimated remaining dry-season days × 1.2 (20 percent redundancy). Rationing has three tiers — normal / conservation / survival — with per-person daily need stepping 4 → 3 → 2 L. Rationing is executed by the water ledger, and the sky-watch post publishes the three-tier water levels daily; drawing beyond the ration carries the same penalties as Chapter 50. The lifting criterion = two effective rains (each event ≥ 20 mm) entered in the ledger — one rain does not lift it, guarding against deceptive rains.

    Acceptance Criteria¶

    • L1: at least two of the three freshwater sources registered; the fish-and-salt seasonal storage at target; the first outrigger canoe making a round trip in coastal waters; the six failure lines posted on the wall.
    • L2: the shell-lime–seaweed-ash–volcanic-ash chemical line at full production; catamarans on fixed rotations; salt-resistant electrics through a rainy season; the abstraction-limit card executed for one year (a stable salinity curve).
    • L3: the island-group communication network in operation; all-round ship repair; trade books balancing at year end; two typhoon seasons with zero major breaches — the island joins the regional civilization network as a "node".

    Safety Boundaries¶

    • Shellfish are forbidden during red tides; put to sea in compliance with the monsoon calendar and the load line;
    • Volcanic-island sulfur mining (toxic gases) requires two-person teams + a wind-direction lookout; lens-water wells must not be over-abstracted;
    • After a typhoon warning, boats must be hauled ashore; the post-disaster recovery order of communications first, then power, then production must never be inverted.
