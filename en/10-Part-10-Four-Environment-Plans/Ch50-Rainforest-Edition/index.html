    Chapter 50: Rainforest Civilization Rebuild Edition¶

    Chapter Objectives¶

    After reading this chapter, the settlement should produce five documents and run them on the wall:

    Deliverable Acceptance criterion
    Rainforest environment assessment conclusion Card 50-01 with measured values for all ten dimensions (water / fuel / clay / metal / silicon / wood / power / transport / maintenance / labor), linked to the master outline's Assessment Form G04
    Fails-first order table (local version) Local ordering of the seven failure lines of Section 50.3, each line's "precursors" written onto the duty board
    Annual rhythm table Dry season (build, burn, store) and rainy season (make, repair, heal) scheduled month by month (Section 50.7)
    Rainforest maintenance cycle table Every item of the Section 50.4 comparison table implemented, with ten-day inspection signatures as proof
    Rainforest substitution matrix ≥5 entries registered in the Chapter 40 four-column format, each trial-produced

    Acceptance for this chapter is not "what has been built" but whether the rainy-season assessment has been passed: only when the four ledgers — mold, fever, leakage, corrosion — all reach target at the end of the rainy season has this chapter been executed.

    Capability Boundaries¶

    Can do:

    • Give the sequencing differences, the consolidated failure table, and the frequency-multiplied maintenance cycles for the Part 1–6 technology library under rainforest conditions (Sections 50.2–50.4).
    • Build four-column substitution matrices for the scarce resources, with boundaries stated honestly (Section 50.5, Chapter 40 format).
    • Place the engineering defense lines against malaria and waterborne disease ahead of all industry, and give the trigger thresholds of the default fever protocol (Section 50.6).

    Cannot do:

    • Cannot change the climate. Dry-season window length is a hard constraint; in a rainy year the window shortens or even vanishes, so the rhythm table must carry a "rainy-year contingency column."
    • Cannot conjure resources from nothing. With no salt springs and no refractory clay outcrops inside the territory, the corresponding matrix cells can only say "no route"; padding them with "theoretically obtainable through distant trade" is not allowed (Iron Law 5 of Chapter 40).
    • Cannot eradicate mold, malaria, or corrosion; can only stretch failure cycles beyond the maintenance cycle — the definition of every institution in this chapter is one sentence: make "decays slowly" outrun "repairs diligently."
    • Does not re-teach processes. Each technology point cites only a chapter number plus one sentence of rainforest difference; building while skipping that chapter's process card counts as an incomplete work step.

    External dependencies (counted into List C): the master outline's Assessment Form G04 and Ledger Regulation No. 02; the Chapter 40 register, contingency box, and post-flood drying procedure; the medical line's fever handling and mosquito-control rules (interface in Section 50.6).

    Two overall checks. First, dry-season length is set by measurement — the dry season begins after 3 consecutive ten-day periods with rainfall <30 mm per period; settlements with a dry season <60 days run under the "no-dry-season regime" (all sun-drying switches to the drying room, fuel ledger doubled, Section 50.2). Second, every workstation sits above the ten-year highest flood level +500 mm (siting criterion, Chapter 37), with evacuation lines staked and gong signals publicly posted.

    50.1 Resources: Rainforest Resource Quick Reference¶

    The rainforest's abundance and scarcity are two sides of the same coin: abundant rainfall means scarcity of dryness; abundant biomass means abundance of mold sources. So this section's quick reference is organized not by products, but by "which raw-material line each abundant item feeds, and which failure and substitution line each scarce item enters."

    Item Rating Destination
    Water Abundant Power and drinking water assured (Chapter 29 waterwheel line); the cost enters failure ④ (waterborne disease) and ⑤ (flood)
    Wood and bamboo Very abundant Construction, fuel, and vessels all run on bamboo and wood (Chapter 13); the cost enters failure ① (mold and borers)
    Plant fibers (rattan, palm, bast) Abundant Rope and textile feedstock uninterrupted (Chapters 7 and 15); the cost enters failure ①
    Ash potash (wood ash) Abundant Industrial alkali assured: soapmaking, glass fluxing (Chapter 14), pickling aid — a chemical resource unique to the rainforest
    Drying Scarce All "sun-drying" operations downgraded, drying rooms moved forward; enters failure ⑥
    Refractory clay Scarce High-temperature interfaces lack material; go with substitute furnace linings + frequent patching (Section 50.5)
    Open-pit mines Scarce The metal line falls back on the heavy-mineral concentrate survey (Chapter 16) and the recycling loop (Chapter 17)
    Salt Scarce Table salt is the rainforest's number-one trade item and number-one substitution problem (Section 50.5)
    Stone Scarce No outcrops except dry-season riverbeds; go with bamboo-reinforced rammed earth substitution + dry-season window quarrying

    Core judgment: the rainforest's scarcity is not "absence" but "cannot be stored." Water, firewood, grain, leather, paper, iron — all present, and all molding, rusting, rotting. This chapter therefore manages "dryness" as the first resource: every sunny day of the dry season is rationed, allocated centrally by the rhythm table of Section 50.7.

    Four rainforest-specific resource items:

    1. Bamboo — the capital for fast housebuilding. It matures in 3–5 years (trees take 20–60), the rainforest's "steel stand-in": tubes (scaffolding, water pipes, air ducts), split strips (woven walls, mats), vessels (bamboo-tube cookware). One-sentence difference from Chapter 13: in the rainforest the construction line is led by elevated bamboo-timber framing. Boundary: bamboo is not durable; the two borer-proofing methods (soaking 30–60 days, or charring the surface over fire to a light scorch) are empirical practices whose effect is verified by registered borer-hole counts; expected life after treatment is 8–15 years indoors and 3–6 years outdoors (empirical values), written into the ledger with mandatory due-date inspection.
    2. Alluvial tin — the rainforest's only welcome gift to metallurgy. Alluvial cassiterite is surveyed by the panning heavy-mineral-concentrate method (Chapter 16): with tin, the copper–tin alloy line opens early; without tin, the metal line falls back to "recycling loop + small-scale bloomery smelting" (Chapters 17 and 18), without forcing capacity. Boundary: alluvial deposits pan out — a ≥30% decline in panned output over 3 consecutive months triggers a standing alert, with an early pivot to prospecting primary ore or to trade.
    3. Rattan — king of rope sources. The backbone material for suspension bridges, lashing, and weaving (the rainforest enhancement of the Chapter 7 fiber library); freshly cut rattan enters the anti-mold process (Card 50-02) within 10 days of cutting, otherwise the strength ledger starts leaking from day one.
    4. Ash alkali — potash is not sodium salt. Leaching and evaporating slash-burn ash and charcoal-kiln ash yields potassium carbonate: soapmaking and glass fluxing (Chapter 14) can be fully self-sufficient — this is the rainforest's chemical advantage. But it must not be eaten as salt — it is bitter and purgative; the food-use boundary is in Section 50.5, written honestly.

    [Card 50-01 Rainforest Site Assessment] (eight fields)

    1. Input materials: the land under assessment and its surroundings; existing population and tool inventory. Do this before breaking ground — the rainforest error is "we built it and then found it will not keep," and rework costs far more than one more survey.
    2. Tools required: record board, staff gauge or flood-mark reference (mud lines on trunks, color bands on banks), rainfall proxies (moss and lichen belts, elders' accounts). Calibration source: the dry season and the flood line are measured or read from physical marks, never estimated by feel.
    3. Sequence and control points: (1) grade all nine resources in 50.1 (water; wood and bamboo; plant fiber; ash potassium; dryness; refractory clay; open-pit ore; salt; stone) → (2) walk all seven failure lines in 50.3 looking for precursors (mold and fungus; damp insulation; metal corrosion; waterborne disease; flood and landslide; drying failure; borers) → (3) map each "abundant" and each "scarce" rating onto the failure line it feeds → (4) set countermeasures and pre-emptive works for the weak items. Control points: both halves are mandatory — resources alone miss the failures, failures alone miss the usable wealth.
    4. Empirical parameters and calibration: dry season = three consecutive ten-day periods with under 30 mm of rain (the construction window is defined this way, not by month); flood safety line = highest flood in ten years +500 mm (hard rule — workshop and granary floor levels must sit above it). The core judgment in one sentence: what the rainforest lacks is not "things" but "keeping" — water, fuel, grain, hides, paper, iron are all present, and all of them are molding, rusting or rotting.
    5. Observations to record: all nine ratings / precursor sightings along the seven failure lines / measured flood-mark elevation / measured start and end of the dry season / weak-item list with countermeasures.
    6. Pass / rework / scrap criteria: pass = all nine and all seven concluded, weak items have countermeasures, floor level above the flood line; rework = any item left blank (go back and measure); scrap = the site sits below the flood line and cannot be raised (move).
    7. Common failures and diagnosis order: (1) no drying capacity discovered in the rainy season → dryness was graded as abundant during assessment; it is scarce, and drying houses must move to the first construction batch (50.2); (2) the first flood fills the workshop → the flood line was never measured, replaced by a few years' memory; (3) dry-season window opened too early → judged by month instead of three consecutive ten-days under 30 mm; (4) bamboo structures collapse in two years → the borer line was not assessed and no treatment was applied (Card 50-02).
    8. L1 / L2 / L3 evidence: L1 = a complete assessment sheet (nine plus seven); L2 = the assessment's conclusions match the actual failure record of the following year; L3 = a second person assesses the same site from this card alone, flood-line readings within 300 mm.

    50.2 Sequence: The Rainforest Technology Route¶

    List of differences from the master outline's order (each with a reason; re-sequence the chapter start-ups accordingly):

    Technology item Master-outline position Rainforest adjustment Reason
    Drying and storage facilities (drying rooms, elevated storehouses, smoke houses) With Chapters 6/10 Moved forward to the first batch The rainforest's "cannot store" precedes "cannot make" — without drying rooms, firewood, grain, green ware, and paper are all constrained
    Anti-malaria engineering (drainage, gauze, mosquito nets) Chapter 45 Moved forward to the settlement-siting stage Fever-driven attrition is the rainforest's number-one capacity killer, ahead of every workshop
    Waterwheels and bamboo-timber construction Chapters 21/13 Moved earlier, before the mining-metallurgy line Use the water-A, wood-A abundant items to the full first
    Salt making — Promoted early as a special project Without salt, both the food-preservation line (Chapter 10) and physiological needs are constrained; ash potash cannot substitute
    Pottery and glass Chapters 11/14 Glass postponed Silicon source B–C and drying scarce — the fuel/drying cost of crucibles and annealing is highest in the rainforest; pottery proceeds as usual
    Metal line Chapters 16–19 Start with heavy-mineral tin + the recycling loop; bloomery smelting at small scale Open-pit mines scarce; capacity waits on the alluvial-tin survey conclusion
    Electrical line Chapters 30–32 Postponed until the insulation system passes rainforest-adapted acceptance High-humidity insulation is the electrical line's rainforest gate (Section 50.3 ②)
    Paper and archives Chapter 41 Moisture-proofing add-ons moved earlier (smoke house + oil sealing) One molded batch of archives collapses a corner of the whole record-keeping system

    Dry-season window discipline: all operations needing "sun" (grain, green ware, paper, firewood, leather) are completed within the dry season and sealed into storage; the rainy season does only indoor work (making, repair, medicine, teaching). Settlements with a dry season <60 days: all sun-drying switches to oven-drying (the drying-room fuel ledger goes into Chapter 42's doubled column).

    50.3 Failure: Which Line Fails First in the Rainforest (Core of This Chapter)¶

    Ordered by "fails first." Each line gives precursors (observable signals for the duty board) and the consequence chain.

    No. Failure line Precursors (observable before failure) Consequence chain
    ① Mold and fungi (leather, rope, grain, paper — products of Chapters 15/10/45) White frost spots on leather; green patches on rope with falling tensile strength; damp caking on grain-bag inner walls; curled paper edges with mold specks Gear lost → archives destroyed → grain stores damaged — all three main lines collapse simultaneously
    ② High-humidity insulation failure (electrical line, Chapters 30–32) Coarse leakage test (Section 31.4.3 method) readings pronounced after rain; water beads chaining on insulators; "tingling" switch handles Shock accident → grid shutdown → whole-plant blackout
    ③ Metal corrosion (hot-humid year-round) Accelerated coupon weight loss (see Section 50.4); rust spots merging across tool faces Cutting-tool life halved → tool-ledger consumption doubled → capacity down
    ④ Waterborne disease (Chapter 45: cholera/dysentery class) Weekly fever and diarrhea counts above baseline (Section 50.6) Attrition → roster collapse (Chapter 37) → epidemic quarantine (Chapter 45)
    ⑤ Flood and landslide Sudden rise in river turbidity; the sound of rain upstream (water sounds at midnight) Workshops flooded → furnaces and kilns destroyed → ledgers and archives soaked
    ⑥ Drying failure Firewood burns sluggish when lit; grain bags warming (Chapter 10 pre-mold sign) Fuel heating value drops → furnace temperature collapses (Chapter 12 ①: check fuel) → grain stores mold
    ⑦ Borer insects (bamboo-timber structures) Frass powder piling at member bases; hollow knocking sound Load-bearing members of elevated structures fail → house collapse

    Item Master-outline routine cycle Rainforest cycle Criterion / method
    Surface-plate / measuring-tool rust-proof oiling Quarterly (Section 23.6) Monthly; fortnightly in the rainy season Coupon comparison after oiling (Section 50.3 ③)
    Electrical insulation testing Must test after every rain (Chapter 32) Must test after every rain + weekly routine Section 31.4.3 coarse leakage test, Card 50-03
    Granary turning and sunning Monthly (Chapter 10) Ten-day inspection; drying rooms run continuously In-bag hand feel + warming check (hand inserted into the bag for temperature)
    Roofing Quarterly inspection (Chapter 13) Monthly inspection; immediate inspection after typhoon/rainstorm Water-pour test
    Leather gear Monthly oiling (Section 7.8) Fortnightly + smoke-house rotation White-frost spot count
    Metal tool oiling Oil immediately after use Oil immediately after use + weekly patrol If rust spots merge, de-rust and re-oil at once
    Ropes (rattan/leather) Quarterly static-load inspection (Section 7.4) Monthly inspection + after-rain hand feel Section 15.7 load-calibration spot checks
    Archive storeroom Monthly airing (Chapter 41) Ten-day airing + smoke-house rotation A dry paper strip must not go soft
    Timber member borer-proofing Annual inspection (Chapter 13) Quarterly inspection + frass-powder patrol Knock and listen for a hollow sound

    The three anti-mold lines (general regulation for all rainforest storage): first line, drying (drying room/smoke house, with the same four dryness criteria as Chapter 10); second line, ventilation (elevated, off the wall, cross-flow vents); third line, agents (mugwort/camphor leaves/fumigation — empirical methods labeled honestly: no guarantee, multiple means combined, effect compared via registered mold-spot counts).

    [Card 50-02 Three-Stage Mold Control] (eight fields)

    1. Input materials: leather, rope, paper, bamboo and timber (anything organic is on mold's menu); fuel for the drying house; smoking material; oil, wax, or clay jars with desiccant for sealing.
    2. Tools required: drying house (the first-batch facility per 50.2), smoke house (rotatable), sealed containers. Calibration source: dryness follows the Chapter 10 "four dryness checks" — do not invent local standards.
    3. Sequence and control points (three stages, order not interchangeable): dry (into the drying house until all four Chapter 10 checks pass) → smoke (rotate through the smoke house half a day at a time — continuous smoking does not reach evenly) → seal (oil coat, wax coat, or clay jar with desiccant, chosen per item). Control point: smoking an undried item is wasted smoke; drop one of the three stages and mold simply arrives later.
    4. Empirical parameters and calibration: smoke rotation half a day; treated bamboo service life 8–15 years indoors, 3–6 years outdoors (empirical, calibrate to local species); the log enforces the expiry check — a bamboo part's life is not an approximate figure, it is a due date written into the ledger.
    5. Observations to record: per batch — item class / drying-house in and out dates with dryness result / smoke rotations / sealing method / storage date / expiry check date.
    6. Pass / rework / scrap criteria: pass = all three stages done and expiry checks on record; rework = a stage skipped, redo it (a missed dry means back into the drying house, not a makeup smoke); scrap = continuous mold patches present — isolate and destroy, do not store.
    7. Common failures and diagnosis order: (1) white bloom on leather → drying incomplete, back to the drying house; (2) green patches on rope with falling strength → not smoked or smoked unevenly; (3) damp caking on the inside of grain sacks → sealed container leaking, switch to clay jar with desiccant; (4) paper edges curling → ambient humidity too high, check whether storage sits against wall or floor.
    8. L1 / L2 / L3 evidence: L1 = one batch through all three stages with records; L2 = three consecutive batches with mold rates tallyable and below untreated controls; L3 = a second person processes a batch from this card alone and passes the expiry-check regime.

    [Card 50-03 Damp-Weather Insulation Testing] (eight fields)

    1. Input materials: the circuit under test (Chapter 30–32 electrical lines); battery bank as test source; galvanometer. This card measures line-to-earth leakage, not continuity.
    2. Tools required: battery bank (series-connected to ≥3× the operating voltage), galvanometer, dry cloth for terminals. Calibration source: the reference is the fair-weather reading of the same circuit — each line is compared against itself, with no absolute threshold.
    3. Sequence and control points: take a fair-weather baseline first → retest after rain and on the weekly routine → record and compare → cut power and investigate on exceedance. Control points: a baseline must exist first (a wet-day reading is meaningless without it); dry the terminals before measuring — a wet terminal reads nothing useful.
    4. Empirical parameters and calibration: test voltage ≥3× operating voltage (battery bank in series); criterion = the after-rain reading must not exceed twice the fair-weather reading, above which cut power and inspect insulators and bushings; in the rainy season "test after every rain" is upgraded to a weekly routine (the 50.4 maintenance doubling).
    5. Observations to record: per test — date and weather / test voltage / reading / ratio to baseline / action (normal, investigate, isolate).
    6. Pass / rework / scrap criteria: pass = reading ≤ twice baseline; rework = exceedance, isolate the circuit and work through sections checking water-beaded bushings, damp insulation, water in joints; scrap = insulation carbonized or punctured (replace that whole run).
    7. Common failures and diagnosis order: (1) readings jump after rain → check bushings for continuous water beading first, then joints for water ingress; (2) a switch handle that "tingles" → late-stage leakage; isolate that circuit immediately, do not wait for the test cycle; (3) the same reading swings up and down → terminals not dried or the galvanometer itself is damp; (4) a reading permanently high with no rain → aged insulation, not humidity.
    8. L1 / L2 / L3 evidence: L1 = one circuit with baseline and after-rain readings compared; L2 = baselines built for all circuits, the rainy-season weekly routine run, and at least one exceedance correctly investigated; L3 = a second person builds a baseline and judges from this card alone, both reaching the same conclusion on whether a reading exceeds.

    50.6 Malaria and Vectors: The Rainforest's Number-One Killer Is the Microbe¶

    Setting the tone: the first killer of the rainforest edition is not beasts, but mosquitoes and water. Anti-malaria engineering ranks ahead of every workshop:

    1. Water line (Chapter 45 moved forward to the settlement period): workshops and residential areas drain away all standing water — invert containers, fill rut puddles, cover water vats and keep fish in them (mosquitofish, if registered); replace dead-water ponds with flowing channels;
    2. Barrier line: mosquito nets (Chapter 15 fine-weave textiles, the rainforest edition's first-priority product) + gauze door curtains + smoldering smoke at bedtime (mugwort/rotten-wood smolder, registered as an empirical method);
    3. Medical line (Chapter 47 interface): weekly fever-register baseline; the default fever protocol — any fever at ≥2 cases/week (twice baseline) triggers it: isolation (Section 45.7), fever registration (Chapter 47 case sheets), water-supply line screening (Section 47.4 four-question form), doubled rainy-season mosquito control. No route for malaria-specific diagnosis (a medical boundary of this manual); manage by syndrome;
    4. Labor linkage (Chapter 37): the rainy-season roster carries a built-in shift-covering redundancy of "20 percent sick" — a roster without redundancy will collapse in the rainy season.

    50.7 Timetable: The Annual Rhythm of the Dry and Rainy Seasons¶

    Season Monthly schedule Main line Auxiliary line
    Dry season First 1–2 ten-day periods of the dry season Build and burn: kiln overhaul and drying-out firing, building foundations, large-scale charcoal, pottery, and lime burning Quarrying (riverbed outcrop window)
    Dry season Middle stretch Sun and store: grain, firewood, green ware, paper, leather dried in batches, stored and sealed Heavy-item transport (dry overland period)
    Dry season Final stretch Gear refurbishment; concentrated tool de-rusting and oiling Flood drills; re-staking evacuation lines
    Rainy season Whole season Make, repair, and heal: indoor workshops (textiles, soapmaking, assembly, repair), teaching and instruction (Chapter 43), health checks and vector patrols Drying-room duty; flood watch
    Rainy season Rainstorm alert Flood contingency (Chapter 40): cut power, raise goods, withdraw lines — execute in the established order, no on-the-spot changes —

    Rainy-year contingency column: in years with a dry season <60 days, automatic triggers — non-essential firing operations canceled, trading for drying services, drying-room expansion started as priority work.

    50.9 Agronomy: Raised Beds, Damp-Proof Cellars, and the Drying Double Safeguard¶

    The overarching contradiction of rainforest agriculture is that crops need water but need even more "not to be waterlogged" — root crops (cassava, taro, yams; the Chapter 10 root-crop line) rot after 3 days submerged. One-sentence difference from Chapter 10: fields switch from "water-retention design" to "drainage design" — all field surfaces above the ditch bottoms, with irrigation demoted to dry-season moisture top-up.

    1. Raised-bed drained fields (bed width and ditch depth by rainfall class)

    Rainfall class (annual rainfall, rain-gauge annual ledger, calibrated per Chapter 3) Bed-top width Ditch depth Ditch width
    <2000 mm 2.0–3.0 m 300 mm 400 mm
    2000–3000 mm 1.5–2.0 m 400 mm 400 mm
    >3000 mm 1.0–1.5 m 500 mm 500 mm

    All three classes are empirical starting values, with two field-calibration checks. First, after the first heavy rain on a newly opened field, ponded water on the bed tops should drain clear within 1 h of the rain stopping; overtime means deepening the ditch one class or raising the bed top another 50 mm. Second, standing water in the ditches should fall below 1/3 of ditch depth within 24 h; if it does not, check the main drainage outlet for blockage, or tighten the bed-to-bed ditch spacing to 10–15 m. Bed sides are compacted in layers: after every 150 mm of loose soil, tamp one pass before laying the next layer; bank-collapse accounting — more than 1 collapse point per 100 m of bed side in the first rainy season judges the compaction insufficient, and from the following year bed building adds one item: "plant creeping bank-guard grass along the bed edges."

    2. Root cellars — the rainforest retrofit of the Chapter 10 cellar (the elevated damp-proof cellar)

    One-sentence difference: the rainforest water table is high — dig deep and you have dug a well — so the cellar changes from "digging underground" to "building above ground." Construction points: the cellar body elevated ≥600 mm off the ground, with the buried sections of the posts treated by the two borer-proofing methods (Section 50.1); a double roof — the upper roof blocks rain, the lower blocks nighttime moisture rebound — with a 100 mm ventilated cavity between the two; vents at eave height fitted with insect mesh; inside the cellar a bamboo-strip mat raises the load another 100 mm, so the roots touch no solid surface directly. ◎ Cellar in/out goods-inspection drill: fingertip-impression rebound — press 20 times each on roots known to be dry and roots known to have rebounded damp, and memorize the hand feel. Compensating physical evidence: draw 3 roots per storage batch and weigh them, re-weighing every ten-day period; a ten-day weight loss >3% judges the cellar too dry (a damp mat into the cellar or a spray of water mist — same handling as Chapter 10), while weight gain or a sticky skin judges moisture pickup (spread out to air, or re-dry). Ledger: stock in/out and the ten-day weighings in one combined book, merged into the Section 50.4 ten-day inspection loop.

    3. Post-harvest drying double safeguard (sun-drying + drying-room relay)

    The rule in one sentence: the sunning yard dries only "half-day grain"; the drying room is the terminus, not the backup. Operating sequence: cut and spread on harvest day (root crops sliced 3–5 mm thick; grain per the Chapter 10 spread-sunning method); the yard duty watch scans the clouds every 2 h — cumulus overhead or distant thunder means gather into the drying room at once — no gambling that "the cloud might pass"; drying-room relay temperature ≤60 ℃, drying until Chapter 10's four dryness criteria (no white core at the cut, a crisp snap when broken, constant weight on re-weighing, no moisture rebound in the bag) before storage. Batch criterion: storage moisture spot checks (stir-dry weighing method, same as the Section 50.4 firewood samples) meeting the target for 3 consecutive batches set the pass line; failing batches go back to the drying room — no downgraded storage; the relay oven-drying firewood consumption is pre-provisioned in Chapter 42's fuel-doubling column.

    4. Rainy-season green-fodder supplement

    With the fields and sunning yards shut down in the rainy season, livestock feed (Section 10.7) switches mainly to green fodder: elephant grass, sugarcane tops, banana stems and leaves, tender vines; cutting radius ≤30 minutes on foot (a labor constraint). Green fodder is >80% water, so the pens must be mixed with dry feed (hay and bran stored before the rainy season) at no less than a 1/3 ratio, otherwise the animals lose condition and scour — criterion: feces-form observation; 3 consecutive days of loose feces means raising the dry-feed ratio. Green fodder is cut and fed the same day; anything left composting overnight is discarded. In the rainy season you do not store green fodder; what you store is dry feed.

    50.10 Bamboo Craft Deep-Dive: Three Fine Trades — Strips, Pipes, Reinforcement¶

    Section 50.1 set bamboo's position; this section gives three deep-processing points. Tier declaration: Section 50.10.3, bamboo-reinforced rammed earth, is register-level pre-research (Tier 1); the strips and pipes items are executed at Tier 3.

    1. Bamboo-strip grading (drawing on the Chapter 7 fiber library)

    One-sentence difference: rainforest bamboo strips are not offcuts but the main tension-bearing material and must be used by grade. Splitting yields layers: green strips (the outer layer with the bamboo skin, tensile and weather-resistant) and yellow strips (the inner layers, brittle and not moisture-tolerant).

    Grade Layer taken Uses Downgrade/scrap criterion
    Top-grade green strip Outermost skin-bearing layer, 3–5 mm wide Rope cores, mat warps, load-bearing lashings Breaking on a 180° bend means downgrade
    Second-grade green strip First layer under the green layer Woven-wall facing, mat surfaces Fuzz rising in patches on the surface
    Yellow strip Inner layers Filling, single-use items, kindling Not for load-bearing use (restricted outright, no rework path)

    ◎ Bend-grading is hand-feel work: with strips split from the same culm, practice 30 times and note the layer each strip that "passes 180° and springs back" comes from. Compensating physical evidence: draw 5 strips per batch for a static hang test — a single top-grade green strip hanging 15 kg for 1 min without breaking passes (empirical value, transcribed into the ledger after calibration); anything whose hand feel is in doubt goes through the hang test, no exceptions.

    2. Three bamboo pipe joining methods (common to water pipes / air ducts)

    Method Practice Labor per joint (empirical) Durability class (empirical) Application
    Bamboo node joint Cut the pipe leaving nodes naturally; drill the node partitions through; socket into the open end of the next section 0.2–0.3 person-hours 1–2 years; node splitting is the main cause Temporary water conveyance, scaffolding
    Sleeve-and-lash joint Remove the nodes from the smaller pipe and sleeve it into the larger; pack the seam with hemp fiber dipped in hot wax/resin; lash with rattan strips ≥3 wraps 0.3–0.5 person-hours 2–4 years; lashings replaced quarterly (monthly inspection in the rainy season) Water trunk lines
    Wood-spigot joint Carve a double-ended tapered hardwood spigot as a detachable joint, removable and refurbishable 0.5–1.0 person-hours 3–6 years; the spigot can be refurbished twice Pump inlets, maintenance-access sections

    Control points shared by all three methods: pipe sections are always cut across nodes (each section contains at least one complete node partition as internal bracing, against flattening); finished joints get the full-water test — let stand a quarter hour; drips <10 drops/min pass, 10 drops up to a thread-like stream may be reworked (add packing and re-lash), a continuous thread-like stream means scrap and replace the pipe. Wood-spigot taper calibration: first carve tapers of 1:8, 1:10, 1:12, two pieces each, and trial-fit; take as standard the loosest class that "holds water when inverted after being driven in"; from this make the wooden template (measuring-tool practice per Chapter 23), then cut spigots in batches to the template. The two borer-proofing treatments on joint parts are mandatory, merged into the borer-proofing ledger (the Section 50.1 due-date inspection system).

    3. Bamboo-reinforced rammed earth trial (register-level pre-research)

    This subsection is Tier 1: it only answers "how many rainy seasons can bamboo reinforcement survive inside rammed earth"; promotion conditions = observation-window data across two rainy seasons + the test-wall crack atlas; missing either, no promotion, and pre-research conclusions must not be used directly on load-bearing walls. Trial design: ram two test walls (each 2 m × 1 m × 400 mm thick), mixed per the local existing rammed-earth recipe; one wall plain-rammed, one with bamboo strips (20–30 mm wide, 5–8 mm thick, treated by the two borer-proofing methods before going into the wall) buried horizontally on a 300 × 300 mm grid. Crack observation window: one wedge-shaped slot pre-cut at the wall top; a 100 mm grid ruled on the wall face; photographed at each rainy season's end, with crack widths measured by feeler gauge and registered. Formalization criteria: annual crack-width increment <1 mm, and, after the second rainy season ends, a 200 mm square inspection window opened and the dug inspection showing the strips free of frass powder and free of softening — only when both conditions hold can "bamboo-reinforced rammed earth" move from the trial row to a formal row in the Section 50.5 matrix; if either fails, continue the trial one more year, or fall back to the thickened plain-rammed-earth scheme.

    [Card 50-04 Bamboo Pipe Joints] (eight fields)

    1. Input materials: bamboo pipe (cut across a node — the node is a natural reinforcement ring, cutting through it ruins one end), hardwood spigot stock, hemp tow, hot wax or resin, rattan or split-bamboo lashing. Internal diameter ≥25 mm or water will not move.
    2. Tools required: splitting knife (Chapter 7 edge tool), awl, wooden mallet, tapered wooden plug (for flaring), wax-melting vessel. Calibration source: internal diameter measured with Chapter 3 rules; sealing judged by "water flows and does not seep," not by how much wax was smeared on.
    3. Sequence and control points: cut across the node → dress the end square and deburr → heat and coat the end and outer wall with wax or resin → wrap hemp tow (in the direction opposite to insertion) → drive in the hardwood spigot (taper fit) → lash with rattan. Control points: cut position (must span a node) and the wax must be fully hot — cool wax only coats the surface and lifts the moment it soaks.
    4. Empirical parameters and calibration: internal diameter ≥25 mm; record life in three parts — joint 1–2 years / lashing 2–4 years / spigot 3–6 years (empirical, calibrate); the log enforces expiry checks — replace before failure, not after the leak.
    5. Observations to record: per run — installed location / internal diameter / joint method / installation date / the three part-lives and their due dates / pressure test result.
    6. Pass / rework / scrap criteria: pass = flows without seeping and due dates logged; rework = joint seeps, take it apart, recoat with hot wax and re-wrap; scrap = pipe wall split (replace the section; do not bandage it with cloth).
    7. Common failures and diagnosis order: (1) joint seeps → wax not fully hot or too little tow; (2) joint blows apart under flow → lashing too loose or spigot taper too shallow; (3) flow shrinks over time → algae or sediment inside, pull the section and rod it out; (4) splits within a year → cut through a node, or used untreated young bamboo.
    8. L1 / L2 / L3 evidence: L1 = one joint plus pressure test record; L2 = a complete line (≥5 sections) with all three part-lives logged and one scheduled replacement executed; L3 = a second person makes one section from this card alone and passes the test, with diameter and due dates logged identically.

    50.11 The Rainy-Season Medical Kits: Supplies Before Cases¶

    The master principle, of the same root as Section 50.6: contingency plans must land on supplies, not on resolve. Three kits and one card, checked kit by kit at the start of the rainy season; moisture-proofing merged into the 37-02 sealing line, and the checks merged into the Section 50.4 ten-day inspection.

    1. Five-day fever medicine kit (drawing on the Chapter 50 system)

    One-sentence rainforest difference from the Chapter 46 medicine-stock system: stock supplies against a "5-day isolation observation period," with one kit each placed at the duty posts, the flood-evacuation points, and every boat. Per-kit list (empirical quotas): 10 packets of oral rehydration powder (formula in the next item), 10 clean cloth strips/gauze pads, 200 mL of strong liquor or alcohol (external use only), one bundle of dried mugwort leaves (smoldering smoke to repel mosquitoes, also the smoke-house starter). For temperature judgment, this manual has no thermometer within bounds: pulse counting is the primary criterion (Chapter 3 timekeeping method; a resting pulse >100 beats/min is registered as suspected high fever and handed to the medic for the Section 50.6 fever register); ◎ the back-of-hand-to-forehead comparison is auxiliary: the same on-duty person first touches a healthy person and then the patient, and must complete 10 practice rounds before qualifying; for misjudgment compensation, the pulse count always prevails. Kit moisture-proofing: oilcloth wrap plus ceramic-jar storage; ten-day kit checks in the rainy season; caked medicines or liquor evaporated beyond 1/3 means replace with new.

    2. Standing oral rehydration supply (drawing on the Chapter 48 formula)

    One-sentence rainforest difference from the Chapter 44 formula: salt is expensive in the rainforest; rehydration salt is charged to a dedicated quota in the salt ledger and must not encroach on pickling-duty salt. Pre-mixed powder, one packet per 1 L of water: salt 3.5 g + glucose 20 g (or sucrose 40 g — slightly slower absorption, acceptable); when no sugar is available, the rice-water recipe (500 mL rice water + 500 mL clean water + 1.75 g salt, mixed fresh and drunk at once, same as Chapter 44). Hard boundary: ash alkali (Section 50.1 ④) is forbidden as a salt substitute — potash is bitter and purgative, the opposite of the rehydration goal. The standing quantity is 3 packets per person per rainy season (empirical value: estimated at a 30 percent rainy-season diarrhea attack rate); calibration method: regress the standing coefficient on this settlement's diarrhea case counts over the past three rainy seasons, transcribe the revision into the ledger, and thereafter consult only the ledger, never memory.

    3. Mosquito-net repair kit

    Mosquito nets are consumables of the Section 50.6 barrier line, and consumables must have a supply line: one kit per 10 nets (mesh patches, thread, crochet hook, cloth-binding strips), hung at the net area's public notice point and drawn whenever damage occurs. Repair criteria: no light-transmitting holes when held against the light; patch edges overlapping the hole perimeter by ≥30 mm; the door-flap overlap pressed so no seam shows. A net serviceability rate ≥95% is a patrol check item; failing in a given ten-day period means activating the repair kit and scheduling the Chapter 15 fine-weave workstation for re-weaving.

    4. Sanitary patrol rainy-season frequency card (drawing on Chapter 49)

    One-sentence rainforest difference from the Chapter 45 patrol system: frequency doubled per the Section 50.4 frequency-multiplying discipline, with "water and mosquitoes" as the organizing theme. One round every 5 days, led by the medic with 2 people (labor hours merged into the Section 50.4 ten-day inspection loop; redundancy drawn from the Section 50.6 "20 percent sick" shift-covering allowance):

    Item Criterion Action when failing
    Standing-water points All registered points dry or converted to flowing water; new finds registered and handled the same day Invert / fill / cover and stock fish (the three Section 50.6 water-line methods)
    Mosquito-net serviceability ≥95% Repair kit clears the backlog within the ten-day period
    Water vats covered All covered Uncovered vats suspended that day until covered
    Weekly fever count Against the Section 50.6 baseline Twice baseline triggers the default protocol
    Medicine-kit check All three locations complete and within the replacement period Make complete within the ten-day period; posts missing kits go understaffed on duty

    50.12 The Alluvial-Tin Beneficiation Line: Store in the Dry Season, Dress in the Rainy Season¶

    The Chapter 16 heavy-mineral survey answers "is there tin"; this section answers "how it becomes concentrate on the monthly ledger." The route in one sentence: mine and haul raw sand in a concentrated effort in the dry season; run hydraulic sluice dressing in the rainy season; merge the two ledgers — pan-dressed tin (dry panning) and water-dressed tin (sluice) — into one book.

    1. Concentrated dry-season mining and haulage at the panning points

    Dry-season riverbed outcrops are the mining-and-haulage window (already listed among the Section 50.7 auxiliary lines): at the ≥3 panning points on the Chapter 16 survey register, mine the tin-bearing heavy sand point by point — mining criterion: only sand layers yielding ≥5 cassiterite grains per trial pan are worked; below that line, abandoned. Raw sand is hauled back and stockpiled under a shed at the sand storage yard (against rain washing out the fine particles), with a three-point record per source: volume, estimated weight, and trial-panning grain count. ◎ Panning swirl hand feel (Chapter 16 method) practice: new workers complete a full 200 pans on discard sand; the qualification criterion is "heavy sand spread into a fan-shaped band in the pan, cassiterite grains gathered on the band's crest"; compensating physical evidence: for every 10 pans of practice, compare pans against a master craftsman on the same sand; anyone whose grain-count difference is >1/3 keeps practicing.

    2. Hydraulic sluice (the rainforest version of the Chapter 16 sluice)

    One-sentence difference: in the dry season the sluice is dismantled and stored, and dressing relies on hand panning; in the rainy season water is plentiful and the sluice is set up to dress continuously — the same sluice, two uses across two seasons. Parameters (empirical starting points): sluice length 3–4 m; grade 8–12%, calibrated on site by three-class trial runs — a grade class passes when sample re-panning of the tailings over 10 pans yields ≤2 cassiterite grains; feed water is set so the sluice water depth is 20–50 mm and the bottom sand does not visibly churn; riffle bars spaced 100–150 mm and 30–40 mm high, with a coarse woven-strip mat on the sluice floor to create vortices. Stop the sluice to clean the concentrate every 4 h; the floor concentrate is re-dressed once more on the pan before going into the concentrate barrel. Recovery verification once per shift: draw 10 pans of tailings for re-panning; more than 2 grains means adjusting the grade or reducing the water.

    3. Pan-dressed tin / sluice-dressed tin joint operation

    Dry-season panning output is booked in the "pan-dressed tin" column, rainy-season sluice output in the "sluice-dressed tin" column, and sand-yard stock in the "awaiting dressing" column. The joint logic: what the dry season stores is raw sand, not concentrate — concentrate has high haulage and storage costs, and water dressing in the rainy season recovers better, so the dry season pans out only the assay and trial-smelting quantities (small-furnace trials, Chapters 16–18), storing the rest as raw sand.

    4. The tin-output monthly ledger (Chapter 38 interface)

    The monthly ledger has four columns: raw sand mined and hauled, concentrate output, tailing spot-check grain counts, and warning-line comparison. The warning line carries over from Section 50.1 ② — output declining ≥30% for 3 consecutive months is flagged red on the books, and in that same month triggers a binary decision: pivot to primary-ore prospecting per Chapter 16, or backfill via the Section 50.5 trade route; the decision is written into next month's plan and may not be left pending. The monthly ledger merges each month into the Chapter 42 materials master ledger, as the input quantity for Chapters 16–19 smelting scheduling.

    [Card 50-05 Alluvial-Tin Sluice] (eight fields)

    1. Input materials: heavy-mineral raw sand from the stockpile (pan test ≥5 grains per pan or hauling is not worth it); diversion-channel water (dry-season minimum flow already calculated per Chapter 29). The dry season stockpiles raw sand, not concentrate — which is why the line has to serve two seasons.
    2. Tools required: sluice body (wood or bamboo, 3–4 m long), felt or coarse cloth lining to trap cassiterite, pan for tailings checks. Calibration source: slope measured with the Chapter 3 level frame and rule; concentrate grade judged indirectly by re-panning tailings.
    3. Sequence and control points: set the sluice and its slope → lay the felt → run water to test flow → feed evenly → stop periodically to clean the felt and collect concentrate → pan-check tailings. Control points: slope (too steep and concentrate runs off, too flat and the feed will not move) and even feeding (batch dumping scours the felt bed).
    4. Empirical parameters and calibration: length 3–4 m; slope 8–12% (empirical; three-step calibration sets the local value); tailings re-pan ≤2 grains per 10 pans defines a passing slope; mining threshold pan test ≥5 grains per pan; tin warning line — three consecutive months of ≥30% decline in output flags red on the monthly ledger and sends you back to the Chapter 16 survey.
    5. Observations to record: per shift — raw sand fed / concentrate produced / tailings check counts / slope / water used / any slope adjustment. Monthly ledger in four columns: raw sand hauled, concentrate produced, tailings check counts, warning-line comparison.
    6. Pass / rework / scrap criteria: pass = tailings re-pan ≤2 per 10 pans with steady concentrate; rework = tailings over limit, adjust slope first then check whether feeding was too fast; scrap = none (the sluice itself is dismantled and stored, re-erected next rainy season).
    7. Common failures and diagnosis order: (1) tin grains in the tailings → slope too steep or flow too high; reduce and re-pan to confirm; (2) feed will not move, bed silts up → slope too flat or water too low; (3) concentrate full of light sand → feeding too fast for the felt bed to stratify; (4) output declining month after month at the same slope → not the sluice; the source is exhausting — watch the warning line.
    8. L1 / L2 / L3 evidence: L1 = one season of sluice work with tailings checks; L2 = three-step slope calibration done, the best step holding ≤2 per 10 pans, and three consecutive months of the four-column ledger; L3 = a second person sets up, slopes and runs to the same tailings figure from this card alone.
