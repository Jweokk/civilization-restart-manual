    Chapter 17 The First Metal¶

    Capability Boundaries¶

    Can do: crucible reduction producing copper and casting it (the master process, directly inherited by Chapter 18); the cold-forging–annealing cycle; three alloy directions — bronze / arsenical copper / leaded copper. A complete copper recycling loop (the first material that can be remelted indefinitely).

    Explicitly cannot do: quantitative alloy composition (Chapters 23, 26); iron tongs, anvil, and hammer (transitional tooling before the Chapter 18 bootstrap); interchangeability supported by ingot consistency (everything one-piece fitted until Chapter 22); heavy-duty hard-rock tools (copper's strength ceiling — the 17.7 limitations table).

    External dependencies: charcoal reserve ≥50 kg; oxide ore at grade ≥20% (the 16.7 economic criterion).

    Chapter Objectives¶

    • Obtain the first batch of metallic copper: both the native-copper cold-forging route and the malachite reduction route must be made to work (regions with native copper must pass acceptance on both, because crucible reduction is the foundational skill for all later metallurgy).
    • Establish the complete operating procedure for crucible reduction smelting: batching, charging, furnace temperature, blast, completion judgment, tapping — this is the master process for iron smelting in Chapter 18.
    • Establish simple casting: stone molds and clay molds, the pouring procedure, and troubleshooting of the four major casting defects.
    • Establish the criteria and operations of the forging–annealing–work-hardening cycle.
    • Establish usage positioning and a maintenance system for copper tools (copper can be remelted — the first material that is "fully recoverable at end of life").
    • Map the alloy directions: bronze ratios, the arsenical copper boundary.
    • Deliverables: ≥1 kg of copper, 3 or more copper tools passing functional tests, and a crucible-smelting parameter archive.

    17.1 Why Some Metals Are Easier to Obtain Than Others¶

    Three physical-chemical facts determine the order:

    1. Oxide stability. The less reactive the metal, the more easily carbon strips oxygen from its oxide. Copper oxide begins to be reduced by CO at roughly 700–800°C; iron oxide needs higher temperatures and reacts far more slowly; cassiterite is both hard to reduce and hard to melt into a liquid (it needs a flux).
    2. Melting point. Copper 1083°C — within reach of the Chapter 12 strong-bellows high-temperature furnace; iron 1538°C — furnaces of this stage are nowhere near melting iron, so iron smelting takes the solid-state reduction route (Chapter 18), while copper smelting takes the liquid-state route.
    3. Native metal occurrence. Copper, gold, and platinum can exist in elemental form at the surface (geological reason: low reactivity). Native copper can be cold-forged directly into objects, skipping the entire smelting process — the cheapest entry point to "the first metal."

    17.2 Native Copper and Early Metalworking¶

    Native copper identification (all three criteria must pass):

    1. Copper-red or brownish-red (green patina on the surface after oxidation — the green patina is itself the indicator; scratch it open to see copper-colored metal).
    2. Malleability: flattens under hammer blows without cracking — the opposite of every brittle mineral; this is a metal-only criterion, more reliable than color.
    3. Density 8.9: a piece of equal size hefts 3.4 times heavier than quartz; a streak plate yields a bright copper-colored metallic streak (a mineral streak is powder color; a metallic streak is the luster itself).

    Cold-forging test (small-sample verification before formal work): take a thumb-sized piece and tap it lightly with a stone hammer on a stone anvil. Stop when it flattens and edge cracks appear — the purer the native copper, the later the cracking. Record "the amount of thinning before first cracking" as the initial quality assessment of that deposit's copper.

    Cold-forging–annealing cycle (this chapter's first process):

    Principle: hammering distorts the metal's internal structure, making it ever harder and more brittle (work hardening); heating to a sufficient temperature lets the structure rebuild and regain softness (annealing = recrystallization). Copper that has hardened under cold forging must be annealed, or it cracks.

    Operating procedure:

    1. Cold forging: light, even hammer blows at room temperature, working from the center outward, thinning ≤20% per round (empirical value, must be field-calibrated).
    2. Hardening criteria (when to stop hammering and anneal): the rebound sound of hammering turns crisp and high-pitched; fine incipient cracks appear at the edges (hairline lines visible against the light); the hammer face starts to skid. Stop at any one of the three.
    3. Annealing: bury the workpiece in the red-hot zone of the charcoal fire (or the high-temperature zone at the furnace mouth) and heat until the surface oxide color goes purple → blue → gray with the first appearance of dark red (about 450–600°C, empirical value, must be field-calibrated; dark red first appears around 600°C; heat-color temperature judgment is auxiliary grading only, per the Chapter 6 rules).
    4. Cooling: annealed copper does not fear fast cooling — plunging it straight into water is perfectly fine and makes handling easier. This is completely different from the quenching of steel in Chapter 19; do not confuse them: copper into water is annealing (softening), steel into water is quenching (hardening).
    5. Cycle: after annealing, continue cold forging. Record thinning and cracking once per cycle to build a forgeability file for this batch of copper.

    First products: select copper sheet — cold-forge native copper lumps into 1–2 mm sheet (3–5 cycles), then cut (hammering + repeated bending to break off blanks) into small knives, awls, and fishhook blanks, and sharpen them on the Chapter 9 grinding table. Functional positioning: cutting soft materials, drilling, scraping — immediately replacing bone awls and some stone-flake knives.

    17.3 Copper Ore Processing and Smelting¶

    Ore pretreatment (continuing the Chapter 16 process):

    1. Oxide ores (malachite, cuprite): crush to 5–15 mm, hand-sort to enrich, charge directly — no roasting needed.
    2. Sulfide ores (chalcopyrite etc.): roast first to remove sulfur (the 16.5 procedure), 1–2 roasting passes, until the ore turns dark and porous; wash off the green sulfatized powder.
    3. Azurite in carbonate ores: pre-bake for over 1 hour in the high-temperature zone at the furnace mouth to decompose it (empirical value) before charging; do not let it contact the reduction zone directly.

    Reduction chemistry (one paragraph is enough): burning charcoal generates CO, and CO strips the oxygen from copper oxide: CuO + CO → Cu + CO₂; on heating, malachite first decomposes into CuO + CO₂ + H₂O, then is reduced the same way. Net effect: green stone + black charcoal + high temperature → red metal. You can operate without understanding any deeper, but two points must be remembered: (1) excess charcoal is needed to maintain a reducing atmosphere; (2) the higher the temperature, the faster the reaction; above 1083°C the product is liquid copper, which is easy to cast into a single piece.

    Crucible furnace operating procedure (master process; execute item by item):

    1. Batching (by volume, empirical value, must be field-calibrated): crushed ore 2 parts + charcoal granules (5–15 mm, never powder) 1 part, loaded in alternating layers. Ore and charcoal are not mixed but layered — the reduction interface stays stable. Add sand (quartz sand) at 1%–3% of total charge weight as flux to form slag (empirical value).
    2. Crucible: the Chapter 11 ceramic crucible, 1–2 L capacity to start. Charge to 80% full (the ore fines collapse when heated and the molten copper pools at the bottom; overfilling spills). Check the crucible for cracks before use — any that rings dull is forbidden (one that bursts when heated will splash copper).
    3. Into the furnace: preheat the high-temperature furnace (Chapter 12) to bright red (about 900°C, heat-color auxiliary criterion); set the crucible at the center of the hearth and pack charcoal around it up to the crucible rim.
    4. Heating and blowing: continuous blast from the bellows (Chapter 12); in about 15–30 minutes the hearth reaches bright orange (about 1000–1100°C, empirical value, must be field-calibrated; re-check with the Chapter 12 standard-specimen method — melting of the copper-swatch specimen means the 1100°C class is reached).
    5. Holding and reduction: keep blowing and adding charcoal, holding at bright orange for about 30–60 minutes (empirical value, must be field-calibrated). Halfway through, stir once gently with a dry wooden stick (refractory end) so the ore grains shift and touch fresh charcoal.
    6. Completion judgment: the flame at the furnace mouth is an orange-yellow CO flame and the crucible interior is calm with no boiling sound; a wooden stick probes in with little resistance and no hard-lump feel — the ore is fully reduced.
    7. Tapping: do it immediately after shutting off the blast (see 17.4 for pouring, or pour straight into ingots). Molten copper left too long in the crucible re-oxidizes.
    8. Cleaning the crucible: once cold, pour out the residual slag and inspect the crucible's inner-wall erosion. A crucible eroded to 1/3 of its wall thickness is scrapped.

    First-furnace acceptance: 1 kg of malachite concentrate should yield 350–500 g of copper (theoretical 58%, actual recovery 60%–85%, empirical value, must be field-calibrated). An ingot is acceptable if its cross-section is dense and rose-red and it flattens under the hammer without cracking. A fracture full of black-spot pores = slag not separated or temperature insufficient; return it to the furnace and re-smelt.

    Failure troubleshooting table:

    Symptom Cause Remedy
    Out of the furnace comes a sintered ore lump, not copper Temperature insufficient / not enough charcoal Increase blast and the charcoal ratio, extend the hold
    Copper beads scattered embedded in slag Slag too viscous (lacking flux) Raise sand to 3%, raise tapping temperature
    Ingots porous and blackened Reduction incomplete / secondary oxidation after tapping Extend the hold; stir with the stick before tapping to pool the copper; pour immediately
    Crucible cracks and leaks Poor crucible / heating too aggressively Change the crucible; stage the heating (preheat 15 minutes first)

    [Card 17-02 One Crucible Reduction Run] (eight fields)

    1. Input materials: crushed copper ore (malachite, azurite, identified per 17.1); charcoal granules; quartz sand flux at 1–3% of charge weight. Ore crushed to bean size — powder too fine chokes the air path.
    2. Tools required: crucible (filled to eight-tenths, leaving room for the boil), bellows (Chapter 12), furnace (Chapter 12 shaft furnace), tongs, record board. Calibration source: no pyrometer — drop a small copper offcut in as a witness; when it melts, the temperature is there.
    3. Sequence and control points: charge ore and charcoal in layers (crushed ore 2 : charcoal granules 1 by volume) → heat → hold to reduce → stir once midway → tap out. Control points: fill to eight-tenths (a full crucible boils over) and judge temperature by the witness copper melting (bright orange 1000–1100 °C is only a visual aid).
    4. Empirical parameters and calibration: ore:charcoal 2:1 by volume, layered; flux 1–3% of charge weight; target bright orange 1000–1100 °C; hold 30–60 min with one stir; first-run recovery 60–85% (1 kg of concentrate yields 350–500 g copper).
    5. Observations to record: per run — ore type and grade judgment / ore-charcoal ratio / flux amount / temperature observation (did the witness melt) / hold time / yield in grams / slag color and volume.
    6. Pass / rework / scrap criteria: pass = copper prills or a cake with recovery ≥60%; rework = unmelted or half-reduced, extend the hold or add charcoal and rerun; scrap = excessive slag with copper dispersed in it (ore type or flux judgment wrong — change the charge).
    7. Common failures and diagnosis order: (1) will not melt → check temperature (did the witness melt) first, then charcoal quantity; (2) copper dispersed in slag and unrecoverable → insufficient flux or no mid-run stir; (3) low recovery → low ore grade (recheck 17.1) or incomplete reduction; (4) crucible cracks and leaks → insufficient preheat or overfilled.
    8. L1 / L2 / L3 evidence: L1 = one run producing copper plus a recovery record; L2 = three runs on different ore sources with comparable recovery and slag; L3 = a second person smelts a run from this card alone, recoveries within 10 percentage points.

    17.4 Molten Metal and Simple Casting¶

    There are no iron tongs at this stage. Ways to hold the crucible (in priority order):

    • Option A · pole-carried crucible: leave two lugs or a waist groove when the crucible is fired; thread two long hardwood poles through, and two people carry it and pour. Wrap wet clay strips around the poles at the binding points for heat insulation (wet clay cracks as heating drives off its water; keep a clay bucket at hand for re-mudding).
    • Option B · clay-ladle ladling and pouring: for small pieces, ladle molten copper from the Chapter 11 clay ladle into the mold — extend the ladle with a long wooden handle, handle length ≥80 cm, so the operator stands one arm's length from the pour.

    Within a radius of ≥3 m of the pouring zone and the carrying route there must be no puddles, no wet soil, no ice or snow (if this cannot be met, lay dry sand first and clear the area).

    Two mold types:

    Mold type Material Suitable for Making
    Open stone mold Talc-bearing soft rock, fine sandstone (dense rock you can carve) Ingots, bar stock, simple flat pieces Cut grooves with a stone-flake knife + quartz sand; groove depth 1.2× the piece thickness (grinding allowance), groove mouth flared into a pouring trumpet
    Composite clay mold Refractory clay (Chapter 11 recipe) Pieces with shape requirements (axe and chisel blanks) Press a solid model (a wood pattern or an existing copper piece) into two clay slabs to take the impression; shade-dry, kiln-fire to harden, bind the mold halves (Chapter 7 rope); leave a pouring gate and fine vent slots (straw-stem thin cross-section, 2–4 of them)

    Pouring procedure:

    1. Mold preheating: bake the mold beside the furnace mouth to 100–200°C (hot to the touch; a water drop boils instantly). A cold or damp mold meeting molten copper will flash to steam and explode — preheating is the first line of explosion prevention.
    2. Skimming: just before pouring, scrape the dross off the copper surface at the gate with a preheated wooden slat.
    3. Pouring posture: crucible mouth close to the gate (drop ≤10 cm), a thin continuous stream — no breaks in the stream, no overflow. A stream that breaks and restarts always produces a cold shut.
    4. Solidification: let stand and cool. Thin pieces 15 minutes, thick pieces 1 hour or more before demolding (empirical value, must be field-calibrated). Never splash water on a hot casting to speed cooling — shock cooling cracks the copper + steam burns.
    5. Shakeout and cleanup: after demolding, hammer off the gate burrs and remove the flash on the grinding table.

    [Card 17-03 Pouring Procedure] (eight fields)

    1. Input materials: molten copper; fired clay mold (made per 17.5, must be preheated); skimming tool.
    2. Tools required: long-handled crucible tongs (heat-insulated), pouring cup, preheat fire. Calibration source: mold temperature judged by water — a drop hisses and boils off at 100–200 °C; if it does not, the mold is too cold.
    3. Sequence and control points: preheat the mold → skim the melt → pour in a thin continuous stream → let it solidify undisturbed → knock out and inspect. Two control points: pour height ≤10 cm (more entrains air and spatters) and an unbroken stream (a break means a cold shut).
    4. Empirical parameters and calibration: mold preheat 100–200 °C (water drop boils); pour height ≤10 cm; remelt loss 2–5% per melt (add it to the charge or the pieces shrink run after run).
    5. Observations to record: per piece — preheat judgment (water reaction) / measured pour height / whether the stream broke / defect type and location (cold shut, gas porosity, slag inclusion, shrinkage).
    6. Pass / rework / scrap criteria: pass = full outline, no cold shuts, no large gas porosity; rework = short fill or local cold shut, top up or remelt; scrap = large porosity or slag inclusion through a load-bearing section.
    7. Common failures and diagnosis order: (1) cold shut (two streams did not fuse) → check mold temperature first, then pour height and speed; (2) dense pinholes → mold still damp (preheat incomplete) or melt not degassed; (3) slag inclusions → the pouring cup was not skimming; (4) shrinkage concentrated in thick sections → riser too small (go to Card 17-06).
    8. L1 / L2 / L3 evidence: L1 = one acceptable casting plus defect record; L2 = five consecutive castings with defect rate comparable to process parameters; L3 = a second person casts an acceptable piece from this card alone, and both agree on "water boils off".

    17.5 Molds, Pouring, and Defect Inspection¶

    Four-major-defects troubleshooting table (ordered by frequency):

    Defect Appearance Cause Countermeasure
    Gas porosity Groups of small holes on the surface or cross-section, inner walls smooth and rounded Damp mold / blocked vents / air entrained in pouring Dry the mold thoroughly; thicken and add vents; reduce the pouring drop
    Shrinkage cavity Central void in a thick section, inner wall rough and dendritic The thick spot solidifies last and feeding is interrupted Place the gate above the thickest point; thickness-transition ratio ≤2:1; after pouring, leave extra copper in the pouring cup to feed
    Cold shut Flow marks on the surface, meeting seams, two layers of skin Copper too cold / pouring interrupted Raise tapping temperature (copper bright orange verging on white); pour in one continuous motion
    Slag inclusion Black-gray patches on the cross-section, easily flaked off Incomplete skimming / the gate washing slag into the cavity Deepen the pouring cup; skim once more before pouring

    Inspection procedure (sample every furnace):

    1. 100% visual inspection: surface porosity and cold shuts are judged directly.
    2. Tap-sound inspection (auxiliary means, with limitations like the Chapter 6 heat color — affected by piece thickness and shape, never the sole basis): a crisp, long ring = dense; a dull, short thud = internal voids or cracks. Set dull-sounding pieces aside separately.
    3. Destructive sampling: take 1 piece per furnace, clamp it in the bench vise (rope clamp), chisel it apart, and examine the cross-section: dense + rose color + fine grains = excellent; record the grade of voids, slag spots, and coarse bright large grains (overheating).
    4. All results go into the furnace-number archive: furnace number, batch recipe, blast time, defects, sample cross-section grade. This archive is the sole basis for tuning parameters (the Chapter 3 system).

    17.6 Forging, Annealing, and Work Hardening¶

    Hot forging (copper's formal shaping method): copper is most plastic between 400–700°C. Bury the copper piece in the red-hot charcoal zone and heat it until dark red is visible (about 600–700°C, empirical value, must be field-calibrated; heat-color auxiliary criterion), then bring it to the anvil and hammer fast — hot forging work-hardens slowly, so it can deform continuously without frequent annealing. When the heat color fades to dark-invisible (below about 450°C), return the piece to the fire; hammering at too-low temperature is the main cause of cracking.

    Four rules of hammering technique:

    1. Light fast blows beat heavy slow strikes (heavy blows shake slag and pores apart).
    2. Orderly progression: from one end to the other; from center to edge (when spreading).
    3. Flip often: alternate the two faces to keep one face from thinning and curling.
    4. Check against the template constantly (the Chapter 9 template method): check after each piece each pass; light gap <1 mm.

    Annealing applied to forgings: when cold-dressing complex pieces (bending, straightening, curling edges), anneal as soon as they harden (same procedure as 17.2).

    Engineering uses of work hardening: copper pieces left un-annealed after final cold forging keep their cold-worked hardness — the only strengthening means for copper chisel and knife edges. Final-forging procedure: after annealing, two final cold-forging rounds (combined thinning ≤30%), then grind the edge immediately and deliver. Cold-worked copper's edge retention is markedly better than annealed copper's, but still inferior to stone tools and far inferior to iron (17.7).

    Copper tool making specifications:

    Tool Process route Specification

    [Card 17-04 Four Rules of Hot Forging] (eight fields)

    1. Input materials: cast billet or copper bar (from 17.3); charcoal fire. Knock off surface scale first, or it gets hammered into the metal.
    2. Tools required: hammer (weight matched to the work), anvil, template (Chapter 9 template method), tongs. Calibration source: temperature by color (start at visible dull red, stop at 450 °C); dimension by template gap, light gap <1 mm.
    3. Sequence and control points (four rules): (1) light rapid blows beat heavy slow ones (heavy blows crack slag and porosity open); (2) work in sequence — from one end to the other, from center to edge when spreading; (3) turn often — alternate faces to avoid thinning one side into a curl; (4) check the template constantly — once per heat. Control points: neither the temperature window nor the template check may be skipped.
    4. Empirical parameters and calibration: hot-working window = visible dull red (600–700 °C) down to 450 °C (work it colder and it cracks); template light gap <1 mm; final cold-work total — the last two cold passes together ≤30%, beyond that it cracks. Cold work left unannealed retains work hardness, the only way to harden a copper chisel or axe edge.
    5. Observations to record: per piece — number of heats / color at each withdrawal / template gap readings / any lap cracks / final cold passes.
    6. Pass / rework / scrap criteria: pass = dimensions match the template, no laps, edge work-hardened; rework = shallow cracks from working too cold, grind out and reheat; scrap = burnt (surface blisters, coarse grain, shatters under the hammer).
    7. Common failures and diagnosis order: (1) cracks while forging → working below 450 °C; (2) surface blisters and scale → burnt, fire too hot or soak too long; (3) dimensions drifting out of tolerance → not checking the template, working by eye; (4) edge curls over → one face hammered too long without turning.
    8. L1 / L2 / L3 evidence: L1 = one forged piece matching its template plus record; L2 = one each of copper chisel, axe, and awl, all dimensionally and hardness acceptable; L3 = a second person forges a template-matching piece from this card alone, both reading the same template gap identically.

    17.7 Maintenance and Reuse of Metal Tools¶

    Copper's first property: scrap is not the end. Copper can be remelted indefinitely (each remelt loses about 2%–5% to oxidation, empirical value); old pieces, gate burrs, flash, and swarf all go back into the crucible. Establish a copper ledger: four columns — intake (newly smelted / recovered), issuance (made into pieces), in process, losses — settled monthly. This is the first material with a complete materials loop — the system starts in this chapter and carries through Chapters 18–19.

    17.8 From Soft Metal Tools to Stronger, Tougher Alloys¶

    Pure copper's ceiling: even work-hardened, it reaches only a fraction of soft steel's hardness (by relative hand-feel and wear criteria; quantitative hardness comparison awaits the Chapter 19 means). Two alloy routes break the ceiling:

    Route 1 · Tin bronze (the main route):

    • Ratio: copper 88–92 parts + tin 8–12 parts (by mass, weighed on the balance, Chapter 3). The empirical optimum window is about 90:10, must be field-calibrated (trial-cast 3 furnaces: 88:12, 90:10, 92:8; file the hardness and toughness comparison).
    • Operation: melt the copper first (crucible bright orange), then press tin pieces into the melt in batches and stir evenly — tin melts at 232°C and is added last to avoid oxidation and evaporation losses.
    • Effect: as-cast hardness about 2–3× that of as-cast pure copper (empirical value, perceptible by scratch and wear comparison); good hot-forging performance; small pouring shrinkage and good fill — castability better than pure copper; bronze is the best choice for casting.
    • Boundary: cassiterite resources are extremely unevenly distributed (16.1). Tin-poor regions cannot count on bronze for hard edges; go straight to the iron and steel route of Chapters 18–19.

    Route 2 · Arsenical copper (alternative route, highly toxic):

    • Principle: reduce arsenopyrite or arsenical copper ore in the same furnace as copper ore; the arsenic dissolves into the copper, and 3%–7% arsenic hardens it significantly (empirical value).
    • Boundary and cost (must be read aloud in full before execution): arsenic compounds are violently toxic and cumulative — at this stage, roasting arsenic-bearing ore and smelting arsenical copper are directly prohibited (a 70–100 step downwind distance is far from enough against arsenic oxides, and there is no sealed kiln, no tail-gas scrubbing, no respiratory protection). Arsenical copper is listed as unlock-conditional: dedicated site ≥500 m + sealed kiln + alkaline scrubbing + respiratory protection + urinary arsenic monitoring; until all four conditions are met, the arsenical copper line is frozen; during the bronze gap, go straight to the iron and steel route (Chapters 18–19)

    Route 3 · Leaded copper (functional alloy): adding 5%–10% lead improves casting fill and wear resistance, for future sliding parts (pre-research for Chapters 20–21 bearings). Lead does not raise hardness. Lead vapor and dust are toxic; handle at the same protection level as arsenic.

    Direction conclusion: at the end of this chapter you own the complete master method of metalworking — "meltable, castable, forgeable, recyclable, alloyable." Chapter 18 pushes the same furnace–blast–charcoal–crucible–forging system to higher temperatures, with the target changed to a more widely distributed metal with a far higher performance ceiling: iron.

    [Card 17-05 Bronze Ratio Gradient Trial] (eight fields)

    1. Input materials: pure copper (from 17.2 or 17.3), tin (source registered per 17.1). Arsenical copper is separate — 3–7% arsenic is a hard toxicity line (see 17.8); only run it with ventilation and protection.
    2. Tools required: crucible, balance (Chapter 3, for copper and tin), sample molds (several identical small bars), bend-test fixture (vise plus angle reference). Calibration source: ratios by weighing, never by eye; performance by bend angle and scar width, never by tapping and listening.
    3. Sequence and control points: melt three charges at three ratios (8%, 10%, 12% tin) → cast identical bars in the same mold → bend each through 30°–90° → measure the scar width at the bend → pick the best. Control points: all three runs under identical conditions (same mold, same temperature, same cooling), or the comparison is worthless.
    4. Empirical parameters and calibration: ratio range copper 88–92 : tin 8–12 (weigh by one consistent method; three-run gradient picks the optimum); Q grading — Q3 = bends through 30°–90° with a scar 0.2–0.5 mm wide, the target band for tool bronze; too hard (snaps) reduce tin, too soft (bends without hardening) add tin. Arsenical copper at 3–7% arsenic, hard toxicity line.
    5. Observations to record: per run — weighed copper and tin / melting temperature / pouring temperature / bend angle / measured scar width / fracture appearance.
    6. Pass / rework / scrap criteria: pass = samples reach Q3; rework = off Q3, remelt one charge one step along the gradient; scrap = brittle fracture (tin too high) or soft collapse (tin too low), remelt the whole charge.
    7. Common failures and diagnosis order: (1) snaps at the first bend → tin high, retry lower; (2) bends but stays soft → tin low; (3) two runs at the same ratio differ wildly → melt or cooling conditions differed, not the ratio; (4) feeling unwell after an arsenical trial → stop at once, ventilate, get medical help; arsine is the most dangerous thing in this process.
    8. L1 / L2 / L3 evidence: L1 = three gradient samples plus Q grading; L2 = the settlement's optimum ratio established and reproduced in one further run; L3 = a second person reproduces the optimum from this card alone, scar widths within 0.1 mm.

    17.9 Casting Tooling in Detail: Cores, Risers, and the Gating System¶

    17.4–17.5 solved "can pour it in, can judge the result"; this section solves "pour it accurately": pieces with internal cavities (axe sockets, rings, tube sleeves) need cores; pieces of uneven thickness (thin axe edge, thick axe shoulder) need risers; and both demand a gating system designed as a system.

    Cores and core boxes (making cavities)

    • Core sand: refractory clay 3 parts + river sand 6 parts + crushed charcoal fines 1 part (by volume, empirical value, must be field-calibrated: passes if a handful squeezes into a lump that does not stick to the hand and the broken cross-section shows no dry powder; too clay-rich generates gas, too sandy and edges crumble off).
    • Core box: the "mold" for the core. Simplest construction = make a solid master pattern in the shape of the cavity, sandwich it between two stone/wood slabs (Chapters 7, 16 tooling), ram core sand around it, then open the box and remove the master — you have a split core box. Brush the box cavity with water and slick it smooth, shade-dry, then kiln-dry it through together with the clay mold (water-drop-boils-instantly grade, same criterion as 17.4).
    • Core armature (against core breakage): a slender core will break under the buoyancy and impact of the molten copper. The armature is made of thoroughly charred hardwood twigs (strong enough, diggable out after pouring, generates no gas; green wood sticks are forbidden — they char, generate gas, and form voids). Keep the armature ≥3 mm below the core surface; anywhere it touches the surface becomes a gas pore after pouring.
    • Core prints and chaplets (against drift — this section's number-one silent failure: uneven wall thickness is only discovered when the casting is sawn open):
    • Core print: form a boss at each end of the core that seats into print seats left in the mold walls. Print length ≥1/3 of the core's unsupported span and ≥10 mm (empirical value, must be field-calibrated: measure by sectioning a wall-check ring; if opposing wall thickness differs by >1.5 mm, lengthen the prints and retry).
    • Chaplet: a cantilevered core (print at one end only, like the core of a cup-shaped piece) must have chaplets — small copper nails or pads, welded into the piece by the copper after pouring. Copper chaplets Ø3–5 mm, 1 per 30×30 mm of core face (empirical value). Chaplet material the same alloy as the casting, surface scraped bright and rust-free — a dirty chaplet welds poorly and becomes a future through-leak point.
    • Verification (the means of catching silent failures): for every new core, first cast a wall-check ring — a ring-shaped test piece for wall thickness only; saw it open and measure wall thickness on four sides; only when the spread is ≤1.5 mm may real pieces be poured. Wall-check ring records go into the furnace-number archive.

    Riser (feeding) — three principles

    1. Place it where solidification ends last: copper shrinks about 4%–6% on solidifying (by volume, empirical value, must be field-calibrated: with the same stone mold, measure the mold cavity volume first, then the copper ingot volume; the difference is the shrinkage). Thick spots cool slowly and solidify last, so the shrinkage cavity necessarily lands in the thick spot — the riser must sit directly above or tight against the thick spot, drawing the shrinkage into itself.
    2. Riser size rules of thumb: an open riser's diameter ≥1.2–1.5× the local hot-spot thickness, riser height ≥1.5× its diameter (empirical value, must be field-calibrated: section the riser at its root — shrinkage contained entirely in the riser's upper half with a dense root is the minimum pass; any void at the root means go one size up and retry). The riser must solidify later than the thick spot it feeds; a riser smaller than the hot spot freezes first and itself becomes a shrinkage source.
    3. Feed pours: 20–40 s after filling, feed hot copper into the riser 1–2 times (empirical value; calibration method: watch the riser's liquid surface — feed the moment a dark film first forms; once the film thickens and blackens it is too late, and what you feed in is cold copper).

    Gating system — three zones (decreasing cross-section)

    Zone Function Specification points
    Pouring cup Receives the copper, skims slag, buffers Mouth ≥2× the sprue, depth ≥30 mm; a dry wood chip may float on it to block the first slag (must be dry; a damp chip generates gas)
    Sprue Delivers metal vertically Conical, wider at top, narrower at bottom; the bottom end is the reference minimum cross-section S; S = 80–120 mm² per kg of copper (about Ø10–12 mm, empirical value, must be field-calibrated: molds not filling or cold shuts → increase; sand wash, excessive flash → decrease)
    Ingates Into the cavity Total cross-section 0.6–0.8 S; located at the thickest part of the piece (doubling as the feeding path); round over the junction with the cavity

    Why decreasing: with a large cup, slag floats in the cup; with the cross-section decreasing from sprue to ingate, the runners stay full of descending copper, slag does not flow back, and gas entrainment is low. After cutting all three zones, check each one against the templates (Chapter 9) — a gate cut too small leaves the mold unfilled, too large washes sand and carries slag; there is no "close enough."

    ◎ Ingate placement hand-feel: on pieces with thick-thin transitions, cut the ingate on the thick side so the copper advances along the wall from thick toward thin — steady fill, fewer vortices. Practice method: first cut 10 trial sets in a dry sand box, pour water, and observe scour pits and swirl marks; 10 sets build the "orientation–fill" intuition. Misjudgment compensation: before pouring a real piece, first pour one test piece of the same wall thickness and section it per the 17.5 inspection procedure to check swirl marks and slag inclusions; only when the test piece passes may the real piece be poured.

    17.10 Copper Alloy Lineage Expansion¶

    17.8 gave the ratios of three routes; this section turns alloy research into a system of "comparable test pieces, accumulable results" (tin bronze), and draws the boundaries of three register-only, not-unlocked directions (brass, cupronickel, lead-tin solder).

    Tin bronze Q-grade test piece system (hardness/ductility comparison ladder)

    Motivation: the 17.8 three-furnace gradient (88:12, 90:10, 92:8) compares quality by hand-feel — change the person, change the conclusion. Replace hand-feel with test pieces and grades.

    • Standard test piece: each furnace casts 3 stepped bars along with the heat, each bar 80×15×4 mm (about 40 g, recheck on the balance ±2 g), cast in the same mold in the same furnace, furnace number engraved on the bar face.
    • Two observable tests:
    • Bend angle (ductility grade): clamp one end of the bar in the rope clamp (Chapter 9), bend it at constant speed around a Ø20 mm round rod, and record the bend angle at which the first visible crack appears. >90° = high ductility; 30°–90° = medium; <30° = brittle.
    • Constant-load scratch (hardness grade): mount a standard copper edge at one end of a lever, hang a 500 g weight on the other side (Chapter 3 weights), drag the tip at constant speed 20 mm across the bar, and read the scratch width against the standard rod. <0.2 mm = fine; 0.2–0.5 mm = medium; >0.5 mm = coarse.
    • Q ladder (relative calibration at this stage; absolute hardness is not promised before quantitative means exist):
    Q grade Bend angle Scratch width Interpretation
    Q1 >90° Fine Too soft (near pure copper); only for ductile pieces
    Q2 >90° Medium Soft; vessels and decoration
    Q3 30°–90° Medium Target grade for tools (chisels, axes, awls)
    Q4 30°–90° Fine Hard and tough; first choice for edges
    Q5 <30° Not counted Brittle; only for pieces that take no impact
    • Testing system: test all 3 bars and take the mode; for furnaces scoring Q3 or above, transcribe their ratio into the ledger as the "working ratio for this ore and this charcoal"; if the 3 bars of one furnace span 2 or more grades, retest once (usually uneven bending speed).
    • Calibration of the Q ladder itself: alongside every batch of test pieces, retest an accepted pure copper ingot (the Q1 anchor) and an accepted bronze chisel (the Q3 anchor) on the same stand under the same load; if an anchor crosses a grade, retest the whole set — the testing means itself must also be tested.

    Brass (copper-zinc) — register only, do not unlock

    • Direction: heat calamine/smithsonite (a zinc carbonate ore) together with copper in one furnace; zinc enters as vapor and dissolves into the copper surface, forming brass (the historical cementation route).
    • Hard reasons not to unlock: zinc boils at about 907 °C, and the temperature needed to reduce zinc ore is exactly high enough to drive the zinc off as vapor — copper, tin, and lead stay in the crucible after reduction, while zinc, once reduced, escapes; this is its essential difference from the other meltable metals. With neither a sealed crucible cover nor any zinc-vapor condensation means available, forcing it = ore wasted + zinc-vapor poisoning, a double loss.
    • Registration action: number and file the ore sample (origin, appearance, streak), and write one line in the ore-site ledger (Chapter 16 format): "cementation brass: to be tried after a sealed furnace and condensation tooling are on file." Trial forbidden until both items of tooling are on record.

    Cupronickel (copper-nickel) — register the source: some copper ores carry nickel; the ingots come out white-colored, hard and tough, distinguishable at a glance from rose-red copper ingots. When encountered: separate furnace, separate accounts, engrave a "W" mark on the ingot face, and do not merge it into the copper ledger (different properties, different uses). Nickel ores often carry arsenic; apply the 17.8 arsenic-level protection throughout.

    Lead-tin solder — register + small-sample trial soldering

    • Ratio: tin 2 : lead 1 (by mass, empirical value, must be field-calibrated: melt the solder onto a copper sheet; after cooling, it passes if hammer-bending does not lift the skin and the cross-section is bright; if brittle, add tin; if it runs without beading, add lead). Melting range about 180–250 °C (empirical value).
    • Small-sample procedure: scrape the copper joint surfaces bright → heat until a faint red first appears in the dark, then stop (about 500–550 °C — this grade is the physical floor for faint red visible in the dark; tin at 232 °C shows no red in the dark; same standard as Chapter 23 tinning) → dot with rosin → touch the solder stick to the joint and wipe it along; the hot copper sucking the solder into the joint means it has taken.
    • Boundary: handle lead toxicity at the 17.8 route protection level; soldered pieces are forbidden from the food line (the food line uses pure tin, see 17.12); register only the low-temperature brazing direction for copper-piece seams and leak repair.

    17.11 Copper Sheet Forging and Structure¶

    17.6 handles shaping; this section handles "how well it is shaped" — the larger and thinner the sheet, the more visible directionality and uniformity problems become.

    Forging direction and fiber flow lines (cross-reversal cold forging)

    • Phenomenon: copper sheet hammered repeatedly in one direction has internal grains stretched along the blow direction into "fiber flow lines"; the sheet becomes soft along the grain and cracks across it — bends open along the lines, like firewood splitting along the grain.
    • Cross-reversal procedure: after each round of forging, rotate the sheet 90° before the next round; spread widthwise, lengthen lengthwise, alternating. No more than 2 consecutive rounds in one direction (empirical value, must be field-calibrated: take one sheet forged 3 rounds unidirectional and one forged 3 rounds crosswise, and test bend angles by the 17.10 method — if the unidirectional piece's bend angle is 30° or more lower, the benefit of reversal is confirmed; record it in the forgeability file).
    • Visual criterion: tilt the forged sheet against the light; over-forged unidirectional sheet shows parallel fine-streak bands; a correctly reversed sheet shows crisscrossing streaks that do not group.

    Annealing homogenization (charcoal-burial method)

    • Problem: a sheet propped over the fire reaches temperature on the fire-facing side while the back is still cold — local overheating (grains coarsen and go mushy) and local under-annealing (still hard) coexist in one sheet; the next hammer blow must crack it, and the crack path is completely unpredictable.
    • Burial procedure: spread charred-through red charcoal across the hearth to a depth ≥3× the sheet thickness; lay the sheet flat inside and cover with ≥2× thickness more charcoal, so it heats at the same temperature from all sides. Estimate the hold at 5 min per 3 mm of sheet thickness (empirical value, must be field-calibrated: pull one piece, quench it, section it, polish it on the grinding table, then cold-forge its edge — if the center cracks while the surface is already soft, it is under-soaked; extend the hold 30% and recalibrate).
    • Exit action: pull the whole sheet out with the rope clamp, and immerse the entire sheet in water at one instant (the 17.2 Iron Law: copper into water is annealing; speed does not affect softening, only uniformity — immersing in batches guarantees one end cools first).

    Sheet flattening (wooden mallet + point-pressure on a flat plate)

    • Principle: a warped sheet = the fibers on one side are shorter than on the other. Beating with a flat hammer face only thins the high spots and cups it worse; the correct approach is point-pressure stretching of the short side, lengthening it to match the long side.
    • Procedure:
    • Level a large stone slab with fine sandstone (Chapter 9 tooling) and set it flat; place the sheet on it and press each corner in turn — the side that rocks is the short side.
    • Short side up; with a hardwood mallet (head 300–500 g, made per Chapter 7), tap dense rows of light points along the short side: point spacing ≈ half the mallet head diameter, force calibrated so the sheet shows shallow round dents.
    • After each row, press-test and re-check; stop when the rocking disappears. Over-correcting (reverse cupping) is far harder to fix than under-correcting — rather three rounds than one greedy pass for flatness.
    • Precision flattening: sandwich the sheet between two flat plates with a ≥10 kg weight on top overnight.
    • Acceptance criterion: laid naturally on the stone slab, each corner pressed in turn, a feeler check with the standard rod shows warp ≤1 mm. ◎ Point-pressure force hand-feel: stop as soon as a dent appears; dent depth is judged by a fingernail scraped crosswise not catching (if the nail catches, it is already past the 0.3 mm class — too heavy). Practice method: 10 scrap copper pieces, one row of points per piece, then measure sheet shape and dent depth; 10 pieces build a "force–deformation" comparison. Misjudgment compensation: always finish by feeler-checking all four corners with the standard rod for warp ≤1 mm; hand-feel only decides whether the next point is struck, never whether it passes.

    17.12 Copper Surface Treatment¶

    Three tasks, ordered by purpose: coloring (identification and decoration), polishing (precision and hand-feel), tinning (the food-safety line).

    Coloring (registered processes, limited to two methods)

    • Sulfide coloring (black-brown film): make a paste of sulfur powder (the frost-like deposit collected from the walls of the sulfide-ore roasting shed and kiln, Chapter 16 site) and water; coat it thinly on clean copper and keep warm 10–30 min to form a deep brown to black copper sulfide film; rinse with clean water and buff the raised surfaces with a soft cloth — "black ground, bright lines." The color grade is controlled by time (empirical value, must be field-calibrated: coat three zones of one copper sheet for 5/15/30 min and compare to set the grades; pin the color samples into the ledger).
    • Oxide coloring (heat color): the decorative use of the 17.2 annealing oxide colors (purple → blue → gray). Bake clean pieces at low temperature with dark-invisible-red as the ceiling (heat-color auxiliary criterion, Chapter 6); when the color arrives, quench in water to fix it. Test pieces first for every batch, then the real pieces.
    • Boundary: color films add friction and impede heat transfer — edges, friction surfaces, and tin-plated surfaces are forbidden to color; coloring is only for decorative and marking pieces (coloring batch-numbered pieces aids identification; color samples and durations go into the ledger).

    Polishing (three abrasive grades + leather wheel)

    • Three abrasive grades (panning-graded, same as Chapter 16): coarse quartz sand (20–40 mesh class) removes forging marks and oxide scale; medium river sand (40–80 mesh class) removes coarse scratches; fine talc powder or fine brick/tile powder removes medium scratches down to a mirror.
    • Progression Iron Law: never skip a grade. Do not move to the next grade before the previous grade's scratches are gone — fine abrasive packs into coarse scratches, polishing into a "pitted mirror," and rework must start over from the coarse grade. Each time you change grades, rotate the scratch direction 90°; whether the scratch direction changes at each grade is the self-check line for grade-skipping.
    • Leather wheel: leather strips wrapped on a wooden wheel (Chapter 7 woodworking), driven by the bow drill or a wheel stand (Chapter 9), with wet abrasive fed in intermittently. Limit the speed to where abrasive is not flung off (empirical value, must be field-calibrated: seeing abrasive spray lines means drop one speed step).
    • Acceptance criterion: against the light, no directional scratches and able to reflect the outline of a hand shadow; polish the internal curves of vessels by hand with a soft cloth and fine powder.

    Hot tinning (food-line application)

    • Why tin: copper meeting food acids (fruit acids, vinegar, yogurt) dissolves into copper salts — blue color, astringent taste, emetic toxicity; boiling acidic food directly in copper vessels is a clear poisoning path. Tin is inert to food acids and melts at only 232 °C; a hot-tinned layer of 0.1–0.3 mm (empirical value) replaces the entire food-contact surface with tin.
    • Pre-tinning cleaning (coating adhesion depends entirely on this step): polish on the grinding table → dilute food-vinegar wash to remove oxides → rinse clean with water → dry completely (any residual water drop will blister the coat).
    • Tinning procedure: melt tin in a shallow clay pot (232 °C, no red visible in the dark; a mirror-calm tin surface is the readiness criterion); cover the melt surface with a layer of rosin powder to exclude air and dross; preheat the copper vessel to 150–200 °C (hot-to-the-touch class), dip it into the tin for 5–15 s, withdraw and, while hot, rub it even with a dry hemp cloth roll — what you rub out is a thin layer; thick spots run and bead.
    • Food-line system: any copper vessel touching food (pots, spoons, bowls) may enter the food line only after its inner surface is hot-tinned and passes light-transmission inspection; thereafter re-inspect by transmitted light once a season — look at the inside against the light; a bright, uniform tin surface is acceptable; copper-red spots (exposed copper) mean back to the crucible to melt off the old tin and re-tin (old tin fully recovered; add a "tin" column to the 17.7 copper ledger, settled monthly the same way).

    1. Input materials: copper piece (degreased and deoxidized — tin will not take on an oily surface); tin (melts at 232 °C).
    2. Tools required: tin crucible, tongs, balance (to weigh the gain and back-calculate coating), skimming tool. Calibration source: coating thickness is not measured in microns — it is back-calculated from weight gain over a known area.
    3. Sequence and control points: degrease and deoxidize → preheat → dip into the tin bath → withdraw and drain → weigh. Control points: bath at 232 °C with a mirror-still surface — dross means the bath is oxidizing from excess heat; skim before dipping.
    4. Empirical parameters and calibration: coating target 0.1–0.3 mm (calibrated by weighing); tin bath 232 °C; dip only as long as needed for full coverage — longer only builds thickness that interferes with fit.
    5. Observations to record: per piece — weight gain / back-calculated coating / surface uniformity / bare copper spots / bath surface condition.
    6. Pass / rework / scrap criteria: pass = even coating 0.1–0.3 mm with no bare copper; rework = local misses, redip after checking that spot for oil; scrap = coating too thick to assemble (melt the tin back off).
    7. Common failures and diagnosis order: (1) tin will not take at all → oil or oxide film, reclean; (2) patches refuse to take → fingerprints or local oxidation; (3) uneven thickness → bath too cool, poor flow; (4) surface dulls and drosses → bath too hot, cool and skim.
    8. L1 / L2 / L3 evidence: L1 = one acceptable tinned piece plus weight record; L2 = five consecutive pieces with coatings inside 0.1–0.3 mm; L3 = a second person tins to the same band from this card alone, back-calculated thickness within 0.05 mm.

    C. Unresolved Dependencies¶

    • No iron tongs, anvil, or hammer: holding relies on carrying poles and the clay ladle; the anvil and hammer are bounded by stone's limits — heavy forging and volume production wait for the Chapter 18 iron bootstrap.
    • No quantitative means for tin, arsenic, and lead content: alloy ratios are inferred from charge ratios and property test pieces; composition analysis awaits Chapters 23, 26.
    • Copper ingot consistency is limited (per-furnace composition and slag content fluctuate): before interchangeability design (Chapter 22 standard parts), all copper pieces are one-piece fitted.
    • The copper-melting furnace's temperature ceiling constrains pouring quality: furnace evolution toward greater blast and higher temperature is solved together in 18.3–18.4.

    Safety Boundaries¶

    • CO poisoning: all furnace smelting operations outdoors or in a forced-ventilation shed (Chapter 12 flue procedure), never in a closed room; blast in shifts, one person ≤30 minutes; headache or nausea means evacuate immediately (the Chapter 6 iron rule).
    • Molten metal meeting water = explosion (this chapter's highest-level hazard): the ground within a 3 m radius of the pouring zone dry, no puddles, no snow or ice; all tools, molds, and wooden sticks dry and preheated; never hold a sweating hand above an open crucible; clear the carrying route of obstacles and do one dry run.
    • Burn treatment: cold-water rinse ≥15 minutes (the Chapter 6 procedure); if molten copper splashes into clothing, remove the clothing immediately — never rinse first and undress after, in that inverted order.
    • Crucible failure plan: for every furnace, prepare a ≥1 m² dry sand/soil zone; if the crucible breaks, let the copper flow into the sand — never pour water on it.
    • Arsenic and lead work protection: see 17.8; when protective conditions are lacking, these two alloy routes are shut down entirely.
    • Sulfide ore roasting flue gas (SO₂): work crosswind; withdraw at the first irritating cough.
    • Leather apron, leggings, and gloves are mandatory for pouring; synthetic fibers do not exist, but wet leather is replaced immediately (wet leather conducts heat fast and causes steam burns).

    Acceptance Criteria¶

    • L1 proof of principle: one full native-copper cold-forging–annealing cycle (thinning ≥50% with no through-crack); one furnace of malachite yielding copper (≥100 g), fracture rose-red and dense.
    • L2 repeatable manufacture: copper recovery ≥35% over 3 consecutive furnaces (kg copper per kg concentrate, empirical value); one furnace of ingots + one furnace of clay-mold pieces all pass 17.5 sampling (cross-section grade acceptable); 3 copper tools (one chisel, one axe, one awl) pass functional tests — the copper chisel cuts a 10 cm groove in limestone without edge curling or chipping; the copper axe chops soft wood 50 times with no edge cracking; the cold-work-hardened sample scratches wood deeper than the same-shaped annealed sample.
    • L3 sustainable production: copper ledger running ≥1 month (all four columns present, monthly settlement balances); charcoal reserve and consumption rate on the books (charcoal per kg of copper stable); crucibles and molds have spares with known wear rates; ≥1 person independently passes the full-process reproduction assessment (the Chapter 3 process card journeyman criterion); maintenance records (the 17.7 grading table) continuously on file.
