### 25.1 Thermometers and the temperature reference

**The first step into quantitative thermometry is making fine glass tubing** — the challenge item left over from Chapter 14, completed in this section (it is also a prerequisite for Chapter 26's chemical glassware).

**The tube-drawing challenge (mandrel method; take one of two routes)**:

- Route A (acid available, Chapter 23): dip a fine iron wire mandrel (0.5–2 mm diameter, straightened after quenching, sanded smooth) into molten glass and draw it out horizontally at uniform speed as a cored glass filament; anneal; soak in dilute acid to dissolve away the iron core, rinse, and you have a tube. Bore = mandrel diameter.
- Route B (no acid): bring two glass rods together and melt them as one; when pulled apart into a "V", the root leaves a slit forming a tube — low yield, unstable bore, emergency use only (boundary: such tubes are fit for manometers only, not thermometers).
- **Bending tube**: attach a short leather hose (Chapter 15 leather stitching) to one end and hold it in the mouth; heat the tube body over the lamp flame, rotating as it softens; bend gently into a U or a right angle, blowing lightly throughout to keep the bore open. Drill tube bending in batches, building stock for Chapter 26's condenser coils.
- Inspection: fill the tube with water and check that the column runs continuous with no blockage; hold it to the light and check the inner wall for large collapses. Classify bores by "how high the water column rises in the tube" — the finer the bore, the higher it rises (recorded as a phenomenon; quantitative capillary law deferred), and box tubes of the same class together.

**The alcohol thermometer (full process)**:

1. Bulb: flame-seal one end of a short, thick tube section (bore 3–5 mm) into a thick bottom — the reservoir bulb.
2. Join: heat the capillary and the bulb tube's open end together until soft; butt-join, rotate and press to fuse; cure upright and anneal (Chapter 14 ash pit).
3. Fill: first charge the bulb with dyed alcohol (tinted with carbon black or cinnabar, Chapter 2); heat the bulb to drive off air, cool so the liquid climbs into the capillary; repeat until the column reaches the tube's top section.
4. Seal: leave an expansion cavity at the tube top, then flame-seal.
5. Aging screening: keep finished units somewhere warm for one week; cull any whose liquid column breaks or whose bulb cracks.

**Temperature scale and calibration (the hundred-degree-scale charter)**:

- Fixed point 1 (zero point): an ice–water slush bucket — crushed ice and water coexisting, stirred; insert the thermometer until the bulb is fully submerged; wait for the column to hold steady for 10 swings (about half a minute) and read. **Calibrate three times each with different water sources and different ice batches** — the column positions should coincide; if they do not, the fixed-point conditions are wrong, and force-averaging is not permitted (this is 25.7's first lesson).
- Fixed point 2 (ethanol boiling point, 78 ℃): calibrate with the bulb immersed in **boiling water**. **Honest declaration: at 100 ℃ the ethanol vapor pressure inside a sealed alcohol thermometer is ≈2 atm; it will swell, crack, and read false — this instrument's calibrated range is 0–78 ℃; for judging temperature in 100 ℃-class hot processes, use the slump-cone set/sample method (Chapter 12) and the thermocouple (Chapter 27, unusable until unlocked)**.
- **Calibration-site altitude note**: boiling points are lower in the mountains than on the plains; the hundred-degree point must state its calibration site, noted as "this thermometer's hundred-degree = the boiling-water temperature at the calibration site". Settlements exchanging thermometers also exchange boiling-point correction data.
- Graduation: between the zero line and the hundred-degree line, graduate by the **diagonal-line equal-division method** (Chapter 3 rule scribing + proportional construction: take 100 parts on the main axis — bisect down to 25 first, then step one 5-part interval at a time with a compass); the graduated plate is scribed and mounted alongside the tube. Each division = 1 degree; readings are estimated to half a division with the magnifier (Chapter 24).

**Precision boundary (honest declaration)**: alcohol column position reads ±half a division (±0.5 degrees); two same-class thermometers reading within ≤1 division of each other at the same temperature passes (L2 criterion); uneven capillary bore is the main error source — draw two tubes from each batch for a full-range comparison (three points: ice, boiling water, room temperature); out-of-tolerance batches are downgraded to temperature-zone indication only. Ceiling: graduation to half a degree is already the limit of mandrel-drawn tube; anything finer needs the drawn-blowpipe craft (a Chapter 14 leftover; a next-step dependency). Mercury thermometer (if cinnabar ore is registered): deferred — its preparation under unknown mercury-vapor toxicity is listed as a forbidden item (safety boundary).

**Temperature reference standards**: two standard thermometers (cross-checked against the sub-standard; a transplant of the Chapter 3 three-rod method); seal one away as the reference; working instruments are rechecked against the reference at two points each quarter, with drift logged.

8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one tube that passes the water test with its bore matching the mandrel; L2 = three consecutive drawing batches with same-class tubes boxed together and good-tube counts registered; L3 = a second person draws a same-class tube from this card alone with matching water and transmitted-light results, and stock is built for Chapter 26 condenser coils.

### 25.2 Balances and mass measurement

**The Chapter 3 balance's precision ceiling** is the pivot: the friction and sway of the rope-hung beam eat the tiny torques. The upgrade = **the knife-edge bearing**.

**Key points for making the knife-edge balance**:

1. Beam: a hardwood beam (or a wrought-iron beam), about one cubit long, with a hardened steel knife edge set into the underside at dead center (Chapter 19; edge line perpendicular to the beam axis, ground straight).
2. Supports: two ground-flat hardened steel anvils (Chapter 20 scraped surfaces) carry the knife edge; the anvil seats are fixed to the upright column.
3. Pans: each beam end hangs a knife edge or fine cords suspending a pan. **Equal-arm verification**: swapping the two pans' loads should still balance — unequal arms are the balance's number-one systematic error, and the swap method is its calibration test (record the correction).
4. Pointer and scale: a vertical pointer at the beam's center, a horizontal scale on the column (equal-division rulings, diagonal-line method); read the pointer's slight deflection against the scale.
5. Sensitivity adjustment: a trim weight on the beam (the center-of-gravity nut) moved up = more sensitive, slower swing; down, the reverse. Adjust until **unloaded, adding the smallest weight (a half-lamina) deflects the pointer 2 or more divisions** — that passes (empirical, must be field-calibrated).

**Oscillation reading method (do not wait for rest; read the swing)**: the beam has friction, so the point where it settles is not necessarily the balance point. Record three consecutive turning extremes of the pointer — left, right, left — as a₁, b, a₂; the rest point ≈ the midpoint of (a₁+a₂)/2 and b. This method turns the randomness of "wait for it to stop" into the stability of "take the swing's center" — classic and necessary; write it into the operating procedure.

**Readability and precision grading**:

- **Readability** = the mass that deflects the pointer 1 division (measured with the smallest lamina weight). Target: one ten-thousandth of the unit stone (empirical, must be field-calibrated; if unreachable, register the honest downgrade).
- Weighing procedure: object on the left pan, weights on the right; add weights from large to small; read three swings and take the midpoint; **the mass weighed must never exceed the balance's rated capacity** (overload dents the knife edge = permanent loss of precision).
- Small weight set: cut from tin/copper sheet; calibrate downward step by step from the unit stone along the binary balancing chain (3.5); engrave the marks and enter them in the archive. **Weights may only be handled with tweezers/tongs** — hand sweat and corrosion are their great enemies.

**Maintenance system**: wipe the knife edge and anvil faces with a dry soft cloth before each day's use; when not weighing, the beam must be lifted off (knife edge clear of the anvils); monthly mutual checks of weights, annual recheck against the reference chain; any weight that has been knocked about is withdrawn immediately and sent for inspection. Balance table: a stone-slab top, independent of the other workbenches, away from doorway drafts (echoes the 25.8 layout).

8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one knife-edge balance passing sensitivity and equal-arm verification with the certificate on file; L2 = readability on target (or downgraded and registered) with a monthly check instituted and a full month of three-swing weighing records; L3 = the weight chain rechecked against the reference annually, balance and weights under sealed custody, and a second person reproducing the verification from this card with matching results.

### 25.3 Precision measurement of length, angle, and time

**Length: from "comparison rod" to "micrometric reading"**:

- The Chapter 3 standard rod + the Chapter 20 gauges solve "comparison"; intermediate dimensions (remainders short of the smallest division) rely on two new tools:
- **Screw micrometer**: a lead screw (Chapter 20; pitch = 1 whole-division unit) drives the measuring spindle, with a graduated dial on its end. Fifty divisions around the dial (equal division of the circle: compass chord stepping + division-by-division verification); each division = pitch/50. The crux of making it: pitch uniformity decides everything — after the screw and nut are mutually lapped (Chapter 20 thread system), verify with **the spindle's full travel checked against the standard rod's whole divisions**, recording per-division errors in the archive.
- **Wedge micro-scale (fallback, when no good lead screw exists)**: a 1:50 taper wedge advances, and the scale on the slope reads magnified 50x. Precision below the screw; use it for rough field measurement.
- Measurement procedure: constant measuring force (after contact, rotate until it slips, then stop); repeat 5 times, take the median, record the range; temperature influence declared — metal expands with heat and contracts with cold; cross-season comparisons are recorded on the 20-degree scale (the thermometer feeds back into the length reference for the first time; the two interlock).

**Angle: chord conversion**:

- Graduating the circumference of a circular dial (wooden/copper disc): step off chords with the compass, then verify by bisection, making an "angle rule"; small angles convert directly by the distance ratio: tanθ ≈ chord/radius (for small θ); compile your own small-angle conversion table (one row per half degree) and file it.
- Right angles still use the Chapter 20 triple mutual-check method as the reference; leveling relies mainly on the water-surface reference (3.3), with the **bubble level vial listed as a challenge item** (grinding the concave inner wall of a bent tube is hard; boundary: for now, level with the water surface of a long trough — inefficient but reliable).

**Time: the seconds-pendulum timer (this chapter's biggest single-point upgrade)**:

- Principle verification (a mandatory experiment): same pendulum length, different amplitudes (under 1/10 arc) — the number of swings in a timed interval should be identical; same amplitude, different lengths — the period changes with length: length to 1/4, period to 1/2.
- **The seconds pendulum**: adjust the length until each half-swing (one tick) interval matches the water clock's graduated segment; take the length at about the 1-meter order (empirical, must be field-calibrated: after the two methods have mutually calibrated each other, register the local seconds-pendulum length in the charter — it differs slightly with altitude/latitude, so "the local seconds-pendulum length" is itself a definition that belongs in the unit charter).
- Timer construction: a rigid stand + a slender pendulum (metal bob, fine cord/thin rod); below, a ratchet or bell keeps count; check against the water clock every 100 half-swings, logging drift.
- Usage boundary: keep the amplitude under 1/10 arc (beyond it the period grows with amplitude, and the fast clock becomes a slow clock — stated honestly: the Chapter 3 water clock's defect reappears here symmetrically); temperature change stretches and shrinks the metal rod — fast in winter, slow in summer (the rod lengthens in summer, the period lengthens, the clock slows — record it as a known systematic error; a compensating grid rod is listed as a next-step dependency); after any move, recalibrate.

### 25.4 Measuring pressure, flow, and liquid level

**The U-tube manometer (the first instrument of pressure quantification)**:

- Structure: a U-shaped glass tube (the 25.1 tube-bending skill) fixed to a graduated board and filled with clean water (dyed); with both ends open, the two columns stand level = the zero position.
- Usage: one end connects to what is measured (furnace blast pressure, pipe gas, a deep-water outlet), the other stays open. Read **the height difference h between the two columns**, pressure reckoned by water-column height (1 cubit of water column — its definition is self-evident; cross-unit conversion goes into the charter). The graduated board is ruled by bisecting the standard rod; read to an estimated half division with the magnifier.
- Boundary: the water column is sensitive in the low-pressure region and awkward in the high-pressure region (the tube gets too long; a 1-rod water column ≈ the blower's ceiling region; for higher pressures, "smaller bore + heavier liquid" is the next step — heavier liquids such as concentrated brine/mercury are a later story; declared honestly: only the low-pressure region is covered for now).
- Liquid level: the **communicating-vessel water gauge** — connect transparent glass tubing to upper and lower ports of the vessel (water tank, evaporation pan); the water level in the tube is the vessel's level. A principle prototype of the boiler water gauge, serving the Chapter 12 furnace and Chapter 28 steam directly. Sounding: measure directly with a graduated rod; remote reading = float + pulley pointer.

**Flow (volume over time as the reference method)**:

- Reference method: the outflow fills a standard measure/standard barrel, timed by the seconds pendulum; flow = barrels/time. Every flow device is calibrated by this method first.
- Efflux velocity law (empirical, field-calibrated): outflow from a side port under head h speeds up with √h — run range/timing experiments at different depths to build this device's "h–flow" lookup table. **Interpolation within the table only; extrapolation forbidden**.
- Open-channel float method (for comparison): time a float over a fixed distance = surface velocity; surface ≠ mean, so back-calibrate a correction factor with the volumetric method (one factor per channel; empirical, must be field-calibrated).

**This section's outputs**: the U-tube manometer, the water gauge, the flow calibration procedure, and the lookup table. **Grade: L2** (two-fixed-point calibration and table interpolation are both institutionally repeatable).

### 25.5 How to measure motion and mechanical work

**Power reference: the lift method (the origin of all power measurement)**

Power = work per unit time. The least disputable work is "lifting a known weight through a known height":

1. The weight: standard weights or stones with registered masses (weighed on the balance, 25.2);
2. The lift: raised through h (measured with the standard rod) over a fixed pulley (Chapter 21);
3. The timing: the seconds pendulum (25.3);
4. Power = mass × height / time. Hand cranking, animal power, waterwheels — all first establish their baseline by this method — **power measured by the lift method is numbered and archived; it is the whole settlement's power reference**.

**Brake dynamometry (measuring the output of rotating machinery)**

Principle: apply a friction brake of known force to the rotating shaft; friction force × moment arm = torque; torque × rotational speed = power.

1. The brake: a wooden brake block (Chapter 7) clamping the rim of a belt pulley or a dedicated brake wheel, the brake rope wrapped once around it;
2. Arm and force measurement: the rope's free end runs over a fixed pulley to a hanging weight pan — at equilibrium the pan's weight = the frictional tangential force; or a rigid arm's end presses on the balance pan for a direct read;
3. Speed: fit a lug at the shaft end that trips a click once per revolution; count with the seconds pendulum (read the revolutions every 100 half-swings);
4. Output power = 2π × rotational speed (rev/s) × torque.
5. Equilibrium criterion: if speed visibly sags under load, the reading is "power at the reduced speed", not "rated power" — load in steps and record the "power–speed" curve; only the plateau of the curve is usable output (empirical procedure, must be field-calibrated).

**Cautions**: the brake block heats up (all the friction power becomes heat) — limit test runs to the 1-minute order, and stop the moment the wood chars; measure idle loss once before and once after each dynamometry run; output = gross work − idle loss.

[Card 25-04 Lift-Method Power Reference] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: a weight of known mass (standard weights, or a stone weighed on the balance and registered); fine cord; a fixed pulley (Chapter 21).
2. **Required tools**: standard rod (to measure the lift height h), seconds-pendulum timer (25.3), balance (to weigh the load), stand and hook, clearing markers (no one stands below).
3. **Sequence and control points**: weigh the load → reeve the cord over the fixed pulley → mark the start and end heights (h measured with the standard rod) → time the whole lift with the seconds pendulum → compute power = mass × height / time → repeat three or more times, report the median and the range. Control points: a uniform lift (an uneven pull makes the timing meaningless); one person judges both start and stop; **clear the area below** (falling weights).
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: P = mass × height ÷ time; the 25.11 worked example: mass 10.0 unit stones (±0.5%, pulley friction included), height 3.0 m (±0.5%), time 20 half-swings (start/stop ±1 half-swing = ±5%), so P = 10.0×3.0÷20 = 1.5 unit-stone·metres per half-swing, reported as "1.5 (±6%)"; **stretching the count from 20 half-swings to 100 dilutes the timing term to ±1% and drops the total error to about 2%** — no new instrument, only a longer measurement. Calibration: weigh the same weight once hung straight on the balance and once through the pulley block; the difference is the friction allowance, logged rig by rig.
5. **In-process observation items**: per run record load mass and weighing record number / lift height / half-swing count and who judged start and stop / cord and pulley condition / three or more readings and their range / the computation and its error annotation.
6. **Pass/rework/scrap criteria**: pass = three or more independent measurements, median and range on file, result carrying an error annotation and numbered as the settlement's power reference; rework = range too wide — first lengthen the timing (20 → 100 half-swings), then check that the lift was uniform and the cord did not slip; scrap = a dropped load, a deformed stand or a seized pulley — take the rig out of service and rebuild; that run's data are void.
7. **Common failures and troubleshooting order**: ① large timing error (the most frequent, and the largest term in the error account) → lengthen the count to 100 half-swings and have one person judge both ends; ② three readings scattered → uneven lift or the cord jumping the groove; pull evenly and dress the cord; ③ result systematically high → pulley friction not deducted; correct by the difference between the straight-hung and through-pulley weighings; ④ load swinging into the frame → add a guide or shorten the cord and remeasure; ⑤ stand sinking → reinforce the base and re-mark the height.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one power measurement each for human and animal power, both with error annotations; L2 = three or more measurements on the same rig with a stable range, the power numbered and filed as the comparison reference; L3 = the power reference under sealed custody and periodically re-measured, with waterwheel/animal/human output all traced back to this method.

[Card 25-05 Brake Dynamometry] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: wooden brake blocks (Chapter 7, replaceable consumables); brake rope (wear-resistant, wrapped once around the wheel); a belt pulley or a dedicated brake wheel; weights and a weight pan; the rotating shaft under test (waterwheel, animal wheel, etc.).
2. **Required tools**: fixed pulley (to lead off the brake rope), rigid arm (alternative direct read), shaft-end lug and clicker (one tick per revolution), seconds-pendulum timer (25.3), balance (weights), water bucket and wet cloth (to cool the block).

Control points: **limit runs to the 1-minute order**, stop the moment the wood chars; measure idle loss once before and once after, output = gross − idle loss.
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: P = 2π × speed × torque; speed read as revolutions per 100 half-swings; test runs limited to the 1-minute order (empirical procedure, must be field-calibrated); loading criterion = when speed visibly sags the reading is "power at the reduced speed", not rated power, and **only the plateau of the curve is usable output**; idle loss measured before and after and deducted.
5. **In-process observation items**: per run record block and rope condition / weights and arm / revolutions per 100 half-swings / block temperature and smell (a scorched smell means stop) / loading steps and the power at each / idle loss before and after / points of the power–speed curve.
6. **Pass/rework/scrap criteria**: pass = speed and weight readings stable at a given load, a discernible plateau on the curve, idle loss deducted; rework = readings jumping — dress the rope, tighten the block, retrim the balance; speed falling continuously — drop one load step and remeasure; scrap = block charred or rope parted (**stop the test at once**), or a shaft bearing deformed by heat — recalibrate after the rig is overhauled.
7. **Common failures and troubleshooting order**: ① block smoking or charring (the most frequent) → overloading or overrunning; stop, cool, load in steps; ② speed falling continuously with no plateau → load too great; back off one step and remeasure; ③ readings jumping → rope slipping in the groove or the block loose; dress the rope and tighten; ④ computed power too high → idle loss not deducted; measure it before and after; ⑤ shaft clicker missing counts → loose lug; tighten and recount.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one complete dynamometry run including idle loss and an error annotation; L2 = a "power–speed" curve for the waterwheel's output with the plateau marked; L3 = the dynamometry procedure and block-replacement system in the ledger, with results cross-checked against the lift-method reference (two independent routes agreeing on the same prime mover).

### 25.6 Experimental records and repeated verification

**The five record elements** (missing any one makes the record invalid):

1. Number and date (numbering rules continue Chapter 4's version management);
2. Purpose (what question is to be answered, in one sentence);
3. Conditions (material batch, apparatus state, environment: temperature/weather/operator);
4. Data (raw readings; no transcribing or embellishing; strike-through corrections leave traces, signed after the change);
5. Conclusion (a statement the data supports, no overstepping).

**The three repetition principles**:

- Key numbers get at least three independent measurements (different people, different apparatus, even better); report the median and the range;
- **Negative results are archived too** — failed experiments are assets that eliminate routes (Chapter 4);
- When reporting conclusions, always distinguish "X was observed under these conditions" from "the general law is X" — the former is fact; the latter needs repetition and controls to be promoted.

### 25.7 How to separate correlation from causation by experiment

Two quantities changing together does not mean one causes the other. Three disciplines:

1. **Control group**: any "treatment" experiment (new fuel, new recipe) must keep a group untreated or treated the old way. Example: to judge "new charcoal raises furnace temperature" — same furnace type, same ore, same blast; alternate the two fuels, three runs each, varying only the fuel;
2. **Single variable**: change one condition at a time. Want to swap the fuel and change the airflow at the same time? Split it into two experiments, or run the full 2×2 four-group combination (costly, but the conclusions are hard);
3. **Confounder screening**: list every factor that might change along with the "treatment" (batch, weather, operator skill) and rotate or balance them. Classic trap: the three furnaces trialing the new fuel all happened to be fired in the morning and all operated by the master craftsman — conclusion void.

**Falsifiable statement**: write the conclusion as "if X is the cause, then changing X should change Y" — the next experiment attacks exactly that prediction. Successful falsification = one route eliminated (an asset); repeated failure to falsify = the rule's credibility rises (this is the Part 0 science core landing in this chapter).

### 25.8 Engineering calculation and estimation

The workshop floor does not need calculus; it needs **estimation** — before work starts, spend half an incense stick computing "will this roughly hold up, and how much material will it take"; if the estimate is wrong, compute again — a hundred times cheaper than a wasted furnace run.

**The four-piece estimation toolkit (each piece gives the formula + an order-of-magnitude check)**:

1. **Proportion conversion**: when batching by mass parts, scale any batch proportionally — example: Chapter 23's nitrate-boiling ratio 5:1; one 60 kg pot of nitrate earth takes 12 kg of ash. **Check**: after computing, add the totals back up — they must equal the planned batch; a mismatch means the arithmetic is wrong.
2. **Rough beam deflection** (universal for river-crossing beams, floor slabs, and line-shaft beams): under the same load, **simply supported with a uniform load: double the span and the downward bend grows about 16-fold (the L⁴ law); cantilever with a concentrated load: 8-fold (L³) — identify the load case before using this**; **double the beam's thickness and the downward bend falls to one eighth**. The point is not exact values but comparison: which of two designs is stiffer, at a glance. **Check**: if the estimated downward bend exceeds the 1/100-of-span order (empirical red line, must be calibrated), thicken the beam or add a mid-column.
3. **Rough shaft torque**: transmitted power P (measured by the lift method) is delivered by a wheel of radius r; tangential force ≈ P ÷ (2π × rotational speed × r). Use: compare the computed tangential force against the rope/belt's calibrated load (Chapter 15's ÷5 safety factor) — exceeding it is a preview of belt slip or a snapped rope. **Check**: tangential force × radius should be about the order of "power ÷ angular velocity"; the two methods cross-check each other.
4. **Order-of-magnitude self-check (universal)**: ask three questions of any computed result — are the units right (only length times length makes area)? Is the magnitude plausible (a bellows' airflow computed at 100 m³/min should be doubted — one person blowing cannot reach it)? Does it degenerate correctly at the extremes (what does the formula give when a parameter goes to zero)? If any of the three fails, recompute.

**Estimation's institutional position**: these four go in before the "parameter calibration" field of the process card — **estimate first to fix the plan, test to fix the parameters, and the ledger fixes the standard**. A project launched straight on feel, when it fails, cannot even be traced to which step went wrong (the arithmetic prerequisite to the Chapter 38 ledger).

[Card 25-06 Engineering-Estimation Four-Piece Set] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: the basic data of the job to be estimated (planned batch and ratio, span and load, transmitted power and wheel radius, speed); current process parameters and past ledger entries (Chapter 38).
2. **Required tools**: counting rods, abacus or paper and pen (Chapter 56 calculation tools), standard rod and gauges (for actual dimensions), balance (for material weights), the "parameter calibration" field of the process card (the estimate sheet is attached to it).

8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one complete four-piece estimate sheet with the four computations and the three self-check conclusions on file; L2 = three estimates of the same type of job compared against actual material used and deflection, deviations logged one by one (no hard threshold — watch the trend); L3 = the estimate sheet written into the engineering system as a mandatory attachment before work starts, with a second person rechecking from the same sheet and agreeing.

### 25.9 How to build the first laboratory

**Siting and layout (continuing Chapter 13 construction)**:

- A separate room, far from vibration and dust sources such as forging/sieving; doors and windows facing each other for through-ventilation, exhausted at the downwind opening;
- Zones: ① wet bench (washing, corrosive operations, by the water source and drain trough) ② dry bench (weighing, assembly, records) ③ balance table (an independent stone-slab table, against a wall away from door/window drafts) ④ heating bench (furnace, lamp, away from wooden walls, with a sand bucket and a water bucket) ⑤ reagent cabinet (shaded and cool) ⑥ archive cabinet (fireproof, separated from the heating zone);
- Vibration isolation: the balance and precision-instrument tables share no foundation with the heating bench; sand boxes underneath if necessary;
- The fume hood in embryo: the heating bench sits in a window-side alcove; open the downwind window while operating — **no operation producing smoke/vapor may be carried out indoors where there is no ventilation**.

The laboratory is the physical embodiment of Chapter 4's record system — **without this room, the instruments of the first four sections will drift out of calibration, go missing, and contaminate one another within half a year**.

### 25.10 Data chart standards

Turn number tables into charts. One correctly drawn chart beats three pages of numbers; one wrongly drawn chart kills three batches of experiments. This section's standards are a mandatory system, paired with the five record elements (25.6): **the chart is an attachment to the record, not a replacement for it** — raw readings are still archived as numbers; the chart only presents.

**The coordinate system's three essentials (missing any one voids the chart)**:

- Each axis states three things: **quantity (which physical quantity) — unit (which charter unit) — division (what one grid square represents)**. Example: "furnace temperature (degrees, 1 grid = 5 degrees)".
- Grid values may only be 1, 2, 5 and their tenfold and hundredfold multiples (1 grid = 5 degrees is fine; 1 grid = 3 degrees is forbidden — reading grids relies on mental rounding, and an odd grid value forces misreads).
- Choose the divisions so the curve fills the paper: data crowded into a corner or spilling off the paper both void the chart. **The origin need not be zero** — a thermometer comparison drawn from −5 degrees up is perfectly legal, as long as the starting value is written plainly on the axis; but **a bar chart's baseline must be zero** (bar height compares magnitudes; raising the baseline is a trick that exaggerates differences).

**Polylines and smooth curves (when joining is permitted, and how)**:

- **Point-to-point polyline**: discrete observations independent of one another — yields by batch, case counts by ten-day period, furnace temperature by run. Straight lines join point to point, pretending to know nothing about what happened in between.
- **Smooth curve**: repeated measurements of one continuous physical quantity under the same conditions — thermometer comparisons, the h–flow table (25.4). The curve passes smoothly through the middle of the point cloud, letting random errors cancel left against right.
- One Iron Law: **data points are always drawn and never swallowed by the curve** — plot measured points as dots or small crossed squares, and the curve may only run through the middle between the points. Moving points to make a curve pretty is **fabrication**, the same crime as transcribing and embellishing raw readings (the 25.6 discipline).
- **Interpolation and extrapolation**: reading a value inside the measured interval is interpolation (legal); extending the read beyond the outermost data points is extrapolation (the 25.4 discipline already stands: extrapolation forbidden). On the chart, draw a solid line between the two outermost points and nothing beyond — to extend, first take more measurements.

**Bar charts (the common language of two kinds of occasion)**:

- Ten-day-period count bar charts for epidemics and injuries: horizontal axis = ten-day period or week, vertical axis = case count, one bar per period. Whether the epidemic is lifting its head shows at a glance in the heights of three bars — far faster than flipping through tables.
- Yield-control bar charts: horizontal axis = batch number, vertical axis = yield or reject count; a bar suddenly off the herd is the signal that something went wrong in that batch — trace the records back by batch number (Chapter 4 version management).
- The bar chart's four rules: equal bar widths, equal bar spacing, a zero baseline, values marked on bar tops.

**Chart annotation system (four lines, lower-right corner)**:

1. Figure number (following Chapter 4's numbering rules, e.g. "Figure 25-10-A");
2. Date drawn;
3. Conditions line: material batch, apparatus state, environment — copied from the conditions field of the five record elements;
4. Draftsman.

Charts without annotations are treated as scrap paper; a chart counts as archived only once pasted onto the corresponding page of the experiment record book and given its page number.

**Self-made graph paper**: rule the grid on glossy paper with the standard rod and dividers, 10 small squares = 1 large square; make the master first, and once it passes verification, copy-print batches from it as stock; the master is numbered and archived; if it warps with damp, remake it.
[Card 25-07  ] (eight fields)

2. **Required tools**: standard rod (the length reference for gridding, verification see Chapter 3); magnifier (Chapter 24, for reading small squares);
3. **Sequence and control points**: fix the two axes (independent variable horizontal) → write the three essentials (quantity—unit—division) → plot the points (measured points crossed, traces left) → draw the lines (polyline or smooth, chosen per the 25.10 rules) → add the four annotation lines at lower right; **control point: before drawing lines, self-check "points on the chart = rows in the data table" — one point missing is one omitted**;

### 25.11 Rough error propagation

The errors of direct measurement are honestly declared throughout this chapter's sections (temperature ±0.5 degrees, length ±half a division, balance ±readability). A quantity computed indirectly comes from multiplying and dividing these numbers, and **error only grows, never shrinks** — copying the instrument's precision verbatim as the computed result's precision is the second kind of fabrication.

**Rule 1 (addition and subtraction): absolute errors add.**

The error of a sum or difference = the sum of the terms' errors. Example: temperature difference = outlet temperature − inlet temperature; two thermometers at ±0.5 degrees each give a difference error of ±1 degree, even if both come from one batch and one verification. **The half-saving trick**: use the same thermometer to read both places in turn and subtract; the thermometer's own systematic bias cancels in the subtraction, leaving only the reading error of ±0.5 degrees — a direct dividend, on the error ledger, of the comparative-measurement idea (this chapter's running thread).

**Rule 2 (multiplication and division): relative errors add.**

The relative error of a product or quotient = the sum of the factors' relative errors; relative error = absolute error ÷ that quantity's value. This rule applies to every "measure A and B, compute C" situation (lift-method power, density, torque conversion).

**Significant figures: keep only one doubtful digit.**

A result's last digit is where the error lives; anything below the last digit is meaningless — extra digits written in when transcribing are called **false precision** and are institutionally treated as data embellishment. Execution: after computing, estimate the error by Rule 1 or 2, then truncate to match the error — if the error falls at the units place, the result stops at the units place.

**Worked example 1 (power = force × velocity: the complete error account of the 25.5 lift method)**:

- The factors: mass 10.0 unit stones (±0.5% actually taken once sling and pulley friction are counted in; calibration method: weigh the same weight once hung straight on the balance and once through the pulley block; the difference is the friction allowance, logged rig by rig); height 3.0 m (standard-rod reading ±0.5%); time 20 half-swings (start/stop judgment ±1 half-swing = ±5%).
- Combined: 0.5%+0.5%+5%≈6%. Power = 10.0×3.0÷20 = 1.5 (unit stone·meters per half-swing); the report reads "1.5 (±6%)" — the last digit is already the doubtful digit; write no more.
- **The error account points to the improvement**: of the three terms the largest is timing. Stretch the count from 20 half-swings to 100 and the start/stop ±1 half-swing dilutes to ±1%, dropping the total error to about 2% — no instrument changed, only the measurement duration; precision triples. There lies the whole value of rough error propagation: **it shows which link deserves the effort**.

**Worked example 2 (density = mass/volume)**:

- Mass 6.2 g, balance reads ±0.1 g → 1.6% relative; volume 2.5 cm³ (displacement method, graduated tube read to ±0.1 cm³) → 4% relative.
- Combined: 1.6%+4%≈6%. Density = 6.2÷2.5 = 2.48 g/cm³, error ±0.14 → keep one doubtful digit; the report reads "2.5 g/cm³ (±6%)".
- To measure more accurately: switching to a finer graduated tube (pressing the volume term from 4% down to the 1% level) pays far better than a more sensitive balance (1.6% is already the small term) — run the numbers before spending.

**Institutional position**: every indirect quantity in this chapter must carry an error annotation when entered into the master parameter table, the ledger, or reports; an indirect quantity without an error annotation is treated as uncalibrated (the corresponding section is downgraded).

### 25.12 Experimental design extensions

25.7 gave three disciplines (control, single variable, confounder screening); this section gives three tools, so the disciplines need not be reinvented on the spot every time.

**Tool 1: the 2×2 factorial (two factors, the "four-cell contrast reading")**:

- The complementary case to the single-variable discipline is "you wanted to see two factors at once anyway" — then do not lazily change one at a time; **run all four combinations**: factor A (fuel: old charcoal/new charcoal) × factor B (airflow: normal draft/doubled draft), four furnace runs. The response here uses yield as the example (yield, temperature, strength — any measurable response works the same):

  | Yield | A low (old charcoal) | A high (new charcoal) |
  |---|---|---|
  | B low (normal draft) | 60% | 68% |
  | B high (doubled draft) | 66% | 70% |

- **Main-effect reading (two contrasts, averaged)**: the effect of A (new charcoal) = right-column mean − left-column mean = 69−63 = **+6%**; the effect of B (doubled draft) = bottom-row mean − top-row mean = 68−64 = **+4%**.
- **Interaction reading (folk judgment)**: what is A's effect under high B and under low B? Under low B, +8%; under high B, +4% — **the two differences clearly unequal, so there is an interaction**: the gain from new charcoal shrinks when the blast is doubled (candidate explanation: new charcoal has more pores and burns faster, and the doubled airflow shortchanges the reduction time — left for later verification). What would single-factor experiments give here? Test old charcoal only and vary the airflow: "doubled draft +6%". Test normal draft only and vary the charcoal: "new charcoal +8%" — **each conclusion is half right; used together they are wrong**: actually running "new charcoal + doubled draft" yields +10%, not 6+8=+14%.
- Cost boundary: four runs are twice the two runs of a single-factor test; but if you were going to split into two rounds of single-factor anyway (four runs in all), the factorial costs no extra run and throws in an interaction judgment for free. **Whenever two factors are suspected of coupling, the four-cell factorial is always cheaper than two rounds of single-factor, and its conclusions are harder.**

**Tool 2: repetition count — the iterative "measure until the range stabilizes" method**:

- Start with 3 measurements and note the range (max − min); measure a 4th and 5th and recompute the range. Range clearly grows (the growth exceeds the smallest reading division) → conditions are still wobbling, keep measuring; **the range stops growing across two consecutive batches (the difference is below the readability order) → stop, and report the median of that batch**.
- Empirical value: most measurements in this chapter converge in 3–5 tries (empirical, must be field-calibrated: weigh the same weight 10 times and plot a range-vs-count polyline (25.10) to self-calibrate once; thereafter each measurement's routine count is set from the curve and entered in the ledger).
- Red line: a range that keeps growing and never converges means the apparatus or the conditions are unstable — **stop and inspect the apparatus; more repetitions cannot rescue bad conditions** (the applicability boundary of the three repetition principles of 25.6).

**Tool 3: randomization — drawing lots to prevent the "morning effect"**:

- The radical cure for 25.7's classic trap (the new fuel's three furnaces all fired in the morning, all fired by the master craftsman): **grouping and order are not decided by people but by lots** — write "morning/afternoon, first furnace/eighth furnace, master/apprentice" onto lots in a tube; draw lots to set the two groups' timing and operators; copy the draw results into the conditions field of the five record elements for the record.
- Minimal rule: **"doing the easy ones first" is forbidden** — once drawn, the order may not be swapped; if a swap is truly necessary, state the reason in the record; if more than half a round's runs get swapped, that round is void and redrawn.
- The lot tube is kept at the laboratory dry bench (25.8); the bamboo lots carry branded numbers; count the lots monthly; missing lots are remade and renumbered into the archive.

[Card 25-08 2×2 Factorial Test] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: the physical materials for two factors at two levels each (example: same-batch new charcoal and old charcoal, ample quantities of each; airflow realised as two damper settings); the same batch of raw material shared by all four runs (a batch difference is confounding, and that is the cardinal sin); comparison record paper and the experiment logbook.
2. **Tools required**: the response instrument chosen to suit the response (thermometer / balance / measuring bucket — all this chapter's calibrated pieces with a valid release slip); seconds-pendulum timer (25.3); lot tube and branded bamboo lots (kept at the dry bench, 25.8); a four-cell table drawn up in advance, with only the response readings to be filled in.
3. **Operating sequence and control points**: ① fix the two factors and two levels (level difference at the ±20% order of the current process) → ② **draw lots for the four runs' order and operators** (lots read "morning/afternoon, first run/fourth run, master/apprentice"; once drawn, no swapping) → ③ record every run in full by the five record elements (condition line with time band / operator / weather / batch number) → ④ fill the four-cell table → ⑤ compute the two main effects (right-column mean − left-column mean = effect of A; bottom-row mean − top-row mean = effect of B) and the interaction (A's two differences under high B and low B clearly unequal means an interaction) → ⑥ conclude with error annotations per 25.11. Control points: **the four runs share one raw-material batch and one apparatus state; the only permitted difference is the two factors inside the cells**; a round in which more than half the runs were swapped is void and must be redrawn.
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: start at **2–3 repetitions per cell** and add measurements by the "range stabilizes" iteration (25.12, Tool 2) — stop when the range has not grown for two consecutive batches and report that batch's median (most measurements in this chapter converge in 3–5 tries); level difference at the **±20% order** of the current process (too small and the main effect drowns in the error, too large and it leaves the range that can be made into process). Calibration: run one coarse gradient round first to set the levels (open the difference wide enough to see one), then come back and run the four cells; compute error annotations item by item per 25.11 (absolute errors add for sums and differences, relative errors add for products and quotients). All of these are **empirical values that must be calibrated to local conditions**.
5. **Observations to record**: each of the four runs' condition line in full (time band / operator / weather / batch number); response readings reported as median and range; corroborating signs (colour of fuel embers, tuyere slagging) recorded alongside; the order in which the four-cell table was filled and a copy of the draw results.
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = four cells complete, both main effects larger than their own error annotations, and the interaction judged (present or absent, with the basis written down); reworkable = a main effect below the error (judged "not detected under these conditions": add repetitions or widen the level difference and re-run); scrap = the four cells' conditions are not matched (material batch or apparatus inconsistent) — void the whole round and redo it; **reading it anyway is forbidden**.
7. **Common failures and troubleshooting order** (by frequency): (1) two rounds disagree → check the lot-drawing record first (was the order privately swapped), then whether material batches were mixed, and finally whether an error annotation was left out (readings' wobble mistaken for an effect); (2) all four cells read almost alike → the level difference is too small and the main effect drowns in the error (run a coarse gradient round first to open up the levels); (3) the interaction comes and goes → too few repetitions per cell (2 often gives a false interaction; go to 3, or add measurements by the range-stabilization method); (4) one cell a clear outlier → check whether that run's time band and operator match the lot drawn (morning effect / change of operator).
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one four-cell table and conclusion in the experiment logbook (main effects, interaction and error annotations all three present); L2 = one interaction detected and a process decision corrected on the strength of it (the record of the recipe or airflow change on file); L3 = the factorial procedure and routine repetition count entered in the laboratory ledger for later chapters' formulation trials to copy as-is, and a second person running one round from this card alone reaching a conclusion in the same direction.

## Safety boundaries

- Wear eye protection for fine glass tube work (broken ends cut like knives); tie back long hair and cinch sleeves for lamp-flame work;
- Time-limit brake dynamometry to keep the brake block from overheating into fire; clear the area below lift-method tests (falling weights);
- No eating or drinking in the laboratory; never taste or sniff reagents (an extension of the Chapters 8 and 23 red lines);
- Any smoke-producing operation must be done at a ventilated station — CO and acid mist are equally deadly (Chapter 6).

## Acceptance criteria

- **L1**: two-fixed-point thermometer calibration succeeds with three same-batch units reading within ≤1 degree; seconds pendulum and water clock mutually calibrated to ≤2% deviation; the lift method completes one power measurement each for human and animal power.

- **L3**: the four great reference standards — standard thermometer, weight chain, seconds-pendulum length, power reference — all under sealed custody with periodic cross-checks; laboratory ledger and safety zoning systems in operation; continuously supplying quantitative data for Chapter 26 chemistry and Chapter 28 heat.

## Failure modes and diagnostics

| Symptom | Candidate causes (by probability) | Verification | Disposition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Large reading difference between two thermometers | uneven capillary bore / wrong fixed-point conditions | recalibrate the ice point three times | cross-check and class within the batch; redo the fixed-point method |
| The balance's two pans not level | beam midpoint offset / unequal sling lengths | fine-adjust with the beam suspended empty | adjust the midpoint; equal-length slings |
| Readability drops suddenly | dull knife edge / dirty anvil face | inspect the edge with a magnifier | regrind the knife edge; wipe the anvil |
| Seconds pendulum gradually slows | amplitude over the limit / heat lengthening the rod | eyeball the amplitude + mutual calibration with the water clock | limit the amplitude; log as a systematic error |
| Manometer reading drifts | air bubble in the tube / contaminated liquid | purge air and remeasure | refill the liquid and purge the air |
| Dynamometer brake block smokes | over-loading / running too long | the time-limit procedure | stop and cool; load in steps |
| Extrapolation crosses the boundary on a chart | the curve extended past the outermost data points to read values | check the range covered by the chart's data points and the position of the value read | strike the extrapolated value; measure the boundary points and switch to interpolation |
| Report digits inflated | error propagation omitted for an indirect quantity, instrument digits copied verbatim | recompute the error per 25.11 Rule 1/2 | truncate to one doubtful digit and reissue the report |
| Factorial conclusion misled by a single factor | four cells not uniform / only main effects read, interaction missed | recompute from the four-cell table whether the two differences are equal | fill the missing cells and redo; rewrite the conclusions per the interaction term |
