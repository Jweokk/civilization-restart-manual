### 26.1 How to Make Simple Laboratory Glassware

Building on the Section 25.1 tube-drawing effort, establish the laboratory-vessel family:

- **Flask/retort**: on top of the blown bubble (Chapter 14), draw a neck — heat the bubble top until it softens, grip the tip and pull it out into a neck; for the side-neck retort (for distillation), heat-open a hole on the bubble's belly and attach an angled tube;
- **Condenser**: three-part construction — an inner tube (a long straight tube, the vapor channel) jacketed in a wider outer tube (the water jacket), with side openings at both ends for the rubber inlet/outlet water hoses (Chapter 15); used inclined, with cold water entering at the low end and leaving at the high end (counterflow condensation, the most efficient direction);
- **Receiver and adapters**: narrow-neck bottles; a delivery adapter (cow-horn shape); ground-joint fittings (ground stopper: core rod and socket lapped against each other + carborundum-grade abrasive, fine abrasive grades from Chapter 9); seal integrity is tested by "stoppered, then suction applied — no leak";
- **Drying tube**: a bulb blown mid-tube holds drying granules (lumps of calcined lime); both ends plugged with glass fiber (the asbestos substitute: glass wool — waste strands from tube drawing);
- **Measuring vessels**: graduated cylinder (a straight tube with etched graduations, the Chapter 3 equal-division method + thermometer water-temperature correction: volumes are standardized on cold water and noted as such); dropper (a fine tube drawn to a point at one end, fused at the other to a rubber-grade soft bulb — where there is no rubber, use a grease-impregnated leather bulb).

**Inspection grading**: uniform wall thickness (view the shadow against the light); passing anneal (Chapter 14 ash-pit annealing; survives sudden heating without cracking); heat-shock resistance graded by the two-level "boiling water – warm water" test — the high grade becomes reactors, the low grade storage.

### 26.2 Distillation and the Separation of Volatile Substances

- **Routine distillation**: the three-phase discipline of heat-up – hold – cut. The thermometer's column position (25.1) decides the cut point — **a steady-temperature plateau = a near-pure component; a climbing-temperature segment = a mixed fraction, collected separately**;
- **[Process Card 26-01 Condensation Control] (review-flagged block)**: condenser water flow is adjusted with **outlet water temperature** as the criterion — outlet "warm but not hot" (hand-feel tier about 35–45 ℃) = flow rate right; outlet scalding = flow insufficient (vapor punching through the condenser = product loss + acid mist escaping); outlet cold = water wasted (the desert/island water-budget red line). Paired with **distillate-rate control**: an empirical tier of "1–2 drops per second" for the distillate (must be field-calibrated — too fast = separation collapses; too slow = heat spent on time); the fractionating column is wrapped for insulation (a column body hot in one place and cold in another = reflux inside the column thrown into disorder = a "white," dry packing). Observation items recorded every 10 min: column-top temperature, outlet water temperature, distillate drop count;
- **The physical boundary of alcohol purification (stated as-is)**: distilling an alcohol–water mixture at atmospheric pressure, the vapor concentration locks at the azeotrope once it reaches roughly eighty or ninety percent — **atmospheric distillation cannot make anhydrous alcohol**. When anhydrous grade (reagent) is needed, the drying method: distill over quicklime (lime absorbs water, not alcohol) to get nearly anhydrous alcohol (purity tested by "burns with no residual water mark + the density reference table");
- **Fractionating column**: a long-neck packed column (glass beads / broken glass tubing) raises separation — every condensation–re-evaporation cycle is one purification opportunity; the longer the column, the more opportunities;
- **The vacuum concept (registration only)**: lowering the pressure above a liquid lowers its boiling point — scale-up to be backfilled after the Chapter 33 vacuum pump;
- **Acid distillation (upgrade: three-acid manufacture has moved to Section 23.9)**: the complete processes and safety protocols for green-vitriol dry distillation to oil of vitriol, the saltpeter route to nitric acid, and the table-salt route to hydrochloric acid are **in Chapter 23, Section 23.9 (Process Cards 23-01 through 23-04)** — not repeated here; this section supplements laboratory-grade details: small-sample trials use the full glass retort set (ceramic retorts are the industrial line; glass retorts serve the reagent line), the receiver sits in a cold-water bath, and **all tail gas goes through absorption bottles (table in 26.10)**.

### 26.3 Crystallization, Precipitation, and Filtration (Laboratory Grade)

The same four operations as Chapter 23, but with the standard raised to reagent grade:

- Crystallization: **slow crystallization + seed crystals + single-habit selection** — from each batch of crystals pick the single crystal with the most standard habit as the next seed (directional selection; purity and crystal habit reinforce each other);
- Precipitation: precipitant added **drop by drop + continuous stirring** (fast addition wraps up impurities); the precipitate cycles through "settle – decant – rewash – resettle," with the wash tested by indicator until the mother liquor's ions are gone — that is the endpoint;
- Filtration: two upgrades — hot filtration (an insulated funnel prevents premature crystallization) and vacuum suction filtration (aspirator + a proto-Büchner funnel: porous ceramic plate + cloth pad);
- **Purity tracking table**: at every purification stage record "yield + melting range (Chapter 25 thermometer)" — yield down and melting range narrower = purification working; yield collapsed and melting range unmoved = at the limit, or contamination.

### 26.4 Gas Collection and Basic Properties

- **Collection over water**: fill the collecting bottle with water and invert it in a water trough; the gas delivery tube enters the bottle mouth and the gas pushes the water out; when collection is done, slide a ground-glass plate over the mouth underwater and lift the bottle out (underwater handling keeps air out);
- **Generator**: a two-neck generating bottle (solid charged in the bottle, acid added through the side neck — the simple Kipp-generator idea: a hose-pinch valve stops the acid feed and thereby stops the gas);
- **Three-property tests** (mandatory for every new gas): ① the burn/support test (a glowing splint relights = supports combustion, as with the products of water electrolysis or heated saltpeter; goes out by itself = inert or non-supporting; ignite and observe flame color and products) ② the limewater test (turns cloudy = an oxide of carbon) ③ the acid–base test (red test solution turns blue on dissolving = the ammonia family; a gas that clouds limewater and dissolves in water to form an acid = the carbonic-acid family);
- **The gas registration system**: four columns entered in the register — name (phenomenon-based naming + number), preparation method, properties, hazards (toxicity / explosibility). **A combustible gas mixed with air explodes at a flame (general empirical rule): purity must be verified before ignition (ignite a small inverted bottleful; only with no explosive pop may the gas be used)**.

### 26.5 Metal Salts and Chemical Reactions

- **Displacement reaction (the origin of hydrometallurgy)**: immerse an iron strip in a copper-salt solution (blue vitriol solution: made from oxidized copper ore + dilute sulfuric acid); red copper plates out on the iron and the solution's color pales — register **wet copper cementation** as the fallback copper-production route for fuel-short situations;
- **The precipitation color table (the beginning of qualitative analysis)**: copper salts blue, iron(III) salts rusty red-brown, lead salts yellow, silver white (if there is silver-ore by-product)... make a standard color-sample tube of each and archive it;
- **Flame tests**: sodium yellow, potassium violet (viewed through blue glass, Chapter 24), calcium brick-red, copper green — a cleaned wire (acid-washed and flame-fired repeatedly until colorless) dips the sample into the lamp flame;
- **Acids with metals/oxides**: dilute acid + iron = hydrogen (collect, verify purity, then ignite); acid + copper rust/iron rust = a salt solution — **acid is the key from "ore → solution"**, laying the line for Chapter 34's leaching purification;
- **The conservation-of-mass check**: weigh a sealed retort before and after calcination (Chapter 25 balance) — mass unchanged while properties change drastically: the operational evidence for "recombination of the elements."

### 26.6 How to Raise Raw-Material Purity

The reagent-grade purification ladder (building on Section 23.3):

1. **Distilled water**: distill at atmospheric pressure in an all-glass train, discard the heads and tails, store the middle fraction in a dedicated bottle — the "solvent zero" of reagent-grade work;
2. **Multi-round recrystallization**: recrystallize the same substance 3–5 times in a row, recording the melting range each time until it stops changing;
3. **Precipitation scavenging**: choose the precipitant per the 26.5 precipitation table to remove a target ion (example: for calcium impurity, add oxalic acid/soda to precipitate);
4. **Vessel discipline**: reagent-grade work uses only glass and glazed-ceramic vessels; metal tools (spoons, scrapers) are acid-washed + distilled-water rinsed + oven-dried before use; **a dedicated bench, physically isolated from industrial-grade operations**;
5. **The purity archive**: every reagent bottle is labeled "grade + inspection record + date," with the recheck interval registered after opening.

### 26.7 Chemical Reagent Storage and Safety Management

**The labeling system (no label = discard immediately; no "guessing")**: name, concentration/grade, preparation date, hazard symbols (three self-drawn symbols: fire / poison / corrosion), preparer.

**Zoned storage**: an acid cabinet (glass bottles on ceramic drip trays, preventing mutual contamination); oxidizers (the saltpeter family) and combustibles/reductants (sulfur, charcoal, oils) stored in **separate rooms**; flammable liquids (alcohol, distilled oils) kept cool and away from fire; deliquescents wax-sealed.

**High-hazard red lines (special warnings in the context of this book's preserved knowledge)**:

| Substance | Hazard | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Concentrated acids / oil of vitriol | Corrosion | Operate with ventilation; pour acid into water (**never water into acid**); keep alkali solution on hand for neutralization |
| Hydrofluoric acid (fluorite + sulfuric acid, for etching) | Deep corrosion, attacks bone | Only the dedicated glass-etching team; leather gloves + ventilation; on skin contact, soak immediately in limewater — **preparation forbidden without protective conditions** |
| Mercury (if cinnabar is available) | Cumulative vapor poisoning | Banned — **until the Section 16.9 mercury-unlocking protocol is built**; after unlocking, follow the mercury discipline of Chapters 33/53 |
| Saltpeter + sulfur + charcoal mixed | Deflagration | The three components are **always** stored and transported separately; mixing happens only at the point of use, in small quantity |
| White phosphorus (the bone-ash + acid route, if attempted) | Spontaneously flammable, highly toxic | Trial production is listed as a development-push project; register the scheme only — no execution without dedicated protection |

**Waste liquids**: neutralize (indicator confirms near-neutral), then discard into a dedicated pit (far from water sources; location registered on the Chapter 2 hydrological map); heavy-metal waste liquids are buried deep and separately, and marked.

### 26.8 Experimental Chemistry vs. Industrial Chemistry

The same reaction, scaled up tenfold, often fails — the three great scale-up effects:

1. **Heat transfer**: a small flask sheds heat fast; the center of a big kettle overheats — reaction heat is harmless at small scale, runaway at large scale (it kills the safety margin and the yield together). Countermeasures: batch the feed; stir (hydraulic stirring, Chapter 21); add a water-cooled jacket outside the kettle;
2. **Stirring and mixing**: at large volume, concentration is uneven — incomplete reaction and side reactions coexist. Countermeasure: longer stirring time does not make up for poor mixing; the stirring structure itself must be improved (measure the actual "feed-to-uniform" time and register it);
3. **Yield economics**: a 90% experimental yield is not worth scaling (labor/fuel/time costs); the industrial line demands "low labor + continuous + recoverable mother liquor" — the Chapter 23 yield ledger, used in scale-up decisions: **only when three small-trial batches hold a stable yield and the raw-material supply is sustainable do you move up one scale tier**.

### 26.9 The Recrystallization Solubility–Temperature Curve (review-flagged block)

Where does multi-round recrystallization's "stage gain" come from? From **the difference in how solubility changes with temperature**. For each new salt batch (or new impurity system), measure once at five temperature tiers and draw the local curve:

[Card 26-02 Solubility Curve Determination] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: salt sample to purify at the 50 g scale (measured in one batch; crush and mix a caked sample first, or the five points describe five different batches); distilled water (the 26.6 solvent zero).
2. **Tools required**: the Chapter 25 thermometer (the objective reading behind the hand-feel tiers), the Chapter 25 balance (salt and water), five identical glass cups (water level scribed on the wall), a stirring rod, graph paper (Chapter 25).
3. **Operation sequence and control points**: (1) prepare one cup of a measured amount of water at each of five temperature tiers (winter water / room temperature / warm to the hand / hot to the hand / near boiling — recorded by hand-feel tier + thermometer) → (2) add salt in small increments, stirring until **it no longer dissolves and crystals remain on the cup bottom** (saturated) → (3) compute "salt consumed ÷ water amount" = the saturated solubility at that temperature → (4) connect the five points into a curve. Control points: **salt goes in small portions, many times** (dumping it in at once = judging saturation before dissolution is complete = the whole curve reads low); record the actual water temperature at every tier; never let a tier name stand in for a reading.
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: the five tiers = winter water / room temperature / warm to the hand / hot to the hand / near boiling (empirical tiers, calibrate to local water temperature and season; re-measure when the season turns); solvent amount = 1.2–1.5× the hot-saturated amount (empirical, must be field-calibrated: too much lowers recovery, too little crystallizes out during hot filtration and clogs the paper). How to use it: the **hot-dissolve, cool-crystallize temperature pair** = dissolve at the curve's high point (near boiling), crystallize at its low point (winter water) — the bigger the gap, the higher one recrystallization's recovery and purification efficiency; a salt with a small gap (insensitive to temperature) switches to the **evaporation crystallization** route (if the curve cannot feel temperature, do not expect cooling to help). Cooling rate: slow (overnight) = large crystals, low inclusion (purity first); quenching (water bath) = fine crystals, fast (one grade down in purity) — the reagent line slow-cools, the industrial line may quench.
5. **In-process observation items**: for each tier record actual water temperature / total salt added / water amount / whether crystals remained / time to dissolve; the curve plot and the five raw points go into the reagent archive together (a curve without its raw points cannot be recomputed).
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = re-measured deviation at three curve points <10%; rework = one point ≥10% (re-prepare and re-measure that point; check the temperature reading and false saturation first); scrap = an anomalous curve (solubility at low temperature above that at high) = a weighing or temperature error; re-measure the whole curve.
7. **Common failures and troubleshooting order**: (1) all points nearly equal ("a curve that cannot feel temperature") → not a measurement error: the salt is temperature-insensitive, switch to evaporation crystallization; (2) the five points will not form a monotonic line → check whether the temperature tier was misread, then whether damp, moisture-picking salt was used; (3) recrystallization recovery far below the curve's promise → the solvent multiple is too large or cooling is not low enough (recheck per item 4); (4) large deviation on re-measuring the same batch → the sample was not ground and mixed, so it is not representative.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one five-point curve + the raw weighing record; L2 = two batches of the same salt with three points cross-compared <10%, and one recrystallization per the curve matching the predicted recovery; L3 = curve and re-measurement records filed continuously, and a second person measuring the same salt from this card alone produces a superimposable curve.

### 26.10 The Master Tail-Gas Absorption Table (review-flagged block)

For every operation that produces irritant/toxic gas, the tail gas must pass through absorption bottles — **the universal apparatus: an inverted funnel half-submerged in the absorbent's surface (prevents suck-back) + two stages in series**:

| Gas | Source | Absorbent | Saturation check (absorption-failure signal) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chlorine | Chapter 34 chlorination; hydrochloric acid + oxidizer | Ash lye / caustic lye | Second bottle turns yellow-green = first bottle saturated; change the liquid |
| HCl | Chapter 23 hydrochloric acid line | Water, three vessels in series (23-04) | Last vessel pungent = add a vessel |
| SO₂/SO₃ | Roasting; acid-making tail gas | Ash lye | Odor breaks through = change the liquid |
| NH₃ | Hide-glue boiling; manure fermentation | Dilute acid (vinegar / dilute sulfuric acid) | Pungent = change the liquid |
| Hydrogen sulfide | Leblanc residue; rotting eggs | Ash lye + (long-term) iron salts | Rotten-egg smell = change the liquid and inspect the residue pile (Chapter 49) |
| **The cyanide family (long-term registration)** | Gold/silver wet methods (long-term) | Alkali solution | **Red line: acidic conditions produce lethally toxic HCN — without dedicated protection and protocols the entire line is forbidden** (this table sets a boundary only, not a process) |

Universal saturation check for absorbents: test the absorbent with red-cabbage indicator paper strips — change the liquid when the color shift reaches the set tier.

[Card 26-03 Tail-Gas Absorption Apparatus] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: absorbent chosen by gas (the 26.10 master table) — ash lye / caustic lye (chlorine, SO₂/SO₃, hydrogen sulfide), water in three vessels in series (HCl), dilute acid (vinegar / dilute sulfuric acid, NH₃), alkali solution (the cyanide family: red line, boundary only, no process); two or more absorption bottles (same size, marked "stage 1 / stage 2"); inverted funnel; rubber hose (Chapter 15); red-cabbage indicator paper strips (same lineage as 26.14).
2. **Tools required**: a bottle rack or fixing (against tipping); stoppers and delivery tubes (self-made ground-joint pieces from 26.12 will do); indicator paper strips; a ventilated station.
3. **Operation sequence and control points**: (1) pick the absorbent from the master table → (2) charge stage one with the **inverted funnel half-submerged in the liquid surface** → (3) put stage two in series (delivery tube below the surface, outlet led to a ventilated point) → (4) connect the reaction's tail gas — the apparatus is in place before the reaction starts → (5) change the liquid at saturation. Control points: **the funnel is half-submerged** — fully submerged means a suck-back pulls liquid straight into the reaction vessel; not touching the surface means incomplete absorption; the apparatus must not be dismantled until the tail-gas phase is complete (stop the gas first, pull the tube second — the 26.15 Iron Law).
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: two stages in series is the standard (a single stage breaks through before its saturation signal ever shows; stage two is the sentinel bottle); the saturation signal is per gas (chlorine: stage two turns yellow-green; HCl: last vessel pungent; SO₂: odor breaks through; NH₃: pungent; hydrogen sulfide: rotten-egg smell); the universal saturation check is red-cabbage paper on the absorbent turning to the set tier (empirical tier, calibrate to the local indicator batch — vegetable juice differs batch to batch, as in 26.14). The change interval comes from the measured time-to-saturation; "I cannot smell it" is not a criterion.
5. **In-process observation items**: per shift record gas species / liquid level in each stage / paper color / whether the surface is bubbling / any signal in stage two / change time and quantity.
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = no signal of that gas in stage two for the whole run and the paper below its tier; rework = stage one at its tier (change the liquid, reseat the funnel, check it is not fully submerged); line stop = stage two at its tier (breakthrough: void that batch, ventilate harder, find the leak); for the cyanide family, one violation = the entire line is forbidden.
7. **Common failures and troubleshooting order**: (1) absorbent sucked back → delivery tube below the liquid or funnel fully submerged (rebuild with the inverted funnel; stop the gas before pulling tubes); (2) stage two at its tier too early → stage one underfilled or already saturated (top up, change); (3) the room still smells while the bottles bubble normally → not enough stages or the wrong absorbent (add a stage per the table, change the liquid); (4) no bubbling at the surface → a leaking joint or the reaction has stopped (look at the reaction first, then the stoppers and gas source).
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one gas-generating run with no breakthrough in stage two throughout; L2 = a month of change records comparable with the saturation signals; L3 = every gas-generating operation (including the hydrogen sulfide red-line) on register with zero breakthrough incidents, reproducible by a second person from this card.

### 26.11 Vessel Washing and the Label Upgrade (Reagent-Grade Hygiene)

- **The cleanliness criterion**: after rinsing, the wall carries a **uniform water film with no beads** — beading = grease (alkali boil → clean water → distilled-water rinse, three stages); test acid residue with indicator paper until the runoff is near-neutral;
- Washing grades: water rinse (general) → soap boil (grease) → dilute-acid wash (metal residue / scale) → distilled-water rinse (the reagent-grade final wash) — **the order must not be reversed** (acid first bakes the grease onto the wall);
- The six label elements (upgraded from the five elements of 26.7): name, concentration/grade, preparation date, hazard symbol, preparer, **expiry/recheck date** — register the recheck date when a bottle is opened; past expiry, recheck or downgrade.

Scale-up discipline (retained from 26.8): scale up only one order of magnitude at a time, recalibrating tier by tier.

[Card 26-04 Three-Stage Wash Cleanliness Criterion] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: vessels to wash (glass / glazed ceramic — reagent grade uses only these two); clean water; soap or alkali liquor; dilute acid; distilled water (final rinse).
2. **Tools required**: wash bottle (26.12 — the hand never touches the inner wall during the final rinse); brush or cloth; red-cabbage indicator paper; a dedicated drying rack; side light (the 24.3 side-light projection bench serves to view the water film).
3. **Operation sequence and control points**: water rinse (general) → soap boil (grease) → dilute-acid wash (metal residue / scale) → distilled-water rinse (the reagent-grade final wash). Control points: **the order must not be reversed** — acid first bakes the grease onto the wall; rinse with clean water between stages, and the final rinse uses distilled water only (one tap-water rinse undoes the work).
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: the cleanliness criterion = after rinsing, a **uniform water film with no beads** (beading = grease); the acid-residue criterion = indicator paper on the runoff reads near-neutral; the three-stage remedy after beading = alkali boil → clean water → distilled-water rinse. Boil duration and acid strength are calibrated to the local water and the grease type (empirical, must be field-calibrated) — **the criterion looks only at the film and the indicator, never at the clock**.
5. **In-process observation items**: per batch record vessel number / duty (reagent or industrial grade) / how each stage was run / film reading (where it beaded) / indicator result / where it was stored after washing; the wash bottle's water-change date.
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = uniform film, no beads, plus a near-neutral runoff; rework = beading (back to the alkali boil and the full three stages) or acid residue (re-rinse to neutral, then the final rinse); scrap = an etched wall (over-boiled or acid-etched) that will never hold a film → downgrade to industrial use, keep it off the reagent line.
7. **Common failures and troubleshooting order**: (1) beading keeps coming back → the alkali boil is too short or the soap is spent (fresh soap, longer boil); (2) the film breaks into lines → finger grease on the wall (hold vessels by the outside only); (3) the indicator still reads acid → no transition rinse after the acid (add a water stage before the distilled rinse); (4) beading again after storage → dirty rack or stood upside down in a waste tray (use the dedicated rack).
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one batch of vessels washed through the three stages with a reading recorded; L2 = a month of wash records with no beading rework; L3 = wash-bottle water-change and wash records continuous, and a second person washing the same batch from this card alone reads it the same way.

### 26.12 Self-Made Fine Glassware (Ground Joints, Stopcocks, the Büchner Funnel, the Wash Bottle)

The 26.1 vessel family is "blank grade"; this section brings four fine workpieces up to "reagent grade." It needs no new equipment — only three things: final-grade abrasive (fine carborundum, Chapter 9), patience, and paired-numbering discipline.

**1. Ground joints (ground stoppers and ground connectors)**

The principle of the ground joint: conical glass surfaces lapped against each other in pairs; the lapped faces, densely covered with fine scratches, interlock and are gas-tight with no gasket of any kind.

- Blanks: the core is a solid tapered rod (a solid glass rod melted at the end, the tip mounded and pulled into a rough blank); the socket is a heavy-wall tube mouth heated and flared into a trumpet cone (flare it on a pre-warmed conical charcoal mold, or press a thick glass rod against the inside of the mouth and spin it outward); both pieces take the same 1:10 taper (a 1 mm diameter difference per 10 mm of cone length, checked with caliper-scribed lines);
- Paired lapping with lapping compound: coarse compound = 100–150 mesh carborundum + grease (warmed to toothpaste thickness); insert the core into the socket and rotate-lap, lifting and re-indexing the core every dozen-odd turns (a quarter-turn per re-index — only then is the scratching even); lap until both cone faces are fully scratched with even contact, wash clean, switch to 300–400 mesh fine grit and fine-lap the same way; for the final pass, lap the pair with fine grit and water alone for about 5 min, then wash thoroughly;
- Acceptance criteria (observable): assembled dry, rotation shows uniform damping; **lifting the core "carries" the whole mating piece with it** (held by atmospheric pressure); stoppered, pulled to negative pressure, mouth water-sealed for 10 min with no liquid-level movement = gas-tight;
- Calibration method: grind three pairs in the first batch and water-seal-test each; all three pass = this technique and that compound batch's parameters enter the ledger; after that, each newly ground pair needs only a single water-seal check.

**The ground-joint numbering and pairing system**: once lapped, engrave the same number on the same side of core and socket (scribed with a diamond point; the hydrofluoric-acid pen is a Section 26.7 red-line item — the etching team engraves on its behalf) — **a ground joint mates with its own partner for life**; cross-lapping ruins both. Keep a ground-joint register: number, mounted on which vessel, lapping date, recheck date.

[Black text] Seizure prevention and first aid for ground joints: when lapping is done, the abrasive must be washed off completely (residual grit = seizure and galling at next use); in storage keep a strip of paper between core and socket; first aid for a seized joint = warm-water soak + slow rotation with both hands (rotate only, never pull); hard twisting in a vise is strictly forbidden — one twisting crack scraps the whole piece.

**2. The stopcock (ground conical plug)**

A stopcock is a rotary switch in a line: the plug sits crosswise in the barrel; turn through an angle for open, turn back for closed.

- Plug: a solid conical plug, 1:10 taper, cone length 25–35 mm, large-end diameter 10–14 mm; barrel: a conical bore heat-opened in the side of a heavy-wall tube (charcoal-mold reaming);
- Lapping: **barrel and plug lapped directly against each other** (no third piece), the compound method as for ground joints; the done criterion = after dry lapping, the plug turns in the barrel with a "silky" hand-feel; once greased, closed it holds suction without leaking, open it turns without sticking;
- Stopcock grease: beeswax 2 parts + grease 1 part, warmed into a paste (too thin = leaks; too stiff = sticks); application = paint three longitudinal ribs on the plug starting from the large end, insert and rotate to spread evenly, and **scrape off every bit of grease squeezed out of the barrel mouth** (grease in the flow path = reagent contamination);
- Rules of use: rotate slowly about the axis only; no side leverage (side force = scratched lapped faces = leaks); on acid and alkali lines, re-grease every half month, entered in the ground-joint register.

**3. The Büchner funnel (the standard vacuum-filtration piece)**

The 26.3 prototype is standardized here into a replicable standard piece:

- Funnel body: a 26.1 heavy-wall glass funnel (mouth 60–80 mm) with a shoulder ledge in the lower section of the cavity to carry the plate;
- Porous ceramic plate: a round plate fired by the potter, diameter = funnel mouth minus 3–5 mm, thickness 5–7 mm; 19–37 holes arrayed in concentric rings, hole diameter 2–3 mm, hole spacing 8–10 mm; after firing, grind the plate face flat with fine grit on a flat plate (an uneven plate = the filtrate runs to skewed holes);
- Assembly and glue seal: seat the ceramic plate into the shoulder with the joint gap controlled at 1–2 mm and seal with tung-oil-lime putty (shade-dry 3 days; for strong-acid filtration switch to a poured sulfur-cement joint, mixed fresh and used at once, and stowed back per the 26.7 zoning rules); lay one layer of filter cloth on the plate face (filter paper is better — same lineage as the 26.3 hot filtration);
- Matching filter flask: heavy-wall bottle + side nipple to the aspirator (the Chapter 15 rubber-hose line); the flask-mouth stopper uses this section's self-made ground-joint piece.

[Card 26-05 Büchner Funnel] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: heavy-wall funnel (mouth 60–80 mm) | porous ceramic plate (19–37 holes, 2–3 mm diameter; substitute: a drilled, wax-impregnated hardwood board — filters water-based liquids only, short life) | tung-oil-lime glue | filter cloth
2. **Tools required**: fine carborundum and a lapping plate (Chapter 9) | plate-cutting saw frame | aspirator and rubber hose (Chapter 15)
3. **Operation sequence and control points**: grind the plate face flat → trial-fit and set the gap (1–2 mm) → mix the glue and seal the joint all the way around → shade-dry 3 days → lay the filter cloth → mount on flask and trial-draw. **Control point: after sealing, trial-draw a vacuum — the seal counts only when not one bubble shows at the joint edge**
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: hole diameter 2–3 mm — calibration: suction-filter a fine-sand suspension; no sand in the filtrate = hole size passes; glue dries through in 3 days — calibration: a fingerprint pressed into the joint glue leaves no mark
5. **In-process observation items**: negative-pressure hold (min), filtration rate (drops/s), filtrate clarity — entered on the vessel card
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: trial-draw with no bubbles and clear filtrate = pass; bubbles at the joint edge = rework, re-glue and shade-dry again; cracked ceramic plate = scrap, replace the plate (the funnel body may be kept)
7. **Common failures and troubleshooting order**: joint leak (glue not fully cured → gap too wide → glue mixed too thin) → filter cloth punched through (hole-edge burrs not ground off → suction too strong) → filtration rate falling toward zero (plate face clogged; backflush)
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 one successful trial-draw; L2 all three funnels pass the vacuum test; L3 continuous vessel-card records + two spare plates in stores

**4. The wash bottle (bent-tube blow type)**

The "final rinse" tool of reagent-grade work: the bottle holds distilled water; blowing pressurizes it and water jets from the fine tip — the hand never touches the vessel's inner wall, and the 26.11 "uniform water film" criterion is carried all the way through.

- Construction: a wide-mouth bottle of the 500 mL class + a two-hole stopper; the blow tube is short (30 mm standing above the stopper, its inner end hanging above the water surface); the delivery tube is long (inner end within 5 mm of the bottle bottom, outer end drawn to a tip and bent 60–90°, the Chapter 25 tube-bending method);
- Operation: seat the stopper firmly (wet the stopper's rim to tighten the seal); blow gently into the blow tube by mouth or through a hose — the tip gives a fine stream; when rinsing keep the tip 10–20 mm from the vessel wall and sweep in circles along the wall; when idle, hang it tip-up on a rack (tip touching the bench = the wash bottle itself gets dirty);
- Hygiene rules: distilled water only; **change the water daily** (stale water grows a microbial film = washed and still dirty); label the bottle "wash bottle" with the water-change date;
- Troubleshooting order when no water comes: stopper leaks (soap solution on the joints, watch for bubbles) → tip blocked (clear with a probe wire) → water level below the delivery-tube mouth (add water) → upper blow tube blocked.

### 26.13 Systematized Qualitative Cation Detection (the Group Detection Table)

The 26.5 precipitation color table is scattered point checks of "see a color, recognize a face"; this section upgrades it into a group-detection workflow — whether the ore sample is a carbonate, an oxide, or a sulfide, it walks the same path, every step with explicit reagents, colors, interferences, and eliminations. This section self-rates tier two to three: the workflow is reproducible; interpretation must be checked against the standard color-sample tubes (made per 26.5) and a blank control tube.

**Step 1: three-tier sample dissolution grouping (water-soluble → acid-soluble → alkali-soluble)**

Take a fresh sample at each tier (leftover residue must not be repurposed); register the whole process:

| Group | Criterion operation | Group-entry criterion | If insoluble |
|---|---|---|---|
| Water-soluble group | Fresh sample 0.5 g + 10 mL distilled water, stir warm 2 min | More than half dissolves | Residue kept for the next tier |
| Acid-soluble group | Water-insoluble residue + 10 mL dilute hydrochloric acid, added slowly (bubbling = carbonate; add one drop at a time to prevent spitting) | More than half dissolves | Residue kept for the next tier |
| Alkali-soluble group | Acid-insoluble residue + 10 mL caustic lye, boiled 10 min | More than half dissolves (= the amphoteric family: zinc, aluminum) | Inert residue registered and archived (outside this table's scope) |

[Black text] Ignite before acid dissolution: if the sample carries grease/organic matter, first fire it in a crucible to a dull-red edge (about the 500 ℃ tier — **judge the temperature with the Chapter 27 protected-tube thermocouple (≤750 ℃) or temperature cones; the Chapter 25 alcohol thermometer's 78 ℃ ceiling cannot measure this**), let it cool, then dissolve in acid — organic matter wrapping the ore grains = the acid cannot reach them = false negative. This is the number-one source of misjudgment in this workflow.

**Step 2: the group detection table**

Water-soluble group (flame color and specific precipitation, two paths run in parallel):

| Ion | Reagent and operation | Positive | Interference and elimination |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sodium | Flame color (the 26.5 clean-wire method) | Yellow | Sodium is everywhere — count only a yellow flame that is **faint and a single flash**; dilute a concentrated solution 10× with distilled water and retest; beading vessels = wholesale false positive; run a blank control first |
| Potassium | Flame color through blue glass (Chapter 24) | Violet | Sodium yellow drowns violet — blue glass is mandatory; strong yellow with weak violet, record "sodium and potassium coexist" |
| Calcium | Flame color | Brick-red | Coexisting magnesium does not disturb the flame color; weak brick-red = trace calcium, record as presumptive |
| Ammonium | 2 mL caustic lye + gentle heat, damp red-cabbage paper suspended at the tube mouth | Paper turns green | Judge by the paper, not the nose; carbonate in the sample also fizzes on heating — watch the paper, not the bubbles |

Acid-soluble group ("one base, four colors"): take 5 mL of the acid solution and add caustic lye drop by drop to excess, watching the precipitate color as you add:

| Precipitate | Identification | Recheck (eliminating interference) |
|---|---|---|
| Blue | Copper | Drop in ammonia water (made on the spot from ammonium salt + caustic lye) — the blue precipitate dissolving into a **deep-blue, transparent solution** = copper confirmed; insoluble = copper and iron coexist; let settle, decant into separate liquids, and retest each |
| Red-brown | Iron(III) | Red-brown insoluble in excess alkali = iron; spot one drop of another aliquot on a white porcelain plate; a yellow-brown ring = corroborating evidence for iron |
| White, dissolves in excess alkali | Zinc/aluminum (the amphoteric family) | Add ammonia dropwise in two separate tubes: re-precipitation = zinc; no re-precipitation = aluminum; for aluminum, "no flame color" further rules out the other white-precipitate ions |
| White, insoluble in excess alkali | Lead | To another aliquot add dilute hydrochloric acid — white precipitate dissolves hot and crystallizes on cooling = lead confirmed; undissolved carbonate contamination (bubbles with hydrochloric acid) = an artifact, ruled out |
| Black | Sulfide (the lead/copper/iron(II) family) | Dilute hydrochloric acid gives a rotten-egg smell = sulfide confirmed; systematic recheck hooks into the 26.15 hydrogen sulfide line (registration only, not routine) |

Alkali-soluble group (cross-validation): neutralize the alkali solution slowly with dilute hydrochloric acid (acid into alkali, ≤1 drop per second, stirring as you add) to neutral tending acidic, then run "one base, four colors" again — the alkali-soluble group should still yield zinc/aluminum; **both paths agreeing = the conclusion rises to "confirmed"; one path only = record "presumptive"**.

**The detection registration system**: every detection is entered in four columns — sample number, ion (confirmed/presumptive, two levels), supporting observations (referenced against color-sample tube numbers), recheck method. No recheck, no "confirmed."

[Card 26-07 Group Detection of Cations] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: 1 g ore sample | dilute hydrochloric acid, caustic lye, ammonia water (26.6 reagent grade) | distilled water | red-cabbage paper | the standard color-sample tube set (26.5)
2. **Tools required**: a set of test tubes | clean wire (flame tests) | the Chapter 25 balance | alcohol lamp and stand | white porcelain plate
3. **Operation sequence and control points**: ignite → water-soluble test → acid-soluble test → alkali-soluble test → staged detection → recheck → register. **Control point one: fresh sample at every tier; control point two: every color judgment is checked against the color-sample tubes**
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: acid/alkali addition ≤1 drop per second (prevents spitting and wrapping); alkali boil 10 min — calibration: boiling the same residue another 5 min adds <5% more dissolution = enough
5. **In-process observation items**: at each step, precipitate color / solution color / flame color / bubbling, each item recorded against the color-sample tube number
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: both paths agree = confirmed; one path only = presumptive; insoluble in all three = inert residue, registered and archived — not counted as failure
7. **Common failures and troubleshooting order**: false negative (organic wrapping → ignite and redo) → false positive (vessel beading → redo with a blank control) → color-sample tubes faded (duplicate the set every half year)
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 one ore sample taken through the full workflow and registered; L2 three people interpreting the same sample independently reach the same conclusion; L3 continuous detection register + a complete color-sample tube archive

### 26.14 Titration Extended (the Complete Protocol)

The chapter's "titration prototype" is raised here into a protocol that can be taught and audited. Titration answers one question: **exactly how concentrated is this bottle of acid/alkali** — the effective strength of milk of lime, the degree of the lye, the ratio of an acid mix: all get their numbers from it. This section self-rates tier three (protocol + blind-examination system, double assurance).

**1. The burette (the Chapter 25 screw fine-feed in place of a stopcock)**

26.1 has no mass-production capability for glass stopcocks yet, so burette flow control goes through two substitute tiers:

- Coarse-control tier: a short rubber tube on the lower end of the straight tube with a single glass bead inside (bead diameter slightly larger than the tube's bore); press the hose beside the bead with thumb and forefinger to release liquid — unpressed, the bead merely blocks the flow; simple construction, alkali-resistant (ground glass stopcocks seize in alkali service, so alkali lines must take this tier);
- Fine-control tier: over the rubber tube, add the Chapter 25 screw fine-feed clamp — turn in one thread of screw, release one thread of liquid; the near-endpoint half-drop depends entirely on it;
- Tube body: a straight tube with a 25 cm graduated section, graduations by the 26.1 equal-division method but **valued by weighing**: weigh the water delivered from the 0–10 mL section on the Chapter 25 balance (1 g ≈ 1 mL) and record the correction factor on the tube card — one card per tube; readings must not be borrowed across tubes;
- Reading rules: bottom of the meniscus, eye level with it, a white card behind the tube; read once at start and once at end, estimating to one tenth of the smallest division; before use, rinse twice with the liquid to be loaded, 5 mL each time — no rinse = residual water in the tube dilutes the load = concentration systematically low.

**2. Three indicator choices**

| Choice | Indicator | Applicable case | Endpoint color definition |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 5 drops of red-cabbage decoction (same lineage as 26.10) | Clear liquids; strong acid–strong base titrations either direction | Acid into base: green → violet, stop; base into acid: red → violet, stop |
| B | Turmeric paper (external indicator) | Colored/turbid sample solutions | Near the endpoint, dip one drop onto the paper every 0.5 mL; the paper turns orange-brown and does not fade for 30 s = endpoint |
| C | Two-tube color comparison (the precision tier of A) | Official data runs | The endpoint color-sample tube (a neutral color pre-mixed with standard solution) and the titration flask compared side by side on a white background; colors level = endpoint |

**Calibrate batch by batch**: the indicator is vegetable juice; every batch differs in color — for each batch, first run three tubes with a standard solution of known concentration to establish "this batch's endpoint color-sample tube"; using it without calibration = endpoint drift, and the whole batch of data is voided.

**3. Operating procedure (half-drop included)**

1. Fill and zero: after rinsing, fill; open fast for a stretch to expel the bubble at the tip; set the meniscus to the 0 line;
2. In the flask place 10.0 mL of sample solution (counted by drops with the 26.1 dropper, or weighed directly) + 5 drops of indicator + 20 mL distilled water to dilute (a paler color makes the change easier to see);
3. Coarse dripping: about 1 drop per second, swirling as you drip (swirl, do not stir — stirring splashes and spins liquid up the walls); watch the speed at which "color appears then fades"; as the fading slows near the endpoint, slow down;
4. **The half-drop operation**: when the color reaches "half faded, not faded," hold a drop half-formed on the tip and wash it into the flask with the wash bottle (26.12) — a half-drop is about 0.03 mL (a full drop about 0.05 mL; calibrate this tube's drop size by "20 drops ≈ 1 mL"), pressing endpoint precision from "last drop" down to "half-drop" grade;
5. Endpoint decision: color reaches the endpoint tier and **holds 30 s without fading back** = endpoint; record the reading; it fades back = not reached, keep dripping;
6. Parallel discipline: at least three portions of the same sample; the maximum spread among the three volumes consumed must be ≤2% (empirical; enter in the ledger after calibration) before averaging is allowed; a large range = an unsteady hand or a drifting endpoint — re-titrate; forced averaging is not permitted.

**4. The blind-drill method for endpoint judgment**

- The instructor prepares three bottles of "exam solution" from standard solution; the true values are recorded only in the instructor's book;
- The trainee titrates blind: three portions per bottle — a range ≤2% across the three passes the "steady hand" gate; the mean within ≤5% of the true value passes the "true eye" gate;
- **Passing both gates = earning the qualification to produce numbers**; the exam date is entered in the ledger; when analytical data is later in doubt, look up that person's exam record. This is the root of turning "trustworthy data" into an auditable system — it matters more than any instrument.

**5. Standard solution preparation (the primary-standard method)**

- Primary standard: anhydrous soda ash (a Chapter 23 alkali-line product, purified by two recrystallization rounds — it does not take up moisture quickly, its equivalent is a whole number, and it can be weighed out directly on the balance);
- Bake to constant weight: spread a thin layer in a shallow glass dish; place in the drying spot (the residual-heat tier at the furnace mouth; a Chapter 25 thermometer beside the dish records the actual reading; target 100–120 ℃) for 2 h; move into a desiccator (lined with calcined lime, the same material as the 26.1 drying tube), cool to room temperature, weigh; re-bake 1 h and re-weigh — **two weighings differing <1% = constant weight**; a larger difference = it still holds water; keep baking and weighing until it passes;
- Preparation example: weigh 10.6 g of constant-weight soda ash (balance 0.1 g tier); dissolve first in about 800 mL of distilled water; transfer to a 1000 mL measuring vessel; top up to the mark drop by drop with the wash bottle; stopper, invert and shake ten times — this gives 0.100 mol/L-class standard solution (10.6 g ÷ 106 g/mol = 0.100 mol, made up to 1 L; the concentration rests on the weighing record, not on volume precision);
- Standardizing an acid with the standard solution: take 10.0 mL of the acid to be standardized + indicator; titrate with the standard alkali to the endpoint, consuming 9.6 mL — the acid concentration = 0.100 × 9.6 ÷ 10.0 = **0.096 mol/L**; transcribe to the label (26.11 six elements) and the concentration ledger together.

[Card 26-06 Standard Solution Preparation] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: anhydrous soda ash (two recrystallization rounds, baked to constant weight) | distilled water (the 26.6 solvent zero)
2. **Tools required**: the Chapter 25 balance (zero-checked before use) | glass weighing boats (26.16) | a 1000 mL measuring vessel (weighing-corrected) | a desiccator | a thermometer
3. **Operation sequence and control points**: two recrystallization rounds → bake 2 h → weigh cool and dry → re-bake 1 h and re-weigh → make to volume → shake to homogeneity → apply the six-element label. **Control point: the constant-weight criterion unmet, no making to volume**
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: constant weight = two weighings differing <1%; make-to-volume water temperature in the 15–25 ℃ tier (making to volume hot = the level drops on cooling = concentration reads high)
5. **In-process observation items**: every weighing value, drying-spot temperature, make-to-volume water temperature, number of inversions
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: constant weight reached = pass; difference ≥1% = rework, keep baking; soda ash caked and darkened = scrap, back to recrystallization
7. **Common failures and troubleshooting order**: moisture picked up before weighing (cool through in the desiccator, then weigh) → filled past the mark (discard and remake; sucking out to level is strictly forbidden) → insufficient homogenization (verify ten inversions)
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 one bottle of standard solution prepared with a weighing record; L2 three bottles prepared independently, concentrations within ≤2% of each other; L3 continuous concentration ledger + retained sample bottles in the archive

### 26.15 Three Gas-Preparation Lines (Generation–Collection–Fullness Check–Tail Gas)

26.4 established the general methods of collection over water and the three-property tests; this section writes the three common gas lines, each as a **four-phase standard**. Universal Iron Law: the generator is never left unattended; all combustible gas is purity-verified first (26.4); **the apparatus must not be dismantled until the tail-gas phase is complete** — withdraw the delivery tube first, remove the flame second; reversed order = suck-back = burst tube.

**1. The oxygen line (heated saltpeter)**

- Generation: load saltpeter (Chapter 23 product) as a thin layer of 20–30 g in a hard glass tube/retort; heat with a gentle flame, moving along the full length — heating too hard = molten spatter; criterion: the delivery tube bubbles in the water at **uniform speed, no bumping**;
- Collection: over water (26.4); when a bottle fills, cover its mouth with a ground-glass plate underwater and lift it out, and continue into a second bottle; log every bottle (number + purity-check record entered in the register);
- Fullness check: underwater, watch bubbles spill from the bottle mouth; after withdrawal, **a glowing splint inserted relights** (relighting = oxygen confirmed; keep on file);
- Tail gas: oxygen is non-toxic and no absorber is set up, but the **flame-out order is an Iron Law**: withdraw the delivery tube from the water first, remove the lamp second; the residue in the tube (conversion products) is scraped out after cooling, registered, and recovered — never flushed into a ditch (nitrates into water = well-water contamination; location registered on the Chapter 2 hydrological map).

**2. The carbon dioxide line (limestone + acid)**

- Generation: a two-neck generating bottle (26.4) charged with limestone lumps (broad-bean to walnut grade, 50–100 g); dilute hydrochloric acid poured slowly through the side neck — **dilute sulfuric acid is forbidden on this line**: sulfuric acid forms an insoluble crust (calcium sulfate) on the limestone surface, and the encrusted stone = the reaction stalls on its own; this is the number-one failure source. Replenishment: gas weak, add acid; acid spent, add stone — alternate to keep it going;
- Collection: air displacement (CO₂ is heavier than air) — delivery tube down to the bottle bottom, mouth up; the gas pools at the bottom and pushes the air out; for high purity, switch to collection over water (dissolution loss traded for purity; register the trade-off);
- Fullness check: a burning splint probes the bottle mouth and goes out at once = full; the limewater cloudiness test (26.4) kept on file;
- Tail gas: CO₂ is barely toxic but suffocating — generating continuously indoors for more than 30 min requires ventilation; pass the excess through a limewater absorption bottle (the 26.10 arrangement) and collect the white precipitate along the way (calcium carbonate, registered on file).

**3. The hydrogen sulfide line (iron sulfide + acid) — [RED LINE: registration only, not routine]**

- [Black text] H₂S is lethally toxic and **anesthetizes the sense of smell** — smelling nothing does not mean it is absent; it means the poisoning is already deep. This line starts only when 26.13 detection genuinely needs a sulfide recheck: two-person teams, a ventilated station, **iron sulfide limited to 2 g per run**, the whole run executed per the 26.10 absorption table;
- Generation: iron sulfide granules (lumps fused from iron filings + sulfur at 7:4, smashed and sieved) in the generating bottle; dilute hydrochloric acid dripped in to keep the gas coming slowly;
- Collection: generally no bottle collection — the delivery tube connects directly to the detection test tube for drop-by-drop use; when sample gas is genuinely needed, collect one bottle briefly by air displacement (H₂S is slightly heavier than air; mouth up);
- Fullness check: lead acetate paper (paper soaked in lead-salt solution and dried) hung at the bottle mouth — **the paper blackens fast = full**;
- Tail gas: two ash-lye absorption bottles in series (the 26.10 arrangement on file), changed on saturation; before dismantling, blow fresh air through the line for 2 min to push the residual gas into the absorption bottles. **Any single violation of the red line (over-quantity, solo work, no absorption) = the line shut down for one week and a full-team post-mortem.**

### 26.16 Sample Weighing and Reagent Hygiene (Four Rules Against Contamination)

All the precision of reagent grade loses in the end to one word: "dirty." Four rules, each with its action and its criterion:

1. **The weighing-boat system**: never weigh samples on paper (paper absorbs moisture and sheds fibers = error sources); use a glass weighing boat — cut from flat glass to 30×15 mm with the edges rounded over a flame (flattened scrap from tube drawing works), fired clean of grease before use, stored in a dry dish; the weighing method = weigh the empty boat's tare first, then "boat + sample," and subtract for the net sample weight; boats are washed by the 26.11 three stages, each boat dedicated to its use, boat number matched to the record page;
2. **Dedicated spatulas**: one dedicated spatula per reagent bottle (glass/porcelain spatulas preferred; metal spatulas limited to stainless steel, acid-washed and passivated), with a colored thread tied on the handle matching the bottle's color code — **one spatula, one bottle, never cross bottles**; one cross-bottle event = both bottles treated as contaminated (recheck or downgrade); this is a "whole-bottle scrapping" grade violation;
3. **The stopper-down rule**: on removing a stopper, set it at once inverted in a bench stopper stand (head up) — head down = bench dirt into the bottle; when done, **re-stopper immediately**, no "in a minute"; before returning it, glance at the head against the light; beading = wipe clean, then return;
4. **Excess never returns to the bottle**: reagent poured out must never be poured back — one drop back = the whole bottle downgraded; the excess goes into the waste jar (the 26.7 system) or into a "downgraded bottle" (label moved to the downgraded tier, for industrial-grade operations); hand-feel training for dispensing: with the 26.1 dropper, practice "take exactly as many drops as you need"; a pass = within two drops.

The four rules drilled as one sentence: **contamination can ruin a bottle of reagent without spending a penny; discipline spends one minute to save a whole batch of product.**

## Safety Boundaries

- All heating/flue-gas operations at a ventilated station; closed systems keep a pressure-relief opening;
- Acid into water, never water into acid; HF and white phosphorus forbidden without protection; the saltpeter–sulfur–charcoal triad stored separately;
- Combustible gas purity-verified before ignition; the gas generator never left unattended;
- The hydrogen sulfide line is registration-only, not routine: two people, a ventilated station, iron sulfide ≤2 g per run, two-stage absorption in place; one violation = the line down for one week with a post-mortem;
- Any "first-time" reaction is demonstrated first at the minimum quantity (fingernail scale) at a ventilated station; scale up only after nothing abnormal.

## Acceptance Criteria

- **L1**: the full condenser–distillation train runs; the alcohol cut fraction is combustible, heads and tails logged separately; hydrogen collected over water, purity-verified and ignited; one displacement copper cementation completed.
- **L2**: distilled water supplied continuously; the same salt through three recrystallizations with the melting range narrowing each time; oil of vitriol made in small batches with concentration registered; the labeling–zoning–waste three systems running with zero violation records.
- **L3**: reagent-grade products (an acid, alcohol, two salts) supplied stably with continuous purity archives; one "experiment → scale up one tier" route walked through per the 26.8 discipline with a yield ledger; zero accidents on the high-hazard list.
