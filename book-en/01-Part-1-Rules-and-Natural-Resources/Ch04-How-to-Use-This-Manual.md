### 4.1 Dependency Levels and Prerequisite Chapter Numbering

**The number is the dependency: Chapter N may cite only results numbered below N.** This is the book-wide mechanical criterion against tool anachronism, and the reader's first check.

**How the reader uses it (three actions):**

1. **Scan prerequisites before reading**: open any chapter and read its "Minimum Prerequisites" table first, checking the chapter number in the "Source" column line by line. Any unsatisfied line: go back and fill it, then return.
2. **Watch citations while reading**: every tool is tagged with its source (chapter number or General Outline document number) on first appearance in the body text. Any untagged tool you see goes into the "jump register" (see Section 4.6).
3. **Check the lists after reading**: cross-check the chapter's List A (existing tools) and List B (new tools) against your physical stock. Items on List A you should already own; anything missing is prerequisite debt.

**"Preview" versus "available" (the most misread rule in the book):** the manual permits writing "this capability will be established in Chapter N" — that is a preview (a forward declaration), and preview items **may NOT be used as existing**. The test is very simple: which chapter's List B does it appear in, and have you accepted that chapter to the corresponding level? Example: Chapter 2 may preview "calcination verification will be backfilled in Chapter 12", but Chapter 2 itself may not burn anything.

**Reader-created content follows the same rule**: any process record you add for your own settlement must also carry a source chapter number and is audited by the same rules. What these rules protect is not the manual's authority but the traceability of your own dependency chain.

### 4.2 The Four Dependencies: Tools, Materials, Energy, and Knowledge

Every "stuck" state falls into one of the four dependency classes. The check action and gap handling for each:

| Dependency class | Check action (what to ask) | What to check | Gap handling |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tool dependency | Are the required tools physically present and in good condition? | Each chapter's A/B lists + your tool archive | Look up the tool's manufacturing chapter and build it first; if no substitute exists, that chapter has not met its start conditions |
| Material dependency | Are the required raw materials enough for this run plus two trial runs? | Resource map + reserve ledger (Chapter 2, Section 3.6) | Check that chapter's alternative-route section; if reserves are short, gather more first |
| Energy dependency | For the required heat/mechanical energy, are fuel and power sufficient? | Fuel entries + energy entries (Section 2.5) | Check upstream in the energy lineage (fire, Chapter 6; hydropower, Chapter 21) |
| Knowledge dependency | Without the manual, can you write out the principle and the key parameter windows? | The chapter body + your recitation test (Section 3.7) | Re-read + oral examination; a knowledge gap must not be replaced by "just trying it" |

**Combined example — the four-dependency rehearsal for Chapter 6 (fire-making):**

- Tools: no hard prerequisite (the bow drill belongs to Section 7.6; Chapter 6 friction fire-lighting uses a fire bow board makeable by hand — the Chapter 6 text governs);
- Materials: fire-starting material (fine dry grass, inner birch bark), tinder, fuel (the standing deadwood entry in Section 2.5);
- Energy: human power;
- Knowledge: the three elements of combustion, fire-starting material grading, ventilation principles.

With all four columns filled, Chapter 6 meets its start conditions. Any chapter can have a start card filled out this way.

### 4.3 Core Routes and Alternative Routes

The **core route** = the manual's default mainline order; its skeleton is the five critical chains (from General Outline G04):

1. Stone tools → woodworking tools → simple machines (Chapters 5, 7, 9)
2. Fire → furnaces and kilns → refractory materials → metal smelting (Chapters 6, 12, 16–18)
3. Metal tools → measurement → precision machining → machine tools (Chapters 19–22)
4. Mechanical power → electromagnetism → generators → electrical power systems (Chapters 21, 28–32)
5. Glass and chemistry → material purification → semiconductor processing → integrated circuits (Chapters 14, 23–26, 33–35)

**The fixed annotation format for alternative routes (Iron Law 5; no element may be omitted):** performance loss / resource cost / applicability boundary / final bottleneck. Example (preview): a flint-free region takes the quartzite/obsidian stone-tool route (Chapter 5) — performance loss: poorer edge retention or excessive brittleness; resource cost: collection from greater distances; applicability boundary: covers only chopping/scraping-class tools; final bottleneck: cannot provide fine-machining edges, so the metal route (Chapters 16–18) must be entered as early as possible.

**Three-step route selection:**

1. Fill in your current state row by row using the environmental assessment table (General Outline G04 part four, ten rows);
2. Verify each row against the resource map (Chapter 2);
3. Cross the two tables to fix this settlement's mainline and the chapters to advance or delay. Principle: **environmental differences change not only material sources but also the order of development** (example: hydropower-rich regions may advance some machine chapters; fuel-poor regions must push the glass and metallurgy chapters back and solve energy first).

**Alternative-route usage rule:** once a substitute goes into service, **that chapter's acceptance criteria must be re-validated; the core route's acceptance values must not be carried over**. Substitute materials almost always behave differently (shrinkage, thermal stability, strength); carrying over old criteria misses real defects.

### 4.4 How to Judge Whether a Chapter May Start

**The three start checks (one column each; fill the start card):**

1. **Prerequisite completeness check**: verify item by item against the Section 4.2 four dependencies; all source chapters must have met their own acceptance standards (at least L1; safety-related chapters follow their own stricter requirements).
2. **Resource margin check**: from the chapter's "resource cost", estimate the materials and fuel for this run plus two failures. **Do not start when the margin is below twice planned consumption** (rule of thumb: at the L1 stage trial-and-error rates are generally above fifty percent; revise in the field from failure records).
3. **Manpower and time check**: the chapter's work-hour estimate ÷ available people = schedule; chapters exceeding one continuous work season must be split into segments.

**"Can be done but should not be done"**: passing the three checks ≠ it should be done immediately. Re-run the priority framework (General Outline G04 part three): what gets blocked if this is not built? Is there a cheaper substitute? What is the opportunity cost of the manpower at this moment? Answer the five questions of Chapter 1 Section 1.5 one by one before starting work.

**Pause criterion:** the same failure mode appearing 3 times in a row (rule of thumb) → mandatory pause; re-examine prerequisites (did the material batch change? are tools worn? a knowledge gap?). Consecutive same-mode failures are almost always a prerequisite problem, not a craft problem.

**Reader's chapter completeness self-check list (core deliverable; tick item by item; one missing item means incomplete):**

**(A) Before starting**
- [ ] This chapter's start card has been filled out (four-dependency check + resource margin + manpower and time)
- [ ] All prerequisite chapters have met their acceptance standards, with levels recorded
- [ ] This chapter's safety boundary has been read, and the prohibition conditions for dangerous operations excerpted somewhere visible on site

**(B) During the work**
- [ ] Every operation is logged in the technical log format (date/parameters/result/failure mode)
- [ ] Failures are filed under the five classifications (see Section 4.5); an unfiled failure counts as never having happened and must never be repeated
- [ ] Parameters are recorded by the window method (minimum usable / typical / maximum usable)

**(C) After completion**
- [ ] Every acceptance criterion of this chapter has been physically measured; "it looks done" was not accepted as a substitute
- [ ] The level reached (L1/L2/L3) has been judged honestly and recorded; L2 requires evidence of 3 consecutive conforming pieces
- [ ] New tools on this chapter's List B have been numbered into the tool archive, with repair/rebuild paths on damage written down
- [ ] List C (unresolved dependencies) has been copied into your own dependency map, with target chapters marked
- [ ] Relevant resource-map entries have been backfilled with verification status (Chapter 2 backfill rules)
- [ ] The next chapter's start card can now be begun from this

### 4.5 Failure Records, Process Improvement, and Version Management

**Technical log format (one volume per chapter, on clay tablet or carved wood, carved column by column):**

| Column | Content |
|---|---|
| Date/operator | day name + person name |
| Goal | what this entry's operation is meant to achieve |
| Prerequisite check | are all four columns of the start card green (if a gap is only discovered now, record it honestly) |
| Operation record | working parameters (length/mass/time/temperature anchor readings) — recorded per the four benchmark classes of Chapter 3 |
| Result | finished-piece dimensions, phenomena observed, pass status of each acceptance criterion |
| Failure classification | one of the five classifications (below) |
| Improvement hypothesis | which variable the next entry will change (one only) |

**The five failure classifications (troubleshoot in this order, eliminating class by class):**

1. **Missing prerequisite**: tool out of condition, different material batch, insufficient energy — back to the start card;
2. **Parameter out of bounds**: operating parameters ran outside the process card's window — back to the Section 3.7 parameter windows; where no window exists yet, run window trials first;
3. **Material difference**: same-named material from a different source behaves differently — back to Chapter 2 for retesting; split the entries;
4. **Operating error**: step order or technique deviated from the process card — supplement the step descriptions via the recitation test;
5. **Wrong criterion**: the acceptance criterion itself does not match the real use — revise the criterion field of the process card and bump the version.

**Improvement loop (single-variable method):** hypothesis → change one variable only → record → if it works, issue a new version of the process card; if not, revert the variable and void the hypothesis on the record.

**Version naming rule:** process name + version number + date index (example: pace-survey calibration v2 / Day 45). New and old cards are linked both ways by "supersedes"; old cards are archived, not destroyed. Oral process changes are forbidden (settled in Section 3.7; this is the enforcement point).

**Three anti-patterns (self-check):**

- Changing several variables at once — attribution becomes impossible; you turn the experiment into a gamble;
- Retrying without records — 3 failures repeated with the same method triggers the Section 4.4 pause criterion;
- Mistaking a fluke for a process — anything not reproduced 3 times gets no card and does not enter List B.

### 4.6 How to Recognize Unreasonable Technical Jumps

A jump = some step invoking something that does not exist. Readers audit **any** technical material with the six signals below — including this manual, external oral knowledge, and other settlements' processes — and register findings on discovery:

| Signal | Test question |
|---|---|
| 1 Tool with no source | Does this tool appear nowhere in Lists A/B and the chapter numbers? |
| 2 Precision with no source | It says "accurate to such-and-such" — in which chapter are the datum, the measuring instruments, and the inspection method established? |
| 3 Broken material chain | It simply says "use steel/glass/reagents" — where is the manufacturing chapter? (Iron Law 3) |
| 4 One piece claimed as a system | "Mastered" is declared with a finished piece but no maintenance, no spares, no resupply plan |
| 5 Acceptance by appearance | The success criterion is "it looks right", with no functional or repeatability test (Iron Law 4) |
| 6 Substitution at no cost | The substitute is presented as equal to the original, with no performance loss or boundary statement (Iron Law 5) |

**Audit exercises (two items; readers answer for themselves):**

- Example 1: "Burn lime in an iron pot." — Which chapter does the iron pot come from? (Signals 1 + 3: ironware comes only after Chapter 18, and pot-class castings carry their own prerequisites; the correct vessel route for lime burning is in Chapters 12–13.)
- Example 2: "Cut six timbers to equal length precisely per the drawing." — In which chapter are the datum and measuring instruments behind "precisely" established? (Signal 2: the length datum is Chapter 3; copying a dimension relies on the standard rod and notch comparison, not on the word "precise".)

**Handling flow:** a jump is found → write it into the "jump register" clay tablet (date/source/signal type/missing dependency) → decide chain repair (return to the missing chapter) or reroute (take an alternative route) → if the entry points at this manual, handle it under the conflict rules at the head of General Outline G01: revise with the Iron Laws and prerequisite numbering as the authority.

**What the register is for:** the jump register is the settlement's technical immune system. Left unmaintained, within three months oral experience quietly mixes in "sounds plausible" anachronisms — and by then nobody remembers where they came from.

### 4.7 The On-Site Use Cycle of the Process Card (new in v2: the complete course of one job)

From v2 on, the book's key operations are delivered as **process cards** (numbered like Card 20-01); a card can be copied onto a single sheet and carried to the workstation. One complete on-site job runs in five steps:

1. **Pre-start verification**: without reciting the body text, run this chapter's "pre-start verification" table and tick each line — no start unless the criteria pass. Whichever item is missing, go back to that chapter and fill it; never start impaired;
2. **Operate by the card**: follow the card's field order (input materials → tools → operations and control points → parameters). Steps marked ◎ are hand-feel steps — execute them by the practice method and compensation criteria given on the card; do not improvise by feel;
3. **In-process recording**: for every item on the card's "in-process observation entries", make one entry per band (color/sound/reading), on the spot and in the original words — any observation added after knock-off must be marked "added later";
4. **Three-way disposition by criteria**: results are sorted into "pass / rework / scrap"; "close enough" is not allowed. Rework follows the card's rework path; scrapped pieces are handled as the card directs (mostly kept as samples in the failure sample library);
5. **Archive and transcribe**: wherever this batch's measured parameters differ from the card's default values, **transcribe them into the ledger after field calibration (Chapter 43)** — the next batch consults the ledger, not memory. Retire the worn card to the archive.

**This cycle is the field form of Iron Law 6 (every acceptance leaves evidence).** A job that skips step 5 has only "got the work done", not "established a capability" — the next batch must grope forward from your starting point all over again.

### 4.8 Minimal Drafting Method (a field, board-carried version of three views, datums, and tolerances)

This section is the **temporary-carrier version** of Chapter 42 (engineering drawing and version control): paper only arrives in Chapter 41, but from Chapter 5 on you already need to draw — on wooden boards, bark, and clay tablets. The rules are identical to formal drafting; only the carrier differs.

**Three views (front/plan/side)**: draw the same object's outline from three directions, under the three mnemonics "aligned, flush, equal width" — the front view and the plan view align vertically; the front and side views are flush left to right; the plan and side views have equal width. Scribe on clay tablets with a scriber (the Chapter 5 pointed tool); on wooden boards, lay out with a charcoal stick and set the lines with a carving knife.

**Datum first**: the first stroke on any drawing is always the datum (a face or an edge); every other dimension is measured from it. Chain relaying is forbidden — chains accumulate error; measured from the datum, errors do not stack (the forerunner of the Chapter 22 tolerance idea).

**Nominal size plus upper/lower deviation**: every dimension is written as "target value + tolerance", e.g., "shaft diameter 20, acceptable 19.8–20.2". When a clay tablet has no room, use tally marks (groups of five with a bar, the Chapter 3 method). **A drawing that gives only target values without tolerances is judged a non-conforming drawing under Section 4.6 Signal 5.**

**Scale and drawing number**: use only integer scales (1:1, 2:1, 1:2; Chapter 3 discipline); carve the drawing number (date + serial, the Chapter 3 archive method) and the drafter's mark in the lower-right corner of every drawing. Wooden-board drawings are branded at a corner (scorched mark from a fire awl) to prevent erasure.

◎ Drawing hand-feel: lay out straight lines by pressing a taut hemp cord into the surface (long lines); use the edge of a straightedge for short lines (the rule after the Chapter 20 three-ruler mutual check). Practice method: draw the same object three times and overlay the three sheets — outlines that coincide pass.

### 4.9 Minimal Record Carriers (writing surfaces before paper)

The paper of Chapter 41 is an **upgrade route**, not the starting point. Starting carriers, in order:

| Carrier | How made | Advantage | Loss risk and countermeasure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clay tablet (Chapter 3, Section 3.6) | Flatten local clay and inscribe; shade-dry | Zero cost, inscribable | Water destroys it — store on high racks under cover; transcribe before the rainy season |
| Wooden board | Chapter 7 planed thin stock; charcoal stick/carving knife | Water-resistant (when inscribed) | Wood checks, writing wears faint — carve, not just write, anything important |
| Bark (birch inner layer) | Stripped with the Chapter 6 tinder source | Bendable, light | Brittle, cracks — store in rolls |
| Leather (offcuts) | Chapter 15 tanned offcuts | Durable, rollable | Costly — record only "permanent-class" entries (units-charter class) |

Transcription upgrade chain: **temporary carrier → fired hardening (Chapter 11 ceramicized clay tablets) → paper (Chapter 41)**. "Permanent-class" content recorded on temporary carriers (units charter, thread standard, batch ledger) must be transcribed when the carrier upgrades, with the old pieces archived — no "rewriting in passing during a carrier change"; go through the loan register of Section 4.10.

### 4.10 Minimal Archive System (three things: numbering, duplicates, loans)

The complete archive system is Chapter 43; from Chapter 5 on only three things are required, and they are the seed of the Chapter 43 system:

1. **Numbering**: every record piece is carved with "date + serial" (the Chapter 3 method) + attribution (which chapter, which card). An unnumbered record piece counts as scrap paper;
2. **Two copies, two places**: permanent-class records are always kept in duplicate, stored apart (two buildings, or a house and a cellar) — a single-copy record equals no record;
3. **Loan register**: record pieces do not leave the premises. If one is needed on site, copy a working duplicate and take that; the master stays in the cabinet, carved with an "on loan" mark — **a record whose master is lost counts as that process demoted back to the first tier** (a pass without evidence is called an impression; Iron Law 6).

**Interface to later chapters**: Chapter 41's paper and ink deliver the "durable and duplicable" carrier; Chapter 42 upgrades this section's "tolerance notation" into full tolerance annotation and version control; Chapter 43 expands this section's "three things" into tiered archives, a negative-results library, and master–apprentice transmission. This section is their root — written into Chapter 4 because from Chapter 5 on, every acceptance uses them.

## Safety Boundary

- This is a methods chapter with no physical operations. The one hard clause: **in any chapter whose self-check list section (A) has not passed, operations involving fire, high temperature, heavy loads, deep water, or falls are barred from starting** — reading yes, hands-on no.
- No one (including readers with modern knowledge) may claim exemption from the three start checks on the strength of "I understand the principle"; the knowledge dependency is checked precisely by the recitation test (Section 4.2).

## Acceptance Criteria

- **L1 proof-of-principle**: for any chapter read, state orally its four dependencies, its current level, and what the next level lacks; for any external suggestion, say which of the six signals it hits.
- **L2 repeatable manufacture**: actually walk one subsequent chapter (recommend Chapter 5 or 6) with the three start checks + the completeness self-check list, with complete technical log and process card traces, re-verified item by item by a second person against the list.
- **L3 sustainable production**: the settlement runs it as an institution — every process carries a version number and a supersession chain; the jump register has a maintainer and a quarterly review; one new member reproduces a level-one process (e.g., pace-survey calibration or the clay settling test) from the archives alone (process card + log + symbol table) and passes acceptance.
