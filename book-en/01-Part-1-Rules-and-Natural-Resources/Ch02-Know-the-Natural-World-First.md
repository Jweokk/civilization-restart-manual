### 2.1 The Difference Between Rocks, Minerals, and Ores

**Three engineering definitions:**

| Concept | Definition | Question to ask during the survey |
| -- | --- | --- |
| Rock | A natural aggregate of multiple minerals (granite, sandstone, limestone) | Can it serve, as a whole, as building material / millstone / dimension stone? |
| Mineral | A single natural compound or element (quartz, calcite, hematite) | What are its streak, hardness, and luster? |
| Ore | Rock in which the grade of some useful component is high enough to be worth recovering | Is the amount picked up on site worth hauling back? |

Key insight: \*\*ordinary rocks are everywhere, ores are rare.\*\* A reddish-brown stone is not the same as iron ore. Criteria always take priority over color impressions.

**First-tier clues (naked-eye, ordered by reliability):**

1. Streak: the color of the powder a sample leaves when scratched on a coarse quartz-sandstone slab. Streak color is far more stable than the color of the bulk exterior.
2. Fracture form: conchoidal fracture (an arced surface like the inside of a shell) is characteristic of homogeneous glassy stones such as flint and obsidian — these are the first choice for knapped stone tools in Chapter 5. Flat fractures and bedding planes indicate sedimentary rock.
3. Hardness grades (see 2.6): the fingernail (about 2.5) and quartz (about 7) bracket out three grades.
4. Luster: metallic / vitreous / greasy / earthy.
5. Crystal form: for crystals with visible faces (hexagonal-prism quartz, cubic pyrite), record the shape.

**Common streak table (memorize 5 rows on site to get started):**

| Mineral | Bulk appearance | Streak | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hematite | Red-brown to black | Red-brown | The most common source of iron (Chapter 18) |
| Magnetite | Black, metallic luster | Black | Markedly dense; iron source |
| Limonite | Yellow-brown, earthy | Yellow-brown | Hydrous iron oxide; iron source |
| Pyrite | Golden yellow, cubic crystals | Greenish-black | "Fool's gold": harder than gold (about 6); gold is soft (about 2.5–3) with a golden streak |
| Graphite | Black, greasy, stains the fingers | Black, shiny | Extremely soft (1–2); used later for lubrication and marking |

**Failure modes:**

1. Memorizing a list of mineral names with no evidence of occurrence. Countermeasure: the manual gives only criteria; "whether there is ore under your feet" must be answered by the field survey of 2.7.
2. Being fooled by bulk color (pyrite taken for gold, red sandstone taken for hematite). Countermeasure: make the streak before drawing conclusions.
3. Taking a single pretty crystal for an "ore vein". Countermeasure: records must state the distribution extent (see the 2.7 table); for a single crystal, honestly write "scarce" in the "workable amount" column.

### 2.2 Soils, Clays, Sands and Gravels, and Sediments

Clay is the common raw material of pottery (Chapter 11), clay tablets (3.6), and construction (Chapter 13); it has the highest priority.

**Four field tests for clay (all bare-handed + water):**

1. Wet-roll test: knead a knuckle-sized soil sample thoroughly with water and roll it into a strip on the palm. It can wrap a full loop around the little finger without breaking = good plasticity, high clay-particle content; it snaps as soon as bent = sandy or low clay content.
2. Finger-rub test: smear a wet sample flat on the fingertip pad. Slick like paste = fine; an obvious gritty feel = sandy. Record the feel in three grades: paste / powder / sand.
3. Settling test: fill a natural container (a split large bamboo segment, a large shell, a folded bark bucket) with water, drop in a knuckle-sized soil sample, disperse it, and let stand (duration counted in heartbeats, see 3.1). Coarse sand sinks within a few beats; silt needs several quarter-hours to clear; the water is still turbid after most of a day = fine clay particles present. The settling-time boundaries are empirical values and must be field-calibrated: settle a sample of known river sand alongside as a control.
4. Dry-shrink test: press the wet sample into a palm-sized clay cake and shade-dry it for two days. Compare the scribed marks: large shrinkage, cracked into several pieces = extremely high clay content; used directly for pottery it will crack, so sand must be mixed in (the recipe is handled in Chapter 11); almost no cracking or shrinking = sandy, insufficient plasticity.

**Sands, gravels and sediments:**

* River sand: rounded grains, usually fairly clean; sea sand contains salt (salty taste, trace-level confirmation) and must be rinsed with fresh water before later use in mortar and glass (the rinsing step belongs to Chapters 13 and 14; here only the "contains salt" property is recorded).

* Quartz sand (mainly white, vitreous-luster grains): enter it on the map and tag it "high priority" — it is the raw material of the glass route (Chapter 14).

* Reading depositional environments: river bends, lake deposits, old channels, wind-deposited loess layers — zones with thick and fine soil are candidates for clay and farmland (Chapter 10) alike; tick both columns when recording.

**Failure mode:** declaring "there is clay" after testing only one spot. Countermeasure: take at least 3 samples along the same valley and run the four tests on each, recording the entries separately — clay quality can change drastically within a few dozen paces.

### 2.3 Wood, Bark, Fibers, Resins, and Natural Binders

**One record per candidate tree; survey dimensions:**

| Dimension | Field judgment |
| ---- | --- |
| Species grouping | Deciduous / evergreen; conifer / broadleaf. Conifers often have resin |
| Trunk form | Straight length of the trunk (measured in paces), curvature, fork height — decides whether straight stock can be taken |
| Material feel | Weigh same-hand-length broken branches in three grades: light (paulownia types) / medium / heavy (oak types); heavy ones later become hammers and axles (Chapter 7) |
| Grain | Along-grain split test: drive a picked-up stone along the grain into a crack; splits easily along the grain = good for taking boards / strips |
| Moisture state | Standing deadwood (dead but not fallen) is drier than fallen wood and burns more readily than green wood — first-choice fuel (Chapter 6) |
| Bast | Peel off a strip of inner bark; does not break when pulled by hand = bast fiber usable (rope candidate, verified in Chapter 7) |
| Exudates | Resin and gum oozing from trunk wounds: pinch for softness, smell, record color (binder and waterproofing candidates, verified in Chapter 8) |

**Other fiber sources:** grass stems (the long, tough ones), rattan, tree rootlets, animal hair and sinew (if there is game). At this stage only the hand-test result "does not break when pulled" is recorded; twisting into rope is the craft of Chapter 7.

**Sustainable harvest rules (starting values):** in each woodland's first harvest year, take no more than one tenth of the trees (empirical value, must be field-calibrated: mark 20 sample trees, record the regrowth year by year, then revise the cap). Standing deadwood and fallen wood are harvested first. Each woodland gets its own separate entry on the resource map, including an "annual harvest cap" column.

**Failure mode:** the resource map records only "there is forest" without species composition and stock volume, and three months later it turns out the good timber species numbers just seven trees. Countermeasure: the distribution and estimate columns must state pace-count dimensions and the dominant species.

### 2.4 Water, Salt, Limestone, Sulfur, and Other Common Raw Materials

**Water (a separate entry for each source):**

* Type: spring / stream / river / lake / rain / snow. Springs and live streams take priority.

* Record: location, seasonality (if unknown, mark "observe for one year"), turbidity (clear / slightly turbid / turbid), odor, water-temperature feel, downstream pollution signs (carcasses, algal blooms, bad smells).

* Drinking-water criterion for this stage (very limited, stated honestly): live water + clear + odorless + no signs of human or animal carcasses upstream. **This is not disinfection** — pathogenic microorganisms are invisible. Until the boiling-disinfection capability is established in Chapter 6, standing water and shallow-pool water is in principle not drunk.

**Salt:** rock-salt outcrops (white / colorless, salty taste, soft enough to crumble between the fingers), salt springs (white salt efflorescence by the spring mouth), salt lakes, seashores. Taste judgment is limited to trace-level confirmation of samples already identified as salty; tasting unknown samples is forbidden. Use preview: food preservation (Chapter 10), the starting point of the chemical industry (Chapter 23). Salt-poor areas are honestly marked "none" on the map.

**Limestone (criterion set for a no-acid era):** gray-white to bluish-gray; can be scratched into powder marks by a quartz block (hardness grade 3–4, below quartz's 7); fracture fairly flat; **often contains shell or coral fossils; often occurs together with karst caves and spring-mouth travertine (loose, porous white lime sinter)** — fossils and travertine are the most valuable indirect evidence. Final verification relies on calcination: lighter after burning, gives off heat and slakes into powder with water (requires the fire of Chapter 6 and the kiln of Chapter 12; backfilled then).

**Sulfur:** yellow, brittle, easily crumbled to powder, distinctive smell; found in volcanic and hot-spring regions. Non-volcanic regions often have no sulfur — record honestly; the lack of sulfur is a hard boundary for certain later chemical and disinfection routes, and is not solved in this chapter.

**Other separately listed raw materials:** flint nodules and bands (in limestone; first choice for Chapter 5); obsidian (glassy volcanic rock, conchoidal fracture, sharp but brittle); kaolin (white earth; the white earth of termite mounds as a clue); ochre pigments; natural grinding stones (large flat slabs of fine sandstone); graphite (see the 2.1 table).

**Failure mode:** treating "should theoretically exist" as "already found". Countermeasure: every map entry must have a location and a physical sample; conjectures without samples go into the "to-investigate list", never into resource entries.

### 2.5 Fuels, Heat Sources, and Usable Natural Energy

**Fuel resources (survey only, no trial burning — trial burning needs fire, which belongs to Chapter 6):**

| Fuel | Identification | Notes |
| ------ | --- | --- |
| Standing deadwood / fallen wood | Dead trees, dry branches | The first main fuel |
| Green wood | High moisture, smoky | Record but do not harvest |
| Animal dung | Steppe / pastoral areas | Combustible once dried (verified in Chapter 6) |
| Peat | Black fibrous soil of wetlands, visible plant remains | Extraction carries collapse risk (safety boundary) |
| Coal outcrop | Black, lighter than a same-sized rock, brittle and stains the hands, layered | Record as "suspected coal"; trial-burning verification postponed until a fire source is obtained |

**Natural energy (record existence and location; utilization is long-term):**

* Hydropower: for candidate sites record the head (paces and eyeball estimate; precise measurement waits for 3.3) and flow speed (the number of beats for a leaf to drift past a two-pace interval; rough measure). Water wheels and drive trains are in Chapter 21.

* Wind: prevailing wind direction (indicated by flag-shaped crowns), a feel-based wind scale: still / leaves moving / branches moving / trunks swaying. Windmills belong to Chapter 29.

* Sunshine: slope aspect (record sunny slopes), cloud-cover grades. Solar energy use belongs to Chapter 52.

* Animal / human power: current livestock species and numbers, and the labor hours that can be invested.

\*\*Failure mode: treating energy existence as energy availability.\*\* A river does not mean hydropower — without head and a water wheel (Chapter 21), river water is just water. The "possible uses" column of an entry may only say "candidate + target chapter number", never "usable".

### 2.6 How to Identify Materials by Appearance, Hardness, Density, and Other Simple Tests

**Pocket test kit (all picked-up natural objects, unprocessed):**

| Tool | Use |
| ------------------------ | ---------- |
| Streak plate: a palm-sized flat coarse quartz-sandstone slab | Streak color |
| Fingernail (hardness about 2.5) | Lower reference |
| Quartz reference block (a white crystal block confirmed to be quartz, hardness about 7) | Upper reference |
| Bark bucket / large shell + water | Settling, float-sink, absorption tests |
| Leaf or grass-stem ruler | Record length grades (no numeric values marked) |

If start point B has bronze artifacts left over, a "copper (about 3)" grade may be added; start points A/C have none — do not add one.

**Pocket hardness ladder table (completed in v2 — match directly on site when prospecting and selecting material, no numbers to memorize)**:

| Test object (scratcher) | Hardness grade | Reading |
| --------------------- | ----- | -------------------- |
| Scratchable by fingernail | <2.5 | Very soft: galena, gypsum, micas — metal-ore candidates |
| Not scratchable by fingernail, scratchable by a copper sheet (start point B) | 2.5–3 | Soft: most sulfide ores |
| **Scratchable by knife / iron nail** (this grade added after Part 4) | 3–5.5 | Medium: malachite, cuprite, fluorite, apatite |
| Not scratchable by knife, **not scratchable by a coin** | 5.5–7 | Hard: magnetite, hematite, feldspar, pyrite |
| Not scratchable even by quartz (reference block) | ≈7 | Very hard: quartz, cassiterite, corundum |

Usage: test grade by grade from top down; **stop at the first "cannot scratch" grade — that is the object's hardness grade** — compared against the 2.1 mineral tables this eliminates half the candidates. Experience note: a scratch counts only if it "leaves a line that cannot be rubbed off"; a powder mark that rubs off does not count (that is the scratching tool's own powder).

**Ten-step identification procedure:**

1. Record the collection location and setting (riverbank / slope / outcrop).
2. Naked eye: color, luster, bedding, crystal form.
3. Fracture: conchoidal / flat / jagged / earthy.
4. Streak: scratch on the streak plate, record the color.
5. Hardness three grades: scratchable by fingernail (<2.5) / not scratchable by fingernail but scratchable by quartz (2.5–7) / not scratchable by quartz (≈7).
6. Density three grades: weigh same-hand-sized pieces — light (clearly lighter than average stone) / medium / heavy (hefty in the hand, mostly metal minerals).
7. Water test: float or sink (wood / lightweight); drip water to see absorption (clay absorbs water and turns sticky, shale does not).
8. Magnetism: a weak criterion in an era without iron tools — record "suspected magnetism" only if fragments attract each other; the main criteria remain streak + density.
9. Compare against the tables of 2.1–2.5 and give a judgment + confidence level (confirmed / suspected / unknown).
10. Bag and number (tied with grass stems), backfill into the 2.7 record sheet.

**Misjudgment recording method:** every time an error is discovered after the fact, add a "misjudgment entry": original judgment / what it actually was / which criterion failed. Misjudgment entries are archived on a par with resource entries — they are the basis for revising the criteria tables.

**Failure mode:** skipping steps to reach a conclusion (writing "hematite" from color alone). Countermeasure: the "judgment" column may be filled only after all ten steps of the procedure card are done; if unfinished, fill in "unknown".

### 2.7 How to Build Your Own Resource Map

**Three elements of the map:**

1. Datum point: stand a head-sized stone at the exact center of the camp; every position on the map takes it as the origin.
2. Direction: the equal-shadow standing-pole method determines north-south — in the morning mark the pole-shadow endpoint A; in the afternoon mark endpoint B when the shadow length equals the morning's; the line AB is east-west, and its perpendicular is north-south. This method depends on no tools and can be re-measured for verification on any clear day.
3. Distance: pace counts. Note "non-standard unit, to be upgraded in Chapter 3". First do a personal calibration of pacing itself: walk 100 paces, measure the length, and record your personal pace length (see the example process card in 3.7).

**Recording media (by durability):** shade-dried scribed clay tablets (fear water; stored on elevated racks off the ground), carved wood, knotted cords (reminder index only, no parameters stored), the camp-ground scribing field (large-scale diagrams). The symbol system is agreed at ≤20 carved symbols (water / clay / stone / flint / iron / fuel / danger / premium ...); the symbol key itself is an archive item, with two backups stored apart.

**Resource survey record sheet template (this chapter's core deliverable; every column mandatory):**

| Column | Content to fill | Example |
| --------- | ----------------------- | ----------------- |
| Number / date / surveyor | Sequential number + day name | 007 / Day 9 / Jia |
| Location | Direction relative to the datum + paces + landform | East by south, about 1200 paces, north bank of a river bend |
| Category | Rock / soil / plant / water / fuel / energy | Soil |
| Judgment + confidence level | Name + confirmed / suspected / unknown | Clay, suspected (not verified by calcination) |
| Test record | Streak / hardness grade / fracture / density grade / four-test results | Rolled strip wraps the finger without breaking; still turbid after most of a day of settling |
| Distribution and estimate | Outcrop or area size (paces) + workable amount scale (scarce / medium / abundant) | About 200 paces along the bank, about 3 fingers thick, scale: abundant |
| Collection difficulty | Bare hands / tools needed / multiple people needed / carry paces | Diggable by hand |
| Possible uses | Candidate uses + target chapter numbers | Pottery (Chapter 11), clay tablets (3.6) |
| Verification status | Unverified / verified (note the chapter number) / misjudged (note the entry number) | Unverified |
| Priority | High / medium / low (per the Chapter 1 priority framework) | High |

**Survey plan (layered expansion):** week 1 covers the half-day-walk circle; month 1 extends it to a full-day walk. Clay, stone, fuel, and water sources are the four priority targets; each day one category is the main line.

**Backfill rule: the map is a living archive.** When later chapters verify or falsify an entry, backfill the "verification status" column within the same month. A map to which entries are only added but never backfilled is, a year later, a pile of expired information.

**Failure modes:**

1. Recording "presence" but not "quantity" — when Part 3 plans production capacity there is nothing to start from. Countermeasure: the estimate and amount-scale columns are mandatory.
2. No datum point or vague landmark descriptions, so a second person cannot revisit. Countermeasure: all three elements of the location column (direction / paces / landform anchor) are indispensable; acceptance is by the revisit test.
3. Symbols lost when the recorder changes. Countermeasure: double backups of the symbol table; the newcomer's first lesson is the symbol table.

## Safety Boundaries

* **Tasting unknown minerals and soils is forbidden**; taste is limited to trace-level confirmation of samples already identified as table-salt class, and nothing may be swallowed.

* Cliffs and cave mouths: observe from afar and mark out the rockfall zone before approaching; do not strike the rock mass overhead. At this stage there is no lighting and no support; mine shafts and deep caves are forbidden.

* Peat-bog sampling requires 2 people together, probing the way with a long pole; no one steps onto soft mud.

* Without a boiling means (before Chapter 6) do not drink standing water; outdoor cooking is not yet established (same as above).

* Felling on steep slopes and climbing for rattan are prohibited outright — woodworking and safety ropes are in Chapter 7.

## Acceptance Criteria

* **L1 proof-of-principle**: complete the first census round of the half-day-walk circle: ≥20 entries + 1 resource map, each entry containing the three location elements and a confidence level.

* **L2 repeatable manufacture**: draw any 3 entries at random; a second person independently revisits them by "direction + paces + landform" and locates all of them successfully; the clay, fuel, and stone/ore categories each have ≥1 entry at "suspected" or above; the ten-step procedure card executed with trace records on 5 samples.

* **L3 sustainable production**: the record-backfill system is running (≥10 entries verified / falsified and backfilled); the symbol table and map double backups checked consistent every quarter; the survey circle extended to a full-day walk; ≥1 misjudgment entry and a revision of the criteria table based on it.
