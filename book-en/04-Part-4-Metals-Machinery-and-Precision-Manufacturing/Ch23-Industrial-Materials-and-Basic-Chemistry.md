### 23.1 Basics of Acids, Bases, Salts, and Solutions

- **Bases**: ash lye water (potassium carbonate, weak); limewater (calcium hydroxide, moderately strong); causticized lye (lime + ash lye → caustic potash/soda; strong base, corrosive);
- **Acids**: vinegar (acetic acid, weak); **strong acids are locally producible from Section 23.9 of this chapter onward**;
- **Salts**: table salt, Glauber's salt (mirabilite), gypsum, saltpeter (joins the list after the Section 23.9 saltpeter boiling).

**Acid-base discrimination in the age before test paper**: natural indicators — red cabbage juice, litmus-lichen extract (turns red in acid, green/blue in base); **tasting is strictly forbidden**. Neutralization: add acid to the alkaline solution and the indicator returns color, producing a salt.

**Key solution quantities**: solubility changes with temperature (most salts dissolve more when heated — the physical basis of purification by crystallization; **saltpeter's solubility changes steeply with temperature while table salt's barely changes at all — this is the key to the Section 23.9 saltpeter-boiling separation**). Concentration is expressed as "grams dissolved per liter of water".

| Material | Field identification | Main uses |

Registration discipline: whenever a new material enters the resource map, register at the same time "simple test method + first trial use + result".

### 23.3 Distillation, Filtration, Crystallization, and Precipitation

The four major separation operations:

- **Filtration** (solid-liquid): a layered filter of cloth → sand → charcoal; felt for fine filtration. The charcoal layer also decolorizes — if the filtrate color lightens, it is working;
- **Crystallization** (purification): slow cooling or slow evaporation of the hot saturated solution, guided with seed crystals; **slow crystallization gives large crystals; fast crystallization gives fine crystals with included impurities**; collect the mother liquor separately for a second use;
- **Precipitation** (impurity transfer): add a reagent that turns the impurity into an insoluble solid that settles out (calcium impurities in ash lye → add soda to precipitate calcium carbonate); decant + wash, twice;
- **Distillation** (volatile separation): glass or copper still; a fractionating column (a long neck packed with small pebbles) improves separation; **collect the fore-run / middle fraction / tail fraction in separate vessels**, and measure density / flammability of each and register.

Record a process card for every step: charge quantity, temperature band, time, yield — **yield is the coordinate of process improvement**.

### 23.4 Fuels, Lubricants, and Cleaners

**Fuel calorific-value ranking** (at equal weight: firewood < peat < lignite < charcoal < bituminous coal ≈ anthracite; empirical values awaiting calorimetric calibration in Chapter 25): **good fuels go first to high-temperature processes**; heating takes low-grade fuel.

**Lubricants** (continuing Chapter 21): animal fat (heavy load, low speed); vegetable oil (higher rotational speed); graphite grease (high temperature / open gears); soap-thickened grease (soap + oil boiled down thick).

**Cleaners**: soap = fat/oil + causticized lye heated together (saponification). Register the ratio per fat type (empirical: excess alkali injures the skin; the product passes when the indicator reads near neutral and the lather is stable). Alkaline boil-washing (ash lye water against greasy soil); acid derusting (vinegar soak → upgraded to dilute sulfuric / dilute hydrochloric acid derusting after Section 23.9). Keep the glycerin byproduct (Chapter 26 reagent).

[Card 23-06 Saponification] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: fat or oil (animal fat or vegetable oil, logged by type and kept in separate pots); causticized lye (lime + ash lye → caustic potash/soda: caustic soda from causticizing Leblanc soda for hard soap, caustic potash from causticizing ash lye for soft soap); water; table salt (for salting-out).
2. **Required tools**: saponification pot (iron — alkalis go in iron), stirring rod, stove fire, indicator (red-cabbage juice or litmus extract), soap-cutting table and drying rack, ceramic salting-out vat, goggles, grease-impregnated gloves, apron.
3. **Sequence and control points**: melt the fat clear in the pot over a gentle heat → add the causticized lye to suit the fat type and boil together with stirring until a film lifted on the rod clings without dripping (soap paste) → check near-neutral with the indicator; if alkali is in excess, add fat and re-boil → turn out into molds or cut into blocks and air 7 days → (continuing 23.12) salting-out: scatter salt, stir gently and boil, let stand to separate, collect the lower glycerin water separately. Control points: add the alkali in portions and recheck with the indicator after each; **tasting is strictly forbidden**.
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: the ratio is registered batch by batch per fat type (this chapter gives no single figure — empirical, calibrate to local fats); criterion = indicator near neutral + stable lather; salt for salting-out 10–15% of the soap paste mass (empirical starting point, calibrated over the 8%/12%/16% three-pot gradient, the clearest separation fixed in the ledger); glycerin water concentration end-point hand-weighs about 1.2 times water.
5. **In-process observation items**: fat type and batch / alkali type and quantity / boiling time and fire color / paste consistency (film on the rod) / indicator reading / product appearance and block-airing period / salting-out separation line and salt quantity / glycerin water volume.
6. **Pass/rework/scrap criteria**: pass = soap near neutral with stable lather, hard soap cut into blocks and aired 7 days without sticking to the knife (a persistently gooey potassium soap is soft soap — register the change of use, do not scrap it); rework = alkali excess that injures the skin — add fat, re-boil and retest the indicator; scrap = under-reacted soap liquor (oil and alkali not combined, never thickens) — downgrade to alkaline boil-wash liquor, never into soap. **Red lines (alkali burns)**: causticized lye is a strong base — alkali on the skin is flushed at once with copious clean water ≥15 min (**never neutralize with acid first**), and alkali in the eye is flushed with clean water ≥15 min and sent down the medical line. **Forbidden zones**: tasting to judge the alkali is strictly forbidden; lead vessels never enter the soap or glycerin line.
7. **Common failures and troubleshooting order**: ① soap that injures the skin (alkali excess, the most frequent) → retest the indicator, add fat and re-boil; ② never thickens, oil and alkali separate → too little alkali or too small a fire; add alkali and keep boiling; ③ product sticky and not setting → potassium-based soft soap; register the change of use; ④ salting-out does not separate → too little salt / paste too thin / boiling too short; add +5% salt per pot and re-boil, and if it still will not separate downgrade to soft-soap liquor; ⑤ dark soap → unrefined fat or too fierce a fire; change the oil, cut the fire.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one pot of soap near neutral with stable lather, cut into blocks; L2 = records for three consecutive batches with a batch-to-batch yield spread ≤10% and the salting-out salt quantity fixed by gradient calibration; L3 = continuous ash lye → causticizing → soap production with monthly yield reports, and the fat/alkali/product three-tier ledgers in Chapter 38.

### 23.5 Basic Metallurgical Chemistry and Metal Surface Treatment

- **Reducing agent**: carbon + CO are the protagonists of iron smelting; make the carbon charge quantitative — excess carbon entering the iron = carburizing (the chemical explanation of Chapter 19);
- **Slagging**: acidic gangue + basic flux melt together; slag fluidity (lift slag on an iron rod and watch it draw threads — the Chapter 18 slag-bar method) is a furnace-condition criterion;
- **The three-piece surface-treatment set**: (1) bluing (heat the steel part into the blue temper-color range and dip in oil/grease); (2) hot-dip tinning (dip sheet iron in molten tin against rust; preferred for food containers; **lead strictly forbidden**); (3) passivation (boil in concentrated ash lye; dark film; register and verify the effect).

### 23.6 Corrosion, Oxidation, and Protection

| Type | Characteristics | Countermeasure |
|---|---|---|
| Dry oxidation | High-temperature oxide scale | Control residence time in the furnace; pickle/grind off the scale (dilute-acid descaling after Section 23.9) |
| Wet corrosion | Rusting in damp conditions | Oiling; painting (pigment + drying oil) |
| Galvanic corrosion | Accelerated at dissimilar-metal overlaps (copper–iron) | Insulating spacers; avoid wet dissimilar-metal contact |
| Stress corrosion | Cold work + corrosive cracking | Annealing to relieve stress |

Maintenance: quarterly inspection and oiling of metal parts; monthly inspection in coastal high humidity (a survival-line item for the Part 10 island/rainforest editions).

### 23.7 How to Control Reactions and Product Purity

Four control variables: **ratio** (balance), **temperature** (fire-color band → thermometer in Chapter 25), **time**, **vessel** (iron for alkalis, glass/ceramic for acids). Three purity checks:
1. **Melting point**: a pure substance has a narrow melting range (on the order of ≤2 ℃); impurities lower the melting point and widen the range (quantified once thermometers arrive in Chapter 25);
2. **Crystal habit**: collapse of the characteristic crystal form signals contamination (table salt cubic; Glauber's salt needle-like; **saltpeter in needle-/hair-like clusters**);
3. **Flame color and precipitation reactions** (systematized in Chapter 26).

### 23.8 Why Chemical Purity Becomes a Later Industrial Bottleneck

| Reagent pure | Repeated recrystallization + distilled water | Chapter 26 |

### 23.9 Local Manufacture of the Three Acids and Soda Ash (New Main Line Added in v2)

#### 23.9.1 Sulfuric Acid: Green Vitriol Dry Distillation (Preferred Local Route)

**Principle chain**: pyrite (FeS₂) is roasted at low temperature → oxidized and water-leached → **green vitriol (ferrous sulfate FeSO₄·7H₂O) crystallizes**; the green vitriol is dry-distilled in a retort → it decomposes into SO₃ and steam → condense and receive: **oil of vitriol (concentrated sulfuric acid)**. This is the true historical origin of "oil of vitriol"; the apparatus needs only a ceramic retort and a condenser tube.

**Step 1 · Roasting and vitriol leaching**:
1. Crush pyrite to 5–15 mm and spread it in the kiln (indirect flame, ample airflow — **you want oxidation, not reduction**, the opposite of iron smelting); roast at low red heat for 4–8 h (empirical) until the ore color turns brown;
2. Water-leach the roasted ore for 12 h (ceramic urn); the leachate should be green and clear;
3. Evaporate and concentrate the leachate until a crystal film forms, then let stand and cool slowly to crystallize — green vitriol crystals. **Crystal-form criterion**: emerald-green coarse prisms = pass; yellowing turbidity = the iron has been over-oxidized (roasting went too far) — add a small amount of iron filings to reduce before concentrating (empirical method; register and verify).

**Step 2 · Dry distillation to make the acid**:

[Card 23-01 Sulfuric Acid by Green Vitriol Dry Distillation] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: fully dried green vitriol crystals (FeSO₄·7H₂O; emerald-green coarse prisms pass, yellowing turbidity means over-roasted — add a little iron filings to reduce before concentrating, an empirical method that must be registered and verified); pyrite crushed to 5–15 mm with coal-bearing gangue picked out (Chapter 16). The vitriol must be sun-dried first — a wet charge yields only water in the first stage and wastes fuel.
2. **Required tools**: ceramic retort (lidded and sealed, fired from below, loaded no more than 2/3 full), ceramic or glass condenser bend, two receiving jars (ceramic/glass, the second in series as a buffer), wooden plugs and sealing clay, an ash-lye absorption bottle on the condenser outlet for tail gas, a balance (to cross-check specific gravity), a wood splinter (charring test), goggles, grease-impregnated gloves, wooden tongs.
3. **Sequence and control points**: ① roast the pyrite in the muffle kiln at low red heat 4–8 h with ample airflow — oxidation, not reduction — until the ore turns brown; ② water-leach the roasted ore in a ceramic urn 12 h until the leachate is green and clear; ③ evaporate to a crystal film, let stand and cool slowly to crystallize green vitriol; ④ load the dried vitriol (≤2/3), run the outlet tube obliquely downward with its end **above** the receiving jar's liquid surface (never below it — back-suction bursts the jar); ⑤ raise the heat slowly on a gentle fire to drive off residual water (this fore-run is thin; discard it or keep it as dilute acid); ⑥ go to a strong fire until the retort body is dull red — white fumes enter the condenser, condense and drip into the jar; ⑦ kill the fire when the white fumes thin; ⑧ let the distillate stand to settle out suspended residue. Control points: an inspection hole in the sealing clay, pressure relief in the system, an ash-lye absorption bottle on the condenser outlet.
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: roast at low red heat 4–8 h (empirical, calibrate to local ore and kiln); leach 12 h; green vitriol → oil of vitriol yield 20–40% (empirical, needs calibration); unit consumption about 4–8 kg pyrite → 1 kg green vitriol → 0.3–0.5 kg oil of vitriol (empirical, needs calibration); product specific gravity ≈1.8 (same-volume hand-weigh about 1.8 times water, cross-checked on the balance, reported only as a comparison).
5. **In-process observation items**: per retort load record the vitriol source and crystal form / charge mass / fire-color band with start and stop times / change in white-fume density / distillate appearance (oily or thin) / distillate mass and specific gravity / state of the tail-gas bottle / yield. The four-column log (charge · temperature band · time · yield) enters the book with each batch.
6. **Pass/rework/scrap criteria**: pass = distillate oily and viscous, specific gravity ≈1.8 times water, clearly heat-releasing on a drip into water, chars a wood splinter black (do it in a ventilated spot), and ≥100 g per batch; rework = distillate thin as water (fire too low or charge wet) — pool and re-distill, sun-dry the charge and recharge; scrap = distillate carrying heavy black suspension (retort overfilled and material carried over) — clarify, then re-distill; a cracked retort leaking fumes means kill the fire and re-seal with clay. **Red lines (burns and poisoning)**: oil of vitriol causes charring burns — wipe concentrated acid off the skin fast with a dry cloth, then flush with copious clean water ≥15 min; any acid in the eye is flushed with clean water ≥15 min and sent down the medical line, and **the victim must never apply a neutralizer**; anyone choking or chest-pained from acid mist withdraws upwind at once and is sent for treatment. **Forbidden zones**: water into acid is forever forbidden (dilution per the 23.10 ten-addition method); no fire is lit unless the apparatus stands outdoors or in a four-sided cross-flow shed and goggles plus grease-impregnated gloves are worn; unlabeled distillate is treated as the concentrated band.
7. **Common failures and troubleshooting order**: ① acid-mist choking or acid splashed on the hands (the most frequent) → withdraw upwind at once, flush as above, then check standing position and wind direction; ② little acid, thin distillate → first check whether the vitriol was dried through, then the roast-leach stage (recheck crystal form; yellowing means over-roasted); ③ receiving jar bursts or back-suction → check whether the delivery tube sits below the liquid, move it above; ④ retort cracked, fumes leaking → kill the fire and re-seal with clay; ⑤ distillate carries black residue → retort overfilled, reduce the charge. On smelling choking white fumes, move the people out first, then look for the cause.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one batch of oil of vitriol ≥100 g that passes the wood-splinter charring test; L2 = yield records for three consecutive batches with a batch-to-batch spread ≤10% and zero accidents under the acid-fog procedure; L3 = monthly production and the feedstock/fuel/purity ledgers entered in Chapter 38, with the residue and tail-gas disposal lines running (interface to Chapter 49).

Yield reference (empirical, must be field-calibrated): about 4–8 kg pyrite → 1 kg green vitriol → 0.3–0.5 kg oil of vitriol.
[Upgrade slot] sulfur burning + lead-chamber process (welded lead-plate box, saltpeter supplying NOx, continuous dilute-acid output) — activate once lead-plate rolling and welding (Chapter 17 lead casting) have matured, for an order-of-magnitude jump in output; see Section 23.11 for the preliminary research, smoke-color observation, and start conditions; this route is registered as the small-scale refining line.

#### 23.9.2 Saltpeter: Boiling and Purification (Shared Feedstock of Nitric Acid and Gunpowder)

1. **Collect nitre earth**: white frosty surface soil at old wall bases and around latrines/livestock pens (most visible on dry days after rain);
2. **Leach**: nitre earth + wood ash (a potassium aid) at 5:1 (empirical starting point), soaked in water 12 h; filter and take the clear liquor;
3. **Concentrate**: evaporate to about one-third of the volume;
4. **Fractional crystallization (separating table salt)**: hot-filter the hot saturated solution **while still hot** (hot filtration removes the table salt that has already crystallized out — table salt's solubility barely changes with temperature, so it drops out during concentration; saltpeter's solubility changes steeply and it stays in the liquor); let the filtrate stand and cool slowly — **needle-/hair-like saltpeter crystals precipitate**; reconcentrate the mother liquor and cool it out again; after two passes, recheck purity by crystal form and flame color (violet flame, Chapter 26);
5. Crystal-form criterion: needle clusters = saltpeter; cubes = table salt contamination (recrystallize once).

[Card 23-02 Saltpeter Boiling] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: nitre earth, one batch of the 50 kg class (source judged per 23.9.2); wood ash; water; firewood. **The source must be logged to the exact spot** for rotation and yield comparison.
2. **Tools required**: leaching tub, filter bed (mat or sand), iron pan for hot filtering, crystallizing basin, flame-test kit (fine iron wire plus flame). Calibration source: nitre is identified by **crystal form plus flame colour** — never by taste.
3. **Sequence and control points**: earth into the tub, leach with water, **hot-filter to remove salt (the soul of this card)**, liquor into the basin, **crystallize by slow cooling**, lift and dry the crystals, flame test. Two control points: **filter hot** (cold filtering carries salt into the crystal and leaves only recrystallization to save it); **cool slowly** (fast cooling gives fine crystals carrying impurities).
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: batch of the 50 kg class; yield **1–2% from poor earth, 5–10% from rich earth (empirical, calibrate to the local source)**; **one harvest per spot per year** (hard rule against exhaustion).
5. **Observations to record**: per batch — source spot / earth mass / water added / liquor volume / crystal mass / yield / crystal form / flame result.
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = needle-cluster crystals plus a violet flame; rework = cubes mixed in (recrystallize once); scrap = source too poor (yield <1%, change spot and update the nitre map).
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) mostly cubes, few needles — salt not removed, filtering was not hot enough; (2) yield far below the band — poor source, change spot; (3) slow, fine crystallization — weak liquor or cooling too fast; (4) flame not violet — potassium salt contamination, recrystallize.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one acceptable batch with records; L2 = consecutive batches comparable; L3 = nitre map updated with yield bands.

#### 23.9.3 Nitric Acid: Saltpeter + Sulfuric Acid, Distilled

1. Load dried saltpeter and concentrated sulfuric acid at about 1:1 by mass (empirical starting point, needs calibration) into a retort (glass best, ceramic second);
2. Heat over a gentle fire — the distillate is nitric acid (extremely corrosive; **the receiving bottle must be glass**; ceramic bottles corroding through is a real accident);
3. Reddish-brown fumes (NO₂ decomposition products) mean overheating — back the fire down one band;
4. **Entirely outdoors + crosswind + goggles**: nitric acid mist damages lungs and eyes faster than sulfuric acid.

[Card 23-03 Nitric Acid Distillation] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: dried saltpeter (23-02 needle-cluster crystals with violet flame); concentrated sulfuric acid (oil of vitriol, 23-01) at saltpeter : acid ≈1:1 by mass (empirical starting point); a clean, dry glass retort and a glass receiving bottle.
2. **Required tools**: glass retort (best; ceramic second, but **ceramic bottles are forbidden as receivers**), glass receiving bottle, condenser/delivery tube, balance (ratios in parts by mass), goggles, grease-impregnated gloves, wooden tongs, ash-lye absorption bottle (tail gas), outdoors or a four-sided cross-flow shed.
3. **Sequence and control points**: weigh the charge at 1:1 into the retort → heat on a gentle fire → distillate into the glass receiving bottle → on seeing red-brown fumes back the fire down one band → kill the fire at the end, leaving the bottle mouth for pressure relief. Control points: **glass receivers only** (a ceramic bottle corroding through under nitric acid is a real accident); the delivery tube never below the liquid; outdoors + crosswind + goggles throughout.
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: saltpeter : sulfuric acid ≈1:1 (mass, empirical starting point, calibrate to local saltpeter purity); the fire setting is "gentle fire — on red-brown fumes drop one band" (fire-color band rules as in Chapter 12); this chapter gives no yield figure, so register charge and distillate volume per batch as that batch's coordinate (empirical, calibrate to local conditions).
5. **In-process observation items**: charge mass and ratio / fire-color band / fume color (colorless, pale brown, thick red-brown) / distillate color and volume / corrosion or weeping on the bottle / state of the tail-gas bottle.
6. **Pass/rework/scrap criteria**: pass = distillate clear, no thick red-brown fumes throughout, the glass bottle intact; rework = distillate tinged red-brown (thermal decomposition) — back the fire down and re-distill; scrap = receiver perforated or distillate heavily laden with residue — discard the bottle, re-distill. **Red lines (burns and poisoning)**: nitric acid mist damages lungs and eyes faster than sulfuric acid — wipe acid off the skin with a dry cloth, then flush with clean water ≥15 min; acid in the eye is flushed with clean water ≥15 min and sent down the medical line; no neutralizer is applied by the victim; anyone coughing on yellow fumes withdraws upwind at once and is sent for treatment at rest. **Forbidden zones**: ceramic bottles are forbidden for nitric acid; no fire without outdoor or cross-flow-shed siting and goggles; water into acid is forever forbidden.
7. **Common failures and troubleshooting order**: ① coughing on yellow fumes or acid splash (the most frequent) → move the people out first, flush as above, then check wind direction and standing position; ② heavy red-brown fumes → overheating, back the fire down one band; ③ distillate yellow with a red-brown tinge → decomposition products dissolved in, back the fire down and re-distill; ④ receiver perforated and weeping → a ceramic bottle was used, change to glass; ⑤ pungent tail gas, absorption incomplete → wrong tube position or fire too strong; enlarge the absorption bottle and cut the fire.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one batch of nitric acid, glass-bottled, the bottle unperforated; L2 = records for three consecutive batches with a batch-to-batch yield spread ≤10% and zero accidents under the acid-fog procedure; L3 = monthly production and purity ledgers entered in Chapter 38, with the nitric-acid-in-glass-only red-line inspection on file.

#### 23.9.4 Hydrochloric Acid: Table Salt + Sulfuric Acid, Absorbed in Water

1. Mix table salt + concentrated sulfuric acid (about 3:1 by mass, empirical starting point) in the retort and heat — pungent HCl gas evolves;
2. Lead the delivery tube into **three water jars in series** (bring the gas in through an inverted funnel or an angled tube end near the water surface, not inserted deep — guards against back-suction): HCl is extremely soluble in water; the three jars absorb it in turn, and when the third jar is nearly odorless, absorption is complete;
3. Accept the resulting dilute hydrochloric acid on the indicator (red cabbage turns red) and its rust-removing power;
4. **Unabsorbed tail gas goes into ash lye** — the HCl is neutralized by the lye and no white fumes escape.

[Card 23-04 Hydrochloric Acid Absorption] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: table salt (dry, unclotted); concentrated sulfuric acid (oil of vitriol) at salt : acid ≈3:1 by mass (empirical starting point); three jars of clean water; ash lye (tail-gas neutralization).
2. **Required tools**: acid-resistant retort (ceramic/glass), delivery tube, **three water jars in series** (inverted funnel or angled tube end near the water surface), ash-lye absorption bottle, red-cabbage indicator, goggles, grease-impregnated gloves, wooden tongs, outdoors or a cross-flow shed.
3. **Sequence and control points**: mix salt + acid at 3:1 in the retort → heat, pungent HCl gas evolves → lead the gas through the tube into the three jars in turn (mouth near the water surface, **not inserted deep**, guarding against back-suction) → when the third jar is nearly odorless, absorption is complete → unabsorbed tail gas into ash lye. Control points: the three-jar series cannot be shortened; tube-mouth position; the last-jar odor call must be confirmed by two people.
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: salt : sulfuric acid ≈3:1 (mass, empirical starting point, calibrate to local salt and acid strength); complete-absorption criterion = the third jar nearly odorless (olfactory judgment); this card sets no figure for the dilute acid obtained — register it by hand-weigh comparison with a color tag (the 23.10 three-band system).
5. **In-process observation items**: charge mass and ratio / vigor of gas evolution / each jar's liquid temperature and odor / last-jar odor / acid quantity obtained / indicator color change / derusting test piece.
6. **Pass/rework/scrap criteria**: pass = the three jars absorb in turn, the last jar nearly odorless, the indicator (red cabbage) turns red and derusting power passes; rework = the last jar still pungent (incomplete absorption) — cut the fire, deepen the inlet or add a fourth jar, then re-absorb; scrap = back-suction that siphons jar liquid into the retort, or acid heavily loaded with salt residue — discard the batch and recheck the apparatus. **Red lines (burns and poisoning)**: HCl white mist chokes the lungs — wipe acid off the skin with a dry cloth, then flush with clean water ≥15 min; acid in the eye is flushed with clean water ≥15 min and sent down the medical line; no neutralizer is applied by the victim; anyone tight-chested from the white mist withdraws upwind at once and is sent for treatment. **Forbidden zones**: the delivery tube never below the liquid (back-suction); tail gas never discharged indoors; no fire without goggles and gloves; water into acid is forever forbidden.
7. **Common failures and troubleshooting order**: ① white-mist choking or acid splash (the most frequent) → withdraw upwind and flush as above; ② last jar still pungent → first check whether the tube mouth is set too deep, then whether the fire is too strong, finally add a fourth jar; ③ back-suction, jar liquid siphoned into the retort → tube set deep or the fire cut too abruptly; bring the inlet near the surface; ④ little acid → recheck the 3:1 ratio and the salt charge first, then whether the fire is too low; ⑤ acid turbid with residue → muddy salt; pick it clean beforehand, or dissolve the salt, settle and decant the brine.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one batch of hydrochloric acid, absorbed across three jars with the last jar odorless, passing both the indicator and derusting acceptance; L2 = records for three consecutive batches with a batch-to-batch yield spread ≤10% and zero accidents under the acid-fog procedure; L3 = monthly production entered in Chapter 38, with tail-gas neutralization and waste-acid disposal lines running (interface to Chapter 49).

#### 23.9.5 Soda Ash: Three Routes and the Choice

| Route | Inputs | Output | Positioning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Natural alkali lakes | Lake-surface alkali frost / brine | Sodium carbonate taken directly | **Best if available** (the best alkali source for glass) |
| Wood-ash line | Firewood | Potassium carbonate (potash) | Existing line (Chapter 8); glass can still be potash glass |
| **Leblanc process (new line in this chapter)** | Table salt + oil of vitriol + coke + limestone | Bowl soda (sodium carbonate) | The large-capacity line when no natural alkali exists |

**The two steps of the Leblanc process (summary + pollution honestly declared)**:
1. Heat table salt + sulfuric acid → sodium sulfate ("salt cake"), releasing HCl (absorbed in water, co-producing hydrochloric acid — the acid and alkali lines feed each other here);
2. Calcine salt cake + powdered coke + powdered limestone at high temperature (Chapter 12 furnace, above red heat for 2–4 h) → black ash; crush the black ash and water-leach it → sodium carbonate solution (filter off insoluble residues such as calcium sulfide) → evaporate and crystallize to crude soda; recrystallize once to remove color.
**Pollution honestly declared**: the leaching residue contains calcium sulfide, which gives off rotten-egg-smelling hydrogen sulfide on contact with acid — **keep residue heaps far from water sources and dwellings, and spray them with ash lye water to bind and fix them** (empirical practice; register and verify); the waste-residue disposal line connects to Chapter 49.

[Card 23-05 Leblanc Soda Ash] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: table salt; concentrated sulfuric acid (oil of vitriol); powdered coke; powdered limestone; water. Charge salt cake : coke : limestone ≈2:1:2 (mass, empirical starting point).
2. **Required tools**: salt-cake reaction pot (acid-resistant, with a gas off-take), the Chapter 12 high-temperature furnace (red heat and above), crushing and sieving tools, leaching tank and filter, evaporating pan, crystallizing basin, ash-lye water (for spraying and fixing the residue heap), goggles, grease-impregnated gloves.
3. **Sequence and control points**: ① heat salt + sulfuric acid → salt cake (sodium sulfate), releasing HCl which the off-take leads into water, co-producing hydrochloric acid; ② mix salt cake + powdered coke + powdered limestone at 2:1:2 and calcine above red heat 2–4 h to black ash; ③ crush the black ash and water-leach → sodium carbonate solution, filtering off insoluble residue such as calcium sulfide; ④ evaporate and crystallize to crude soda; ⑤ recrystallize once to decolorize. Control points: the charge ratio, calcination temperature and duration, a clear leachate; **keep the residue heap far from water sources and dwellings and spray it with ash-lye water to bind and fix it**.
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: salt cake : coke : limestone ≈2:1:2 (mass, empirical starting point, calibrate to local coke and limestone grade); above red heat 2–4 h; this chapter gives no soda-ash yield figure, so register charge and crude-soda output per cycle (empirical, calibrate to local conditions); one recrystallization to decolorize is the standard.
5. **In-process observation items**: mass of each charge component / calcination fire color and duration / black-ash color and odor / leachate color and odor / residue mass / crude-soda output and shade / residue-heap disposal record.
6. **Pass/rework/scrap criteria**: pass = black-ash leachate clear with no black stench, crude soda pale after recrystallization, residue heap sited and already fixed with ash lye; rework = leachate black and foul (coke excess or insufficient calcination) — re-proportion and re-calcine, recrystallize to decolorize; scrap = the heap leaking or encroaching on a water source (red line) — remove it at once, fix with ash lye, register and report. **Red lines (poisoning and burns)**: the HCl and acid mist released in step one choke the lungs and burn — wipe acid off the skin with a dry cloth, then flush with clean water ≥15 min; acid in the eye is flushed with clean water ≥15 min and sent down the medical line; no neutralizer is applied by the victim; **the leach residue contains calcium sulfide and gives off hydrogen sulfide (rotten-egg smell) on contact with acid — no acid washing or acid dumping beside the heap, and on smelling it withdraw upwind**; anyone dizzy or nauseated is sent for treatment at once. **Forbidden zones**: the heap stays away from water sources, dwellings and vegetable plots; no acid charging without goggles and gloves; water into acid is forever forbidden.
7. **Common failures and troubleshooting order**: ① rotten-egg smell at the heap or people dizzy (the highest poisoning risk) → pull everyone out, stand upwind, spray ash lye, and check whether acid liquor has run into the heap; ② leachate black and foul → coke excess or insufficient calcination; re-proportion and re-calcine; ③ crude soda dark → too few recrystallizations or residue not filtered out; ④ low soda output → off-ratio charge, calcination short of red heat, or incomplete leaching; ⑤ reaction pot or off-take leaking (HCl escaping) → kill the fire and re-seal, people withdraw upwind.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one cycle of crude soda, pale after recrystallization, with a leachate that is neither black nor foul; L2 = records for three consecutive cycles with a batch-to-batch yield spread ≤10% and complete heap fixing and disposal records; L3 = monthly production entered in Chapter 38, the residue disposal line running (interface to Chapter 49), and the three-tier feedstock/fuel/purity ledgers in operation.

### 23.10 Concentration and Dilution Safety System (New in v2)

Once the three acids enter the site, the biggest source of accidents is no longer "cannot make it" but "wrong concentration used". This section consolidates the concentration management of every acid and alkali liquor in this chapter into one registration system: **every bottle of liquid must have a band, a hand-weigh record, and a color tag tied to its use.**

**Three-band concentration registration**:

| Band | Definition (concentrated acid : water, by volume; empirical starting point) | Hand-weigh density comparison (relative to water) | Permitted uses | Color tag |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Concentrated | Stock solution or <1:3 | Sulfuric stock about 1.8 times; hydrochloric/nitric stock about 1.2–1.4 times | Gas-making feedstock, descaling, charring test | Red |
| Medium | 1:5–1:10 | About 1.05–1.15 times | Derusting, ore leaching, neutralizing waste liquor | Yellow |
| Dilute | 1:20 and thinner | Feels close to water | Washing, drenching, final pickle stage | Blue |

- Each band is tied to permitted and forbidden uses: hang the tag on the outside of the ceramic bottle; **if the liquid in the bottle does not match its tag, the whole bottle goes back for re-mixing**; unlabeled liquid is always treated as "concentrated" band (worst-case principle);
- Hand-weigh method: fill the same small vial once with water and once with the test liquid, weigh both on the balance (Chapter 3), and divide the weights to get "grams per milliliter"; report only the comparison ("about 1.1 times water") and do not pretend to precision. Calibration method: use saturated brine (hand-weighs about 1.2 times water) as the reference anchor at 1.2; recheck the balance and the vial volume once every ten days.

**Floating-egg method (dilute band and salt solutions only)**: calibrate each new batch of eggs in two vats first — fresh water (sinks to the bottom) and 5% brine (hand-weighs about 1.03; floats upright mid-immersion) — and note this batch's posture; then test the unknown liquid and read the comparison: sunk <1.03; upright mid-immersion about 1.03–1.05; crown just breaking the surface about 1.05–1.08; more than half exposed >1.10. **Red line: the floating-egg method is forbidden on acid of medium band and above** — eggshell is calcium carbonate; in acid it fizzes and smokes violently, ruining the reading and splashing acid onto people.

**The "acid into water" ten-addition method (full procedure; a hard site-wide rule)**:
1. Equipment: ceramic vat (capacity ≥ 3 times the total volume of acid plus water — margin for boiling and expansion), glass rod or hardwood rod, small acid-measuring vial;
2. Water goes into the vat first (the full amount in one go); lean the rod against the vat wall;
3. Add the acid **slowly down the rod in ten separate pours**, each about one-tenth of the total; after each pour, stir gently in circles with the rod ≥10 turns;
4. Add the next portion only when the back of a hand held against the vat wall for 2 s does not burn; goggles and grease-impregnated gloves throughout; outdoors or in a cross-ventilated shed; standing rules same as the Section 23.9 acid-fog red line;
5. After the tenth pour let stand ≥10 min; re-check the concentration band by floating egg or hand-weigh; hang the matching color tag; register the ratio;
6. **Water into acid is forever forbidden**: water hitting a concentrated-acid surface boils at once, throwing acid mist and acid droplets into the face — this is the leading cause of death in dilution accidents, and the rule is repeated here as often as it takes.

**Three-step spill response (contain — neutralize — clean)**:
1. **Contain**: ring the spill with dry sand or dry earth to stop the flow first; direct water flushing is forbidden — water spreads the acid surface and releases heat, enlarging the accident;
2. **Neutralize**: scatter ash-lye powder or baking soda inside the ring to cover the acid surface; fizzing means neutralization is proceeding; keep scattering until it **no longer fizzes**;
3. **Clean**: once the fizzing stops, sweep the sand into the waste-residue drum with a wet broom (acid residue gets its own drum; it does not go into general waste), rinse the ground once with clean water, and register the spill location and estimated quantity.

Acid on skin: wipe concentrated acid off quickly with a dry cloth first, then flush with copious clean water ≥15 min (concentrated acid releases heat on contact with water; wiping first reduces the heat burn; dilute acid can be flushed straight away); acid in the eye is always flushed with clean water ≥15 min and then sent down the medical line — never apply a neutralizer yourself.

### 23.11 Preliminary Research on the Lead-Chamber Sulfuric Acid Upgrade (Activate Once the Lead-Plate Production Line Matures)

**Positioning**: once Chapter 17 lead casting and lead-plate rolling/welding have matured, sulfuric acid upgrades from "batch dry distillation" to a "semi-continuous lead chamber". This section is preliminary research — apparatus, control variables, and observation tables are all designed and finalized, **but no construction before the start conditions at the end of this section are met**: erecting the tower before lead-plate welding is mastered stacks leak risk and lead-poisoning risk on top of each other.

**The three pieces of apparatus (bottom to top)**:
1. Sulfur burner: sulfur burns slowly in a small ceramic furnace; adjust the air intake with the furnace-door opening (dull red tending yellow is the slow-burn band; fire-color band rules as in Chapter 12);
2. Lead chamber: a large lead box (lead plate ≥2 mm thick, rolled and welded) holding shallow water or circulating dilute acid at the bottom;
3. Circulation tower: one coke-packed tower; its top sprinkles saltpeter-dosed dilute acid to recover nitrogen oxides from the tail gas for reuse — saltpeter is the most expensive consumable in this process, and the circulation tower is the machine that saves it.

**Weld-seam leak testing (the lead chamber's lifeline)**: the kerosene penetrant method — paint kerosene on the outside of the weld; after 10 min, check the inside for oil marks; no marks = pass. Feedstock may be charged only after all three pieces of the whole tower pass the leak test.

**Control and observation registration**:
- Tower temperature band: three touch bands (cool/warm/hot), logged every 2 h; the lead-chamber shell is normal when "a palm can stay on it 3 s" (that is, the not-burning-hot band); burning hot means sulfur burning too hard or circulation gas too large — close the furnace door one band and re-log after half an hour;
- **Smoke-color observation (the only online instrument in this process)**: colorless = normal operation; pale brown = nitrogen oxides slightly over, cut the saltpeter; thick red-brown = saltpeter excess or burning too fierce — cut the saltpeter and back the fire down; colorless but slow acid output = saltpeter short — increase the saltpeter-liquor sprinkling. The observation table takes one row per hour: smoke color / acid drip rate / touch temperature, three columns, entered in the same format as the Section 23.9 batch records;
- Product: lead-chamber acid is dilute acid (hand-weighs about 1.3–1.5 times water), feeding dilute-acid bulk uses such as derusting and leaching directly; re-distilling it in a ceramic retort can concentrate it to oil-of-vitriol band (re-distillation procedure as in Section 23.9.1).

**The capacity comparison ledger, green-vitriol line against lead-chamber line** (empirical values; verify against real accounts after startup):

| Item | Green vitriol dry distillation | Lead-chamber process |
|---|---|---|
| Unit consumption | Pyrite 4–8 kg/kg acid | Sulfur about 0.5 kg/kg acid (converted to sulfur basis, computed at 70% yield) |
| Operating mode | Batch; two people, half a day per retort load | Continuous; duty-round inspection system |
| Saltpeter consumption | None | Yes (recycling has losses, about the 10 kg class per ton of acid; needs calibration) |
| Positioning | Small-scale refining line + backup line | Main acid production line |

At equal manpower the capacity is about 5–10 times higher, but it eats saltpeter. **Priority discipline**: saltpeter also supplies the gunpowder line; when the two lines contend for saltpeter, gunpowder takes priority and the acid line falls back to the green-vitriol process — the green-vitriol process is therefore kept forever and never retired.

**Start-conditions checklist (work begins only when every box is ticked)**: (1) all three lead-weld pieces pass leak testing (kerosene method); (2) stable sulfur supply entered in the monthly ledger; (3) a calculation sheet showing saltpeter monthly output leaves a surplus after deducting the gunpowder line; (4) the smoke-color observation table and operating procedure printed and posted on the wall; (5) ≥2 trained operators, each having run three batches of the green-vitriol process independently.

### 23.12 Oleochemistry: Salt-Out Glycerin Line, Hard Soap and Soft Soap, and Candles

**Salting-out and glycerin collection (continuing Section 23.4 saponification)**: saponification produces soap + glycerin; the glycerin dissolves in the soap liquor, and separation relies on salting-out:
1. Re-boil the soap paste in the pot until a film lifted out clings without dripping; scatter in table salt at about 10–15% of the soap paste mass (empirical starting point); stir gently and boil ≥10 min;
2. Let stand ≥1 h: the soap floats and cakes into a layer (pass criterion: a clean separation line, the lower liquor clearly "thinner" than the upper); the lower layer is glycerin water;
3. Collect the glycerin water separately; evaporate and concentrate it in a ceramic pot over a slow fire until it hand-weighs about 1.2 times water; seal in a small-mouth ceramic bottle, keep dust off, and register;
4. Calibration method: three small pots at graded salt additions 8%/12%/16%; register the salt amount of the one with the cleanest separation into the ledger and fix it; if no layer separates, handle per the failure table;
5. Uses: skin moisturizing, leather care (merged into the Chapter 21 oil line ledger), and reserved as a reagent feedstock slot.

**Hard soap and soft soap**:

| | Sodium-based hard soap | Potassium-based soft soap |
|---|---|---|
| Alkali source | Caustic soda obtained by causticizing Leblanc soda | Caustic potash obtained by causticizing ash lye |
| Form | Firm blocks; stores and transports well | Translucent paste; sticky and soft |
| Uses | Laundry; the mainstay of industrial degreasing | Works in cold water; hair washing; near-liquid soap |
| Discrimination | Cut into blocks and aired 7 days, the knife does not stick = pass | Persistently gooey means it is soft soap — register and change its use; do not scrap it |

The same pot of fat, a different alkali, a different soap — **the element in the alkali decides whether soap is hard or soft**. When planning feedstock, fix the soap type before ordering the alkali: if hard soap for storage and transport is wanted, the sodium source of Leblanc soda must be secured.

**Candle manufacture**:
- Feedstock ranking: beeswax > stearin (the solid fraction pressed out of animal fat cold) > ordinary tallow (honestly noted: smoky, weak flame; an emergency-grade item). Empirical melting points: beeswax about 62–64 ℃, tallow about 45–50 ℃. **Casting-time criterion: a thin skin has just formed on the surface of the melted wax** — mold at that moment (switch to thermometer readings and register them once thermometers arrive);
- Wick: cotton or hemp yarn braided tight in three strands; soaked in 5% brine and sun-dried (less black smoke and less over-fast wick burning — empirical practice; verify by controlled comparison: burn a brined wick and a plain wick 1 h each, compare smoke volume and flame, register the better one); wick diameter ≈ 1/10 of candle diameter (make one test candle each at the graded sizes 1/8, 1/10, 1/12 and trial-burn 1 h; register and fix the one with the steadiest flame and no deep wax pool);
- Molded method: wick centered and held taut (the mold mouth locates it with a slotted wooden slat clamping the wick); pour the wax at skin-forming temperature; cool through ≥2 h before demolding; pass = glossy surface, wick centered, no pores in the cross-section;
- Dipped method: tie the wick bundles to a crossbar and repeat "dip in wax — air 2 min"; each layer adds thickness on the order of 0.5–1 mm; dip 15–25 times until finger-thick (empirical; floats with wax temperature: cooler wax gives thicker layers but a rougher surface). Suits a small workshop turning out dipped candles in parallel batches.

1. **Input materials**: wax or fat (picked clean of refuse, **free of water and salt** — water in hot fat spatters, salted fat smokes black); wick thread (cotton or hemp, three-strand tight braid); substitute: stearin for beeswax (weaker flame, more smoke — **must be labelled honestly**).

### 23.13 Pigment and Paint Line

**Three pigment sources (all feedstocks already on the local register)**:

| Pigment | Key production points | Criteria | Uses and red lines |
|---|---|---|---|
| Iron red / iron yellow | Red/yellow clay or limonite earth: pan out the fine slime (let the slurry stand and take the top layer) → sun-dry the cake → roast at low temperature; yellow earth over-heated turns red and becomes iron red, **the hotter, the darker** (three-kiln gradient: back of hand can touch / lower limit of red heat / red heat; compare colors, pick the band, register) | Grinds fine; silky and grit-free between the fingers; after settling in water, the supernatant is nearly clear | Building and wood coloring; non-toxic |
| Red lead (orange-red) | Thin lead sheets stacked in a muffle furnace, oxidized for a long time with airflow (on the order of 300–500 ℃); scrape off the orange-red surface powder and grind it fine | Uniform orange-red color; grinds fine without grit | **The mainstay rust preventive; linked to the Section 17.8 lead red line** |
| Indigo | Indigo-plant leaves + water + a little ash lye; ferment warm 3–7 days until the liquor turns blue-green and the leaves rot → beat the indigo with lime (whip until it foams and the foam turns deep blue) → let stand for the indigo to settle, take the bottom mud, sun-dry into cakes | The indigo cake returns to blue in water | Mainly cloth dyeing |

**The three Iron Laws of the red-lead workstation (the Section 17.8 red line carried onto the paint line)**: (1) dedicated person, dedicated tools, dedicated clothing; the workstation stands alone and **shares no vessels, brushes, or water buckets with the food line**; (2) red-lead paint is forbidden on tableware, food containers, water-bucket interiors, and granary interiors — apply only to boat bottoms, the outside of ironware, and structural frames; (3) every lead-painted article is marked with a red bar and its item number registered.

**Drying oils**:
- Linseed oil: best drying, light color — first choice for topcoats;
- Tung oil: stronger waterproofing and faster drying, but raw tung oil skins into wrinkles — **it must be boiled (cooked)**: boil over a slow fire until "a drip into water beads up and does not spread" (a drop into cold water congeals into a bead that holds its shape — that is enough fire). **Boiling tung oil is a designated high-fire-risk operation for the whole book**: outdoor sand bath; dry sand and wet burlap sacks at hand beside the oil pot; a dedicated watch person who never leaves the post; sleeves tied tight;
- Semi-drying oils (rapeseed oil etc.) are never used alone, only as softening blends; register the blend ratio batch by batch.

**Paint mixing**: grind the dry pigment powder until it feels grit-free between the fingers, then blend with the cooked oil at powder : oil ≈ 2:1 (by volume; empirical starting point). **Consistency criterion: paint lifted on the stick hangs a thread about 5 cm without breaking** — too thick, add oil; too thin, add powder; record the ratio for every batch mixed, and record the hanging-thread length with it.

**Painting regime (number of coats and drying criteria)**:
1. Derust the substrate: medium-band acid pickle → rinse with clean water → dry through ≥1 day;
2. First coat thin, as primer (paint slightly thin) → surface-dry (a touch leaves nothing on the finger) → **hard-dry (a fingernail scratch leaves no white mark)** → only then the next coat;
3. 2–3 coats in total. On the linseed-oil line, surface-dry about 1–2 days and hard-dry 3–7 days (empirical values; they swing widely with temperature and humidity; postpone in rainy weather). **Painting the next coat over a face that has not hard-dried is the leading cause of films that never dry** — better to stop work for three days than to rush that half day.

5. **Observations to record**: pigment batch number and color step; oil type and degree of cooking (tung oil beading in water / linseed clear); powder-to-oil ratio and thread length; derusting method and days dried through; date of each coat, surface-dry and hard-dry start and end (recorded to the day); number of coats; test panel numbers and results (1 month outdoors / 7 days' water soak); lead-bearing part numbers and red bar marks; operator and weather (rain postponements logged).

6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = test panels 1 month outdoors without peeling or chalking, or 7 days' water soak without blistering; rework = heavy sagging and brush marks (fine-sand after hard-dry and add a coat) / local non-drying (strip and re-brush, check substrate moisture); scrap = the oil has gelled into lumps (sieve out the clear liquid for downgraded use, discard the gel) / the substrate has rusted and lifted (back to the pickle).

7. **Common failures and diagnosis order** (by frequency): ① not drying (most common: next coat over a face not hard-dry, damp substrate, oil under-cooked or already gelled, rain and low temperature) → stop and wait for hard-dry, strip and re-brush, change to properly cooked oil; ② blistering (damp substrate, or not dried through after derusting) → strip back to the base, dry through, repaint; ③ peeling (derusting incomplete, pickle residue not rinsed off) → back to the pickle, rinse through, dry through before painting; ④ sagging (paint too thin, one coat too heavy) → thicken, apply thin coats; ⑤ film wrinkling (tung oil not cooked through, one coat too thick) → re-cook to the beading point, thin each coat; ⑥ uneven color (pigment not ground fine, paint mixed in portions) → mix the whole batch at once, grind grit-free; ⑦ obvious brush marks (stiff, clogged bristles, paint too thick) → change the brush, thin the paint, brush crosswise.

8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = 3 test panels archived (with ratio, thread length, drying days); L2 = three batches consistent and the component ledger continuous; L3 = a complete component ledger (which component, which paint, what date, by whom) with lead-bearing red bar marks matching part numbers one to one, and a second person mixing paint from this card to the same thread length and drying calendar.

### 23.14 Hydrofluoric Acid: Register Only, Do Not Implement (Boundary Section)

**The reaction itself**: fluorite powder + concentrated sulfuric acid → hydrofluoric acid gas, absorbed in water. The feedstock (fluorite registered in Section 23.2) and the apparatus (an absorption build the same as the Section 23.9.4 three-jar hydrochloric line) are both within local capability — **technically feasible**. This manual nonetheless designates it a book-wide forbidden zone: **register the principle only; do not implement, do not trial-produce, do not "just run a small test"**. The full reasons are laid out below, so successors can re-examine whether this boundary still holds:

**Reason one: it wounds bone, and there is no cure.** Hydrofluoric acid burns differ fundamentally from the three acids — the fluoride ion penetrates the skin and strikes deep tissue and bone directly; at first there may be nothing but an unremarkable small red patch, then, hours to days later, agony and deep ulceration. And **no local production line can produce the antidote** (calcium gels and the like are on no production line of this chapter), so the main three-acid burn protocol — "flush with clean water + neutralize with ash lye water" — is largely useless against it. Glass vessels cannot hold it (it dissolves glass) — only lead-lined or wax-lined vessels work, which stacks lead-poisoning risk on top. A chemical that cannot be survived must not enter local production lines, however useful it is.

**Reason two: the uses are tiny and the risk enormous.** Its early uses are only glass engraving and, far later, silicon purification — both small-quantity, non-survival-critical uses whose benefit/risk ratio simply does not hold. Worse: dilute hydrofluoric acid has almost no irritating smell, so a leak is hard to notice. Leaks of the three acids you can see and smell; this one you cannot — colorless, silent, pain that arrives late: the worst class of leak.

**Alternative routes (all implementation-grade, usable immediately)**: abrasive-grinding engraving — scribe the design with a steel needle or a scriber (Chapter 20 marking tools), then grind along the lines with an oilstone or grinding wheel dipped in a fine-abrasive water slurry, giving frosted figure-work; or thin-coat the vessel surface with beeswax → needle-etch through the wax → abrade the exposed glass with a fine-sand water slurry → remove the wax with hot water. The results cover every practical need for vessel marking, graduations, and ornament — no need to open the forbidden zone.

**Unlock-conditions checklist (reconsider only when all are met; if one is missing, do not move)**:
1. Lead-lined / wax-lined vessels manufactured and passed leak testing;
2. A calcium-antidote production line built and holding stock (after the sanitation-line expansion has been formally approved as a project);
3. A dedicated fume hood + a written and drilled calcium-antidote emergency plan for operators;
4. A registered bulk demand on file (e.g., silicon purification formally approved as a project).

Until then, fluorite's identity on the resource map is only "metallurgical flux candidate", and its hydrofluoric route stays registration-frozen. **The existence of this boundary is itself part of this chapter's lesson**: something within capability may still be declined because "it cannot be survived".

## Safety Boundary

- **The three strong-acid enemies**: oil of vitriol (sulfuric acid) charring burns; nitric acid yellow fumes attacking the lungs; hydrochloric acid white fumes choking the lungs — **everything outdoors or in a cross-ventilated shed; stand upwind or crosswind; goggles + grease-impregnated gloves + wooden tongs**; eye splashes flushed at once with clean water ≥15 min (link to Chapter 44); **acid into water, water into acid forever forbidden** (when diluting, run the acid slowly down the rod into the water).
- **Receiving-vessel red lines**: nitric acid in glass only; delivery tubes never below the liquid; leave pressure relief in every distillation system.
- Strong alkali (causticized lye) handling as in v1; tasting strictly forbidden; lead vessels never enter the food or reagent lines.
- SO₂/SO₃ tail gas from pyrite roasting and dry distillation must pass through the ash-lye absorption bottle; hydrogen sulfide risk of Leblanc waste residue and the residue-heap red line.
- Ventilation for open-flame distillation rooms follows the Chapter 6 CO rules.
- **The ten-addition dilution method is a hard site-wide procedure**: water into acid forever forbidden; add slowly down the rod in ten portions; the floating-egg method is forbidden on acid of medium band and above; unlabeled liquid is treated as the concentrated band (Section 23.10).
- **The three red-lead Iron Laws**: dedicated person, tools, and clothing; forbidden on tableware, food containers, water-bucket interiors, and granary interiors; lead-painted articles marked with a red bar. Zero vessel sharing between the red-lead workstation and the food line (Section 23.13, linking Section 17.8).
- Boiling tung oil is a designated high-fire-risk operation: outdoor sand bath; dry sand and wet burlap sacks in place; a dedicated watch person who never leaves the post (Section 23.13).
- **Hydrofluoric acid is a book-wide forbidden zone**: register only, do not implement (Section 23.14); before the unlock-conditions checklist is complete, any trial production is a violation; fluorite is registered only as a metallurgical flux candidate.
