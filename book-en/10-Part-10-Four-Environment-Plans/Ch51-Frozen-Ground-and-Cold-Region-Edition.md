## Chapter Objectives

Presents the complete reconstruction execution plan for cold/frozen-ground conditions: frozen-ground type determination → five-stage route → special-risk sections. This chapter introduces no new technology; it does only **reordering and cold-region conversion**; the core proposition is to turn "overwintering" from a survival problem into a production rhythm.


## Capability Boundaries

**Can do**: overwinter survival systems (insulation / seasonal storage / CO discipline); continuous winter production in dugout workshops; bog iron–birch tar local metallurgy (annual output ceiling governed by the swamp regeneration rate); dual-power overwintering and ice-road transport; a scheduling system in which "winter does not reduce output".
**Explicitly cannot do**: large buildings in permafrost regions (no route, registered as-is); heavy-load winter lubrication (no solution, run at reduced speed); changing the length of the ice season (adapt by scheduling only).
**External dependencies**: labor-intensive seasonal fuel storage (an all-hands autumn project); the annual bog iron reserve ledger (data from two or more winters).

## Environmental Assumptions (Assessment Table Conclusions)

| Item | Typical cold/frozen-ground answer | Route consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Heat | The primary survival resource | Insulation = the first "material" |

### 51.1 Stage One: Heat and Survival Systems

- **Insulating structure before heat generation**: semi-subterranean dugout / sod-roof house (cold-region edition of Chapter 13: dug half a meter into the ground + log walls + sod roof, with an entrance bend as a windbreak); **small space, high density** (per-capita volume squeezed to the minimum); double-layer hide door curtains;
- Heating: stone-built kang-style heat-storage stove (one burn warms the whole night — the stone heat-storage mass is frozen ground's "battery"); **CO discipline doubled** (sealed space + heating = the most dangerous combination under the Chapter 6 red line; vents-stay-open rule);
- Long-term food preservation: natural ice cellar (ice harvested in winter and buried in sawdust cellars — frozen ground comes with its own "cold store"); fish oil and dried meat (Chapter 10 + fishing-hunting); **winter calorie ration ledger**: per-capita daily fuel consumption measured and registered; autumn storage = winter consumption × ice-season days × 30% redundancy (empirical formula, must be field-calibrated locally);
- Freeze-proof water supply: insulated wellhead cover + deeply buried water lines (below the frozen-ground active layer), or winter snow melting (snow-melt fuel counted in the water ledger — **water in frozen ground carries an energy cost too**).

### 51.2 Stage Two: Establishing a Stable Workspace

- An insulated workshop = the precondition of industry: **dugout workshop** (excavated halfway into the frozen ground, buffered by ground temperature); furnace-kiln zone separated from the precision-work zone (one end wants heat, the other wants constant temperature);
- Dry storage of raw materials and tools: condensation and frost — cold items entering the house first warm up and shed frost in a "transition room" before going into storage (metal parts rust the moment they see frost; the real point of frozen-ground moisture protection is frost protection);
- **Low-temperature materials discipline**: cast iron embrittles at low temperature (using cast iron for impact tools in winter is strictly forbidden — cold-region edition of the Chapter 17 red line); lubricants graded by temperature (animal fat hardens when cold; thin oil + graphite for winter, Chapter 21); sealing leather hardens — full grease-change maintenance before winter.

### 51.3 Stage Three: Minerals and the Metallurgical Route

- **Bog iron (the frozen ground's natural gift)**: limonite nodules on lake-marsh and river beds (deposited by iron bacteria) — **chopped out through the ice in winter, sun-dried and roasted** (dewatering and desulfurization), they become premium feedstock for the Chapter 18 bloomery furnace; Scandinavia's centuries of ironmaking history prove this route sustainable (moderate output ceiling — enough for tools, not for large industry; registered as-is);
- Whole use of the birch: **birch tar** (bark dry distillation, Chapter 23 route) = all-purpose glue and preservative (Chapter 7 tool handles, Chapter 15 leather, boat seams); birch wood = utensils and fuel;
- **Soapstone (if present)**: unfired pottery carved directly into pots (a transitional solution for fuel-less frozen-ground zones) + furnace lining material;
- Metallurgical-season scheduling: concentrate blast smelting in summer (fuel dries easily, open-air work); forging and finishing in winter (indoor processes); coal (if outcrops exist) reserved first for metallurgy and kilns; dwellings burn wood and peat (cold-region execution of Chapter 23's "good fuels go to high temperature first").

### 51.4 Stage Four: Power and Machinery

- The river ice-locked period = half of the annual hydropower ledger as a blind spot: **dual-power configuration mandatory** (cold-region edition of the Chapter 29 redundancy law): summer waterwheel + winter animal power / atmospheric (Newcomen-type) steam engine (if the fuel ledger allows);
- Ice damage to hydraulic structures (ice-floe strikes, ice expansion): waterwheel sluice gates inspected before autumn, ice channels cleared before the spring flood (two rows added to the annual inspection table);
- Low-temperature starting and sealing: annual "pre-winter servicing" for machinery (oil change, clearance recheck, belt tensioning — Chapter 21's weekly inspection made annual);
- Snow transport (the frozen ground's "highway"): sledges + snowshoes (Chapter 7 woodworking), winter ice-surface freight convoys — **winter is in fact the bulk transport season** (frozen-ground swampland is impassable in summer).

### 51.5 Stage Five: Electrical and Precision Industry

- Low-temperature battery management: lead-acid capacity plunges at low temperature, and a deeply discharged battery is freeze-damaged — **the battery room must be heated** (a dugout battery room); winter charge/discharge parameters get their own table (cold-region edition of the Chapter 32 density table);
- Conductor icing and tower snow loads: line patrols doubled in frequency for winter; spot checks for cold-brittled porcelain insulators (Chapter 32 seasonal inspection tightened);
- Constant temperature for the precision shop: dugout + furnace waste heat ducted in = a natural constant-temperature room (**the cold of frozen ground is conversely a free constant-temperature resource** — stable in summer too; both the Chapter 34 Czochralski furnace and the Chapter 35 photolithography alignment benefit);
- Electronics (Chapters 33–36): silicon-device characteristic drift at low temperature is measurable and controllable (the Chapter 33 temperature-coefficient table serves this); packaging is oriented to surviving thermal cycling (resin potting + cushioned structures).

### 51.6 Special Sections for the Frozen-Ground Edition

1. **Frozen-ground building risk**: thaw subsidence (a warm building melts the frozen foundation, which settles) — raised ventilated floors (a cold-air break) or pile foundations crossing the active layer; **overwinter settlement observation** made a formal institution (Chapter 3 measurement + Chapter 13 building, combined);
2. **Snow- and ice-melt drainage**: spring meltwater is a flood peak — drainage ditches dredged before autumn, supplies marked with a raised line;
3. **Seasonal reserves**: the three great seasonal-storage ledgers of fuel / food / spare parts (winter road closure = external resupply at zero; the daily-life edition of the Chapter 29 downtime redundancy law);
4. **Low-temperature effects table for metal/seals/lubrication**: list "winter substitutions and maintenance items" workstation by workstation (a consolidated table of the 51.2 items, posted on the wall);
5. **Continuous winter production**: days are short — lighting (Chapter 32) is in the frozen-ground edition a "means of production," not a consumer good; work hours rearranged by daylight (precision work on day shift, furnaces on night shift).

### 51.7 Failure: Which Line Breaks First in Cold Regions

In order of first failure. Omens written on the duty board.

| No. | Failure line | Omen | Consequence chain |
|---|---|---|---|
| ① | **Low-temperature failure of lubrication and seals** (Chapters 21/28) | Grease turns hard and white; leather seals harden and leak air | Bearings run dry → shaft destroyed; bellows leak air → furnace temperature collapses |
| ② | **Cold brittleness of cast iron and steel** (Chapters 17/18) | Tapping sounds brittle at low temperature; fracture surfaces flat and even | Hammering a cast-iron part in winter = shatters on the spot, injuring people |
| ③ | **Fuel miscalculation** (insufficient seasonal storage / wet wood) | Inner layers of the woodpile form an ice shell; the kang cannot hold heat through the night | Hypothermia → loss of personnel → whole plant shut down — **the frozen ground's first cause of death is cold, not hunger** |
| ④ | **Water system freezing** (wells / ditches / pipes) | Taps run dry at night; ice cones at the wellhead | Whole plant loses water; pipes and fittings crack from freezing |
| ⑤ | **Battery capacity plunge** (Chapter 32) | Winter charge/discharge times stretch abnormally long | Night lighting and telegraph cut off |
| ⑥ | **Thaw subsidence** (warm building melts the foundation) | Thresholds sink; new diagonal cracks on walls (Chapter 13 settlement-crack pattern) | Building tilts → furnace/kiln bases misaligned |
| ⑦ | **Spring flood and ice drift** | Changes in snow albedo; river ice turning crumbly and gray (structural whitening) | Hydraulic structures smashed; low-lying workshops flooded |

### 51.8 Maintenance: Winter Frequency Doubling and Pre-Winter Servicing

| Item | Normal cycle | Frozen-ground cycle |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-winter servicing (oil / clearances / belts) | Annual inspection | **Mandatory annual inspection + completed before first frost** (before the first hard frost) |
| Heat-storage stove flue | Seasonal cleaning | **Monthly cleaning** (low-temperature exhaust flue gas easily accumulates tar and soot — chimney fires are a frozen-ground specialty) |
| Ice-surface clearance | — | **Ice thickness measured every ten days** (bore and measure: on foot ≥2 fists, sledge loads ≥3 fists; empirical, needs calibration; early spring and late winter prohibited outright) |
| Thaw-subsidence observation | — | **Weekly** (observation stakes + threshold scribe lines; Chapter 13 scribe-mark method) |
| Battery room temperature | Weekly inspection | **Daily inspection** (heated room lower limit = hand-feel "not cold" level) |
| Frozen food store | Monthly stocktake | Ten-day stocktake (freeze–thaw cycles = accelerated spoilage; thawed-then-refrozen food is discarded) |

[Card 51-02 Pre-Winter Servicing] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: every machine and tool in the settlement (belts, bearings, hydraulics or water systems, leather parts); grease, thin oil, graphite grease, leather dressing. **Must be finished before the first hard frost** — servicing after the frost sets in is work done in frozen ground for nothing.
2. **Tools required**: wrenches, grease gun, tensioning tools, drain or insulation materials. Calibration source: clearances and belt tension rechecked with Chapter 20 rules, not by feel.
3. **Sequence and control points (tick every item, none skippable)**: (1) grease changed to thin oil or graphite grease (cold viscosity) → (2) all leather parts redressed → (3) clearances rechecked → (4) belt tension set → (5) water systems drained or insulated. Control points: **every item ticked and signed** (one omission parks a machine all winter) and **completion before the first frost** — the deadline is itself a criterion.
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: the annual inspection is upgraded to **mandatory plus pre-frost completion**; the heat-storage stove flue goes from seasonal to **monthly cleaning** (cold flue gases leave tar and soot — chimney fires are a cold-region specialty); battery-room temperature from weekly to **daily** check (the "not cold to the hand" level in the heated room is the floor); frost-heave observation **weekly** (witness posts and threshold marks, Chapter 13 scoring method).
5. **Observations to record**: per machine — servicing date / signature on each item / grease and oil types / measured clearances / water system action / first start-up after winter sets in.
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = all five items ticked and done before frost; rework = an item missed (make it up, but once past the frost do it indoors after warming); scrap = water system froze and split (replace the parts).
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) machines seize in the first week of winter → grease was not changed to thin oil and set solid; (2) belt snaps → tension never rechecked; (3) water system splits → draining not done, or half done; (4) frost-heave cracks found in spring → weekly observation was not run, the early signs were missed.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one winter's per-machine servicing sheet with all five items; L2 = two consecutive winters with no downtime or freeze damage from missed servicing; L3 = a second person runs a pre-winter service from this card alone, with matching clearance readings.

[Card 51-03 Three Winter-Stock Ledgers] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: fuel (firewood, charcoal, oil), food (grain, dried fish, meat), spares (belts, edges, rope, nails).
2. **Tools required**: balance (Chapter 3), storehouse and bin marking, inventory sheets. Calibration source: weighing and ten-day stocktaking, never "looks like enough".
3. **Sequence and control points**: estimate winter consumption → compute the autumn-stock target from the formula → stock each item before autumn ends → **stocktake every ten days** → ration as soon as half remains. Control points: **the ice-period days in the formula must be locally measured** (not an average), and the redundancy factor may not be dropped.
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: **autumn stock = winter consumption × ice-period days × 1.3** (empirical formula, ice days measured locally); ten-day stocktakes; **red line: rationing starts when half the stock remains** — not when it is nearly gone.
5. **Observations to record**: for each of the three — target amount / actually stocked / ten-day balances / consumption rate / date rationing began.
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = all three at target before winter and stocktakes unbroken; rework = an item short, emergency procurement or production rescheduling in late autumn; scrap = runs out mid-winter (this season's rationing regime failed; the estimation formula must be reviewed).
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) fuel gone before spring → ice days estimated from memory, recompute from measurements; (2) food in the frozen store thaws and refreezes → ten-day stocktaking skipped; freeze-thaw cycling accelerates spoilage and **anything thawed and refrozen is discarded**; (3) a stoppage for want of a spare → consumables were stocked but not wearing parts; (4) rationing never started at the half mark → the red line was not enforced.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one season's three ledgers (target, stocked, ten-day balances); L2 = two consecutive seasons complete and never running out; L3 = a second person estimates a season from this card alone and stocks to it, estimates within 10%.

### 51.11 Kang Heat-Storage Stove Construction Card (Card 51-04)

The heat-storage stove is frozen ground's "heat battery" — the principle of one burn warming the whole night is **stone-bed heat storage + a long flue**:

- **Construction**: firebox (stone-built, interior clear size about 2 chi square) → stone-slab kang surface (3–5 courses of large flat slabs stacked, each bedded on refractory mortar — a gap leaking smoke = CO red line) → the flue snakes through the kang interior for 2–3 bends (**total flue length ≥4 m, empirical** — too short and the heat runs off with the smoke; too long and draft is insufficient and smoke reverses) → smoke outlet higher than the eaves (12.3: low in, high out);
- **Firing regime**: one hard burn of 1–2 h before sleep (three stages: kindling — full fire — banked fire); once the fire bed is sealed under ash, the stone bed releases heat slowly all night; in the morning the kang surface "tolerates a long touch of the back of the hand" (40–50 ℃ band) = pass;
- **Acceptance**: a new kang's first firing must be a "curing burn" — three days of empty small fire to drive out moisture (a wet stone bed will condense water and steam the first night); every flue joint leak-checked with a smoke thread (burn damp grass in the firebox; wherever a seam smokes, patch it with clay);
- **CO red line restated**: smoke leaking through kang seams is an odorless night killer — **one smoke-thread leak check per month + watching the flame of an indoor torch** (a weakening flame triggers a ventilation check).

[Card 51-04 Kang Heat-Storage Stove: Construction and Firing Regime] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: stone for the firebox (internal clear about **2 chi square**), large flat stone slabs (3–5 layers for the kang surface), refractory clay mortar, ash (for banking), fuel.
2. **Tools required**: masonry tools, damp straw for the smoke-line leak test, a torch for flame watching indoors. Calibration source: CO leakage is found by **smoke-line testing** and **torch flame observation**, never by smell — CO has none.
3. **Sequence and control points**: stone firebox → long flue (the heat-storage body) → 3–5 layers of slab for the kang surface with **refractory clay mortar bedded between slabs** (**a leaking joint is a CO red line**) → **three days of gentle test firing on a new stove** (dry-out: a damp stone bed condenses water and steams the first night) → normal firing regime. Control points: **bedding the slab joints** and **the three-day dry firing** — economizing on either costs lives.
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: firebox clear about 2 chi square; 3–5 slab layers; **firing regime: one hard burn of 1–2 h before sleep (kindle — full fire — bank), the banked ember bed releasing heat through the stone all night**; a morning kang surface you can hold the back of your hand against (40–50 °C) is the normal band; **smoke-line leak test monthly plus torch flame check indoors** (a weakening flame means ventilate and investigate).
5. **Observations to record**: per firing — start and end times / the three phases' durations / banking time / morning hand test / flue cleaning date / leak test result.
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = morning surface holds the hand, leak test shows no seep, indoor flame normal; rework = surface stays cold (too few slab layers or flue too short), or a leak (lift slabs and re-bed); scrap = firebox cracked through (rebuild; do not patch).
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) waking cold at night → the three firing phases were not completed (especially banking), or too few stone layers; (2) headache and nausea indoors → **open windows, evacuate, check the flue for leakage — this is CO poisoning**; (3) chimney fire → flue not cleaned monthly, tar accumulation; (4) part of the surface stays cold → flue routing short-circuits the gas path.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one stove built with dry-firing and first-month firing records; L2 = a full winter fired to the regime, no CO incident, monthly leak tests unbroken; L3 = a second person builds one from this card alone and passes both the leak test and the dry-firing acceptance.

### 51.12 Winter Bog-Iron Harvesting and Roasting (Card 51-05)

- **Winter harvesting**: select iron-rich stretches of lakes and marshes (marked in summer: oily-film water color / rusty springs on the bank); after ice clearance per Card 51-01, chop ice holes (1 chi square, spaced 3 paces apart in a line); a long-pole scoop (hemp net bag + hardwood pole) dragged along the bottom — the nodules (limonite spheres, 1–5 cm in diameter) have a distinct feel on the bottom (◎ drag feel: sand is soft, spheres are hard; half a day of practice makes it recognizable); scoop each hole 20–30 min; daily output 30–80 kg of raw ore (empirical, must be field-calibrated);
- **Sorting**: drop nodules into water — sinking = real (iron nodule density 3–4); floating = mud egg, discard;
- **Roasting (critical — removes water and organic matter)**: spread on a stone-slab bed 3 fingers thick; gentle fire → strong fire for 2–4 h, turned twice — criteria: color turns dark red, rings clean when struck, no black core in the fracture (black core = organic matter not burned out; in the furnace it absorbs heat and makes slag); record roasting weight loss (weight loss around 30% is the normal band, empirical);
- **Into the furnace**: roasted nodules are the Chapter 18 bloomery furnace's "selected ore" (grade about 40–55%; bog iron's advantage is **almost no gangue** — less slag, less charcoal);
- **Reserve ledger**: two winters of harvesting data + a swamp-area estimate → "annual iron output" enters the Chapter 38 ledger — bog iron is sustainable, but **the annual output ceiling is governed by the swamp's regeneration rate** (iron-bacteria deposition adds millimeters per year; overdraft means exhaustion).

[Card 51-05 Winter Bog-Iron Harvesting and Roasting] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: bog-iron concretions (**marked in summer** — iridescent films on water, rusty springs at the shore; find the rich beds before winter); fuel for roasting. Ice must first be cleared per Card 51-01.
2. **Tools required**: ice chisel (cutting **1 chi square** holes), long-handled rake or basket, stone slab bed for roasting, mallet for the ring test. Calibration source: grade and roast completion read from the fracture and the ring, not from instruments.
3. **Sequence and control points**: ice cleared → chisel holes (**1 chi square, one every 3 paces**) → haul concretions → **sort by dropping in water (sinks = true ore, floats = mud balls, discard)** → roast (spread **3 fingers thick** on the slab bed, gentle fire then strong for **2–4 h, turned twice**) → charge the furnace. Control points: the water-drop sort and the turning count.
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: holes 1 chi square, one per 3 paces; roast criterion — **color turns dull red, rings clear, no black core on the break** (a black core means organics not burned out, which eats charcoal in the furnace); grade about **40–55%**; bog iron's advantage is **almost no gangue**, so little slag and less charcoal; smelting season concentrated in summer, winter forging only (production red line).
5. **Observations to record**: per winter — bed locations / hole count / total concretions / discard ratio from sorting / roast batches and turning counts / fracture readings / iron yield.
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = dull red, clear ring, no black core, normal slag in the furnace; rework = black cores, spread out and roast again; scrap = heavy mud-ball contamination (the water-drop sort was skipped).
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) excessive slag in the furnace → under-roasted or poorly sorted; (2) furnace will not hold heat and charcoal burn soars → roasting incomplete, organics competing for oxygen inside; (3) mostly mud balls hauled → the summer marking was wrong, re-mark the beds; (4) holes caving in → went out on ice below clearance, back to Card 51-01.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one winter's harvest and roasting records plus one acceptable furnace charge; L2 = two consecutive winters of data, with annual iron output and bog-area estimate entered in the Chapter 38 ledger; L3 = a second person runs a winter's work from this card alone and produces acceptable ore.

[Card 51-01 Ice Thickness Measurement and Clearance] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: the ice surface to be measured (river or lake reach); a pole or rope as a safety line, one measuring and one watching. **This card governs lives**; the clearance criteria are not negotiable.
2. **Tools required**: ice drill or chisel (to cut through), a graduated sounding rod (Chapter 3), colored marker flags. Calibration source: thickness measured with the graduated rod — **never "it looks thick enough"**.
3. **Sequence and control points**: **three-point sampling** (near shore, center, and any doubtful spot, one hole each) → measure thickness → **take the minimum** as the criterion for that reach → set the clearance class from the table → plant flags → re-survey every ten days. Control points: **minimum of three points** (ice varies; an average will lie) and **re-measure at once when temperatures rise sharply**, do not wait for the next ten-day round.
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: person on foot ≥2 fists of ice, sledge load ≥3 fists (empirical, calibrate to local ice quality); **red line: late winter and early spring are closed regardless — no argument** — that ice is "white ice," structurally unsound however thick; re-survey every ten days.
5. **Observations to record**: per survey — date and temperature / three thickness readings / the minimum used / clearance class / flag positions / interval to next survey.
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = minimum meets the class and it is not a closed period; rework = short of class, narrow the clearance (foot traffic to closed) or detour; scrap = gray-blue ice or water seeping through (structure gone) — close that reach entirely.
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) a vehicle goes through → an average or single reading was used; (2) the same reach held yesterday and collapsed today → spring or thermal influence under the ice, not avoided at route selection; (3) late-spring ice thick enough yet failing → the closed-period red line was ignored; (4) readings vary widely → flowing water or air pockets beneath, take the minimum and add measurement points.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one complete three-point survey with a clearance decision; L2 = a full ice season with no gap in the ten-day surveys, and at least one active downgrade or closure in response to falling readings; L3 = a second person judges the same reach from this card alone, reaching the same clearance class.

### 51.13 Ice-Road and Sledge Transport Regulations

- **Ice-road route selection**: straight river reaches, slow current (freezes solid in winter), avoiding spring eyes and warm-spring influence stretches (locally thin ice) — route survey identical to Card 51-01's ten-day re-measurement;
- **Sledge specifications**: hardwood bottom board (oak/birch) + bottom surface **poured with water to freeze an ice shell** (the ice shell's friction coefficient is far below a wood surface — on freezing nights pour water three times to form the shell; re-pour before each trip); load ≤1.5 times the dry-season cart load (ice-bearing redundancy);
- **Convoy discipline**: spacing between sledges ≥10 paces (one ice crack does not claim the whole convoy); heavy sledges take the old ruts (compacted old ruts are safer); **during spring thaw the ice road closes along its whole length regardless of load** (structural degradation);
- **Efficiency ledger**: ice-road freight efficiency is 1.5–2 times that of dry-season dirt roads (empirical) — **winter is the frozen ground's bulk transport season**, the ledger expression of the 51.4 conclusion: seasonal redistribution of transport labor hours.

### 51.14 Dugout Workshop Construction Specifications

- **Excavation depth**: half into the frozen-ground active layer (about 1–1.2 m, fixed after measured frost depth) — digging into the permafrost layer means the summer-thaw/winter-freeze cycle destroys the walls; **dig only into the active layer**;
- **Structure**: round-log cribbed walls (each course interlocked, seams chinked with moss) + round-log floor grillage + double floor (ventilation channel underneath guards against thaw subsidence — the 51.6 gate) + sod pitched roof (soil cover ≥1 chi);
- **Zoning**: furnace end (kilns, forging — flue straight through the roof) and precision end (measurement, assembly — the constant-temperature end) separated by one insulating wall; the temperature difference between the two ends can reach the 20 ℃ scale (empirical);
- **Vestibule mandatory**: double-door buffer (prevents heat loss on door opening + keeps frost from striking straight into the precision end);
- **Acceptance**: on an outdoor −30 ℃ class night (the team's historical extreme band), the precision end holds ≥5 ℃ and the furnace end remains workable, with the fuel ledger within Chapter 37 quotas.

### 51.15 Using the Constant-Temperature Resource (Free Precision Room)

The cold of the frozen ground is an enemy and also a resource: **the underground constant-temperature layer's temperature barely changes all year** (below the active layer it stays near the annual mean year-round; 0–4 ℃ level in cold regions) —
- The dugout precision end's temperature drift is far smaller than a surface building's (surface: 50 ℃ annual drift; dugout: within 10 ℃, empirical) — **the "constant-temperature discipline" of Chapter 22 micrometer verification and Chapter 35 photolithography alignment is naturally half met in frozen ground**;
- Winter precision scheduling: the processes most afraid of temperature drift (gauge calibration, wafer measurement, instrument assembly) are fixedly scheduled at the dugout end, and kept clear of the furnace end's firing periods (firing raises the whole dugout by 2–3 ℃; calibration pieces must be offset in time from the furnace);
- The food ice cellar (51.1) and the constant-temperature precision room share the same ground-temperature principle — the one-pit-two-use design is entered as a Chapter 13 building variant.

### 51.16 Frozen-Ground Farming and Fisheries: Fish-Run Calendar, Dried-Fish Line, and Cellared Vegetables

The frozen-ground growing season cannot feed the whole team; fishing and storage are the backbone of the winter protein ledger — this section rearranges Chapter 10's farm-storage processes for the cold region, introducing no new equipment.

**Fish-run calendar (a forecasting system for the spring shore-landing window)**:
- Principle: fish wintering under the ice come shoreward to feed during the melt period; the window is short (about 10–20 days start to finish, floating with ice conditions — empirical, needs local calibration); missing the window = losing a whole winter's protein ledger;
- The three-piece calendar kit, recorded for two consecutive years: ① ice-crack day (the day structural water-permeable cracks appear in the ice) ② first-catch day (shore catch first reaches ≥10 kg of fish in a single day) ③ water-temperature signal (≥4 ℃ for 3 consecutive days at 10 cm water depth near shore); the three are cross-compared year by year into a calendar; from the third year the window can be forecast (calibration method: back-substitution testing year by year; forecast error ≤3 days = usable; if out of tolerance, add an air-temperature item the next year);
- Under-ice winter fishing kit: chop ice holes and set nets (hole-chopping process same as Card 51-05; hole diameter matched to net width); net-hauling discipline — **shake the net the instant it leaves the water, strip the fish the instant they leave the net** (a wet net freezes hard within 10 min in low temperature; a hard net's strands snap at a single fold);
- Scheduling linkage: the fish-run window = the raw-material flood peak of Card 51-07's dried-fish line; processing manpower rostered half a month ahead (the food version of the Chapter 29 redundancy law: losing people during the run = a ledger shortfall).

**Dried-fish line (cold-region edition of Chapter 10 drying and Chapter 53 fish salt-drying; full operating text in Card 51-07)**:
- Cold-region advantage: cool shade drying naturally suppresses bacteria and fly maggots; **salt can be cut to under half the temperate formula** (with daytime temperature in the <10 ℃ band, salt-free shade drying is possible);
- A cold-region-specific reverse risk: the repeated freeze–thaw of day-sunning and night-freezing cracks the flesh tissue and squeezes out water — dehydration is fast but the tissue turns mushy, storage life shortens, and pests are attracted; **rule: out by day, in by night**, or favor shade drying in a ventilated shed (no direct sun, through-draft) — shade drying is the proper cold-region road; sun drying is the backup;
- Dryness criterion: single-fish dry weight = 30–35% of fresh weight (empirical range, calibrated on the first run); ◎ hand-feel band: fold the fish double — the backbone snaps while the flesh face shows no water = pass; practice method: on the first run take 10 fish, weigh them through the whole process, and build an archive (archive kept in the ledger); thereafter practice 2 runs comparing the fold-snap feel against the archive; misjudgment compensation: any hand-feel doubt goes back on the scale for re-weighing.

**Cellared vegetables (freeze-proof retrofit of the Chapter 10 cellar)**:
- The only hard retrofit: **burial depth below the frozen-ground line** — the cellar floor must lie below the local maximum frost depth (measured frost depth entered in the Chapter 3 overwinter archive; with no data, dig test pits and measure first; copying the convenient southern depth is forbidden);
- The frozen-ground cellar's risk is "too cold and freeze–thaw," not "not cold enough" (51.8 already lists thawed-then-refrozen = discard): target cellar temperature 0–4 ℃; ◎ temperature-control criterion (bowl-ice method): hang a bowl of water at the cellar mouth — a full bowl of ice by morning = too cold (add a straw seal layer), no ice = too warm (enlarge the vent), a one-finger ice shell not filling the bowl = pass; practice method: calibrate by comparing two consecutive ten-day periods of bowl appearance against actual vegetable condition at cellar opening; misjudgment compensation: one max-min thermometer (Chapter 3 temperature-measurement item) kept in the cellar;
- Stacking and cellar-entry safety: frost-hardy vegetables (Chinese cabbage, turnip) by the door side; frost-tender vegetables (potato, radish) in the middle and upper layers; **potatoes turn green and poisonous in light** — use a shaded lamp with weak light inside the cellar; lamp test before entering (Chapter 6 oxygen-deficiency red line: carry a lantern in; if the flame weakens or goes out, the person withdraws and the cellar is ventilated); cellar work in two-person teams.

**Short-season crop seed selection (cold-region reweighting of Chapter 10's three criteria)**:
- Weights become: **earliness > cold hardiness > yield** — without enough growing days, the yield criterion is meaningless;
- Hard criterion: keep only varieties with "growing period ≤ local frost-free period × 0.8" as seed stock (20% redundancy against early frost); fields still green on first-frost day are registered as "late-ripening abandoned fields" and excluded from seed stock the following year;
- Directed domestication: each year, separately harvest and store the earliest-ripening 10% of plants as seed — after 5–8 consecutive generations clear earliness is visible (empirical; register ripening date and grain weight yearly in the ledger; a break in the generations undoes all prior work).

### 51.17 Fur and Leather Processing (Cold-Region Edition of Chapter 15)

In frozen ground, pelts are both clothing raw material and hard currency of trade. Chapter 15's tanning mostly takes dehaired leather as its main line; the cold-region specialization is **hair-on tanning** — hair that does not mat, underfur that does not shed, a pelt that does not rot.

**Two routes of hair-on pelt tanning**:
- **Brain tanning (first choice for next-to-skin clothing)**: brush an emulsion of animal brains on the flesh side — 1–2 brains per pelt (the starting ratio to pelt area is empirical; in the first season build an archive of brain amounts matched to pelt sizes); mash the brain with warm water into an emulsion (enough that rubbing between fingers yields no grittiness), coat the flesh side fully, roll up and leave half a day, scrape off the excess and spread out; **half of success lies in stretching**: repeatedly pull and knead throughout, from half-dry to fully dry; a skin that hardens after drying = insufficient stretching; rework method: let the pelt re-dampen overnight wrapped in a wet cloth, then re-coat and re-stretch; pass criteria: rustles softly and feels supple when kneaded, no dead crease when folded, hair stands when shaken with the fur side out;
- **Smoke tanning (first choice for tent curtains and boot uppers)**: hang the pelt above a smoke pit to smoke; aldehydes in the smoke fix the skin while repelling insects and moisture; start with the pelt 30 cm from the smoke source (empirical; calibration: hold the back of your hand between pelt and smoke source — a long touch without scalding means the distance is right; if it scalds, raise it); smoke material: decayed wood and birch-bark shavings (resin-rich open-flame smoke turns sticky and deposits black — reject); turn the pelt every 2 h; 4–8 h in total;
- Combined route: brain tanning followed by smoke tanning is the most durable but doubles labor hours — choose by use and enter it in the ledger; the whole team's pelts may not all be double-processed (labor-hour discipline).

**Pelt grading (three tests, one unified trade standard)**:
- First test, underfur depth: part the guard hairs and look at the underfur layer — skin not visible = top-grade winter pelt; needle substitute: a fine needle inserted into the underfur disappears with no tip showing = top grade (this is the compensation for tactile misjudgment of the second test);
- Second test, skin softness: fold, squeeze, release — the crease smooths out at once = soft skin (usable for next-to-skin clothing); the crease stays = stiff skin (downgraded to straps and gear wraps);
- Third test, hair shedding: push the palm against the grain three times — no hair falls = sound; up to 3–5 hairs fall = downgraded to lining; hair falls in clumps = hair roots damaged (acid rot or decay already deep); shave the hair, keep the skin, handle as a separate case;
- ◎ grade-banding practice: blind-sort 20 pelts into three grades and compare against a skilled hand's results; ≤4 errors to graduate; misjudgment compensation is the needle test / fold test / stroke count from the three tests above — any doubt in hand feel defers to the physical-evidence tests; grading by eye alone is not permitted.

**Moth-proof winter storage**:
- The insect-damage window is the **warming-back period and summer cellar storage**, not deep winter: smoke once before storage (same pit as smoke tanning; doubles as insect proofing); hang mugwort or camphor bundles in the store (use if available locally); shake out and air once per ten days on a clear day (in ventilated shade; **direct sunlight forbidden** — sunning fades the underfur color and embrittles the skin);
- Bookkeeping: grading results enter the team ledger as "grade × count"; in trade, keep the standard unified — top-grade winter pelts and off-grade pelts differ severalfold in price; selling mixed grades = a loss on the books.

### 51.18 Hypothermia and Frostbite Protection (Chapter 44 Interface)

In frozen ground the bulk of casualties is cold injury, not industrial injury. This section is the cold-region specialization of the Chapter 44 medical general principles; the handling discipline is **trained to the whole team** — cold-injury first aid cannot wait for medics to arrive; the first handler is the workmate at your side.

**Hypothermia grading and rewarming procedure**:
- Grade by behavior, not by self-report (a hypothermia victim's self-assessment cannot be trusted): stage one, shivering phase — unceasing shivering, clumsy movements, normal answers; stage two, dullness phase — shivering weakens, speech slows, "paradoxical undressing" appears (feels hot and wants to remove clothing; an extremely dangerous sign); stage three, rigid phase — shivering stops, cannot walk unaided. **Shivering stopping is not improvement but worsening — the frozen ground's most lethal misjudgment** (posted verbatim on the duty board);
- Rewarming procedure (field handling for stages one and two): move into a warm room → strip wet clothes and change into dry → warm sugar water for anyone who can swallow → warm-water bags placed over clothing against the torso (bag temperature not above 45 ℃, the "long touch of the back of the hand without scalding" band); **rapid heating is forbidden (fire-roasting, pouring hot water directly, rubbing the limbs)** — principle: in severe hypothermia, the limbs' cold blood surging back to the core makes core temperature first fall and then destabilize (rewarming shock); and frost-numbed skin has lost sensation, so fire-roasting inevitably burns without the victim knowing;
- For stage three the field does one thing only: **stop further heat loss** — wrap in dry clothes and dry blankets, carry flat with no jolting (rough handling can trigger arrhythmia); rewarming is handed to medical care (Chapter 44 interface; transport observation records in Chapter 44 format);
- Observation record: consciousness, shivering, and breathing recorded every 10 min (a dedicated recorder for the whole rescue; reconstructing from memory afterward is not allowed).

**Four-degree frostbite interpretation and handling boundaries**:
- First degree, frost-reddened: pale turns red and swollen, tingling and itching — after rewarming and warming, apply grease; may remain on a light-duty post;
- Second degree, blisters: clear blisters appear after rewarming — **do not pierce them** (an intact blister is a sterile natural dressing); for blisters too large for bandaging, puncture-drain at the blister edge and keep the blister skin as a cover;
- Third degree (purplish-black necrosis through the full skin layer) and fourth degree (deep into muscle and bone): **on site, only keep warm and send for care; all debridement, piercing-scraping, and ointment attempts are forbidden** (Chapter 44 handling boundary; any field "treatment" manufactures infection);
- Rewarming operation: immerse the frozen limb in 38–42 ℃ water for 20–30 min — **water temperature must never be set by the casualty** (a frozen limb has no sensation and will certainly be scalded); the standard is a healthy person's elbow finding it acceptable; effective-rewarming criteria = skin reddens and sensation returns in the "numbness → pain → itch" sequence (**pain is good news**);
- Red lines: an un-rewarmed frozen limb must not be rubbed (ice crystals are cutting tissue; rubbing = doubled mechanical damage), must not be fire-roasted, must not be walked on (walking on frostbitten feet = crushing tissue with every step).

**Snow-blindness prevention (smoke-darkened slit goggles)**:
- Mechanism: strong light reflected off snow burns the cornea; symptoms **appear only that evening** (eye pain, tearing, photophobia) — same-day snowfield exposure is easily missed as the cause and mistaken for "caught a chill getting up at night";
- Making them: birch bark or thin wood slats with horizontal slits (slit length about 30 mm, slit width about 5 mm, empirical; calibration: being able to clearly make out the ground within three steps of your feet = slit width acceptable), inner face smoke-darkened to cut glare, cords on both sides, worn goggle-style;
- Handling: rest shielded from light 1–3 days, cold compress for pain; self-healing is possible; **repeated snow blindness = cumulative corneal damage** — goggles on snowfield work are discipline, not an option (add one line to the morning checklist: "goggles carried").

**Winter clothing layering system**:
- Three-layer system: inner layer wicks sweat (fine soft fabric next to the skin, **changed as soon as it is sweat-soaked** — wet underwear is hypothermia's first accomplice); middle layer stores heat (many thin fluffy layers beat one thick wrap; layer counts set for the three bands −10/−20/−30 ℃); outer layer blocks wind (fur or dense weave; the point is wind blocking);
- Work discipline: at the first light sweat from work, open the outer layer to shed heat; for static posts (night watch, ice fishing, sentry duty), changing into a dry inner layer before going on duty is mandatory; personnel with a frostbite history are registered by post (the risk of refreezing the same site is doubled);
- Morning mutual check: before setting out, two people face each other — white cheeks or fingertips (first-degree frostbite omens) are rubbed warm on the spot before work resumes; white patches that do not recede mean stopping work for treatment.

### 51.19 Frostbite Treatment Card (Card 51-06, eight-field abstract)

- **① Input materials**: warm water 38–42 ℃ (≥10 L per session, must submerge the injured limb), dry clothes and dry blankets, warm-water bags placed over clothing, warm sugar water, clean dressings; substitute: mild stage one/two cases may use mutual body-heat warming (armpit, abdomen); alcohol rubbing forbidden; snow rubbing forbidden.
- **② Required tools**: water thermometer (Chapter 3 temperature-measurement item, ice-point calibrated); emergency compensation: crushed-ice chips floating up in the water mean temperature out of tolerance — discard and do not use.
- **③ Operation sequence and control points**: remove frozen shoes and gloves (pour warm water where stuck to skin to separate; hard tearing forbidden) → immerse in 38–42 ℃ water 20–30 min → remove, wrap in dry dressings, elevate the injured limb → blisters not pierced → keep warm under observation or send for care. **Control point: water temperature maintained throughout; when changing or heating water, lift the injured limb out first, then add hot water**.
- **④ Empirical parameters and calibration**: water temperature 38–42 ℃ — calibration: a healthy person's elbow immersed 5 s finding it "warm but not hot" = the acceptable band; the rewarming-duration criterion takes precedence over the clock (stop when skin reddens and sensation gradually returns).
- **⑤ Process record items**: body part / color at first sight / blisters present or not / water temperature and rewarming time / consciousness and shivering state — recorded item by item in the Chapter 44 medical-record format.
- **⑥ Pass / rework / handover**: pass = reddening, sensation returning; rework = still pale (recheck water temperature and re-immerse, at most two rounds); handover = purplish-black, cloudy blister fluid, severe pain after rewarming that will not stop (third degree or above; keep warm and send for care).
- **⑦ Failure troubleshooting (verify in order for the same symptom)**: no reddening after 30 min of rewarming — ① water temperature insufficient (re-measure by thermometer or elbow test) → ② incomplete immersion (shoes, socks, gloves not fully removed) → ③ third-degree or worse frostbite (send for care immediately).
- **⑧ L1/L2/L3 evidence**: L1 one treatment record; L2 whole-team training assessment (simulated treatment, everyone passes; roster archived); L3 archive of actual winter cases and review records.

[Card 51-06 Frostbite Treatment] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: water at **38–42 °C** (at least **10 L** per immersion, enough to submerge the limb); dry clothing and blankets; warm water bottles wrapped in cloth; warm sugar water; clean dressings. First- and second-degree cases may use body-warmth rewarming; third degree and above require water.
2. **Tools required**: water thermometer (Chapter 3 instrument, **ice-point calibrated**); fallback — **if ice crystals float in the water it is over temperature, discard it**. No thermometer is not a reason to skip treatment.
3. **Sequence and control points**: **remove frozen boots and gloves (separate stuck skin with warm water pouring, never tear)** → **immerse in 38–42 °C water for 20–30 min** → wrap in dry dressings and elevate → give warm sugar water → record → decide on referral. Control points: **no rubbing, no fire, no snow** (these three cause most frostbite disability); err on the cool side of the temperature range.
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: water 38–42 °C (calibrated by a healthy elbow: 5 s feels "warm, not hot"); rewarming time **is judged by tissue, not by clock** — **skin turning pink and sensation returning** means done; the clock does not.
5. **Observations to record**: site / initial color / blisters present / water temperature and rewarming time / consciousness and shivering state — recorded item by item in the Chapter 44 case format.
6. **Pass / rework / referral criteria**: pass = pink and sensation returned; rework = still pale (recheck water temperature and immerse again, **at most twice**); referral = purple-black, cloudy blister fluid, no sensation after rewarming (to surgery — Chapter 44 debridement, Chapter 49 amputation red lines).
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) still pale after 30 min → (a) water too cool (measure or elbow-check) → (b) immersion incomplete (boots, socks, gloves not fully off) → (c) water too shallow to submerge; (2) large blisters turning black after rewarming → third degree reached, stop rewarming and refer; (3) patient shivering uncontrollably → whole-body hypothermia; rewarm the trunk before the extremities (Chapter 44 hypothermia-first rule).
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one treatment record; L2 = whole-team training with every member passing a simulated case, names on file; L3 = actual winter case files with review records.

### 51.20 Dried-Fish Line Card (Card 51-07, eight-field abstract)

- **① Input materials**: fresh run fish (into the line within ≤2 h of leaving the water; if over time, salt first, then dry), salt (if available: 5–10% of fish weight; salt-free if daytime temperature is in the <10 ℃ band), drying-rack poles and rope netting; hang 30–50 fish per rack, one fist apart, not touching back-to-back.
- **② Required tools**: fish-splitting knife (Chapter 7 blade, sharpened its full length), scale (Chapter 3; rechecked against standard stones on the first day of the run), drying shed (ventilated, no direct sun).
- **③ Operation sequence and control points**: gut (**a broken gall = the whole fish downgraded; excise the bile-contaminated section of the cavity**) → split along the back and spread flat, backbone unbroken → salt-cure 0.5–1 day (salt-using band) → hang on racks to shade-dry → turn every 2 days → take down when the dryness standard is met. **Control point: out by day and in by night, or shade-dry inside the shed the whole time, to prevent freeze–thaw cycles**.
- **④ Empirical parameters and calibration**: dryness = 30–35% of fresh weight (calibration: on the first run take 10 fish, weigh through the whole process, build the archive into the ledger); drying period 5–10 days (governed by the dryness criterion, not the calendar).
- **⑤ Process record items**: fresh weight / salt amount / dry weight at take-down / drying days / weather — entered in the ledger rack by rack; compute the shrink ratio (dry weight ÷ fresh weight).
- **⑥ Pass / rework / scrap**: pass = backbone snaps on fold, no wet fingerprints on the flesh face, no mold, no maggots; rework = wet-soft fingerprints (re-hang 2–3 days); scrap = mold patches joining up, maggot burrows, gray foul rot after freeze–thaw (discard or into field fertilizer; eating forbidden).
- **⑦ Failure troubleshooting (verify in order for the same symptom)**: fish racks going slimy in batches — ① hang spacing too dense, no ventilation → ② left out in the rain and re-dampened → ③ raw material out of water too long (salt the next batch earlier).
- **⑧ L1/L2/L3 evidence**: L1 one sample fish kept from the first qualified rack; L2 three consecutive racks with pass rate ≥8/10; L3 full-run shrink-ratio table and winter consumption review.

[Card 51-07 Dried-Fish Line] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: fish from the run (**into the line within 2 h of leaving water**; beyond that, salt first then dry); salt if available (**5–10% of fish weight**; may be omitted when daytime temperature is below 10 °C). Time out of water is this line's first criterion, not its last.
2. **Tools required**: filleting knife (Chapter 7 edge, fully sharpened), balance (Chapter 3, **rechecked against the standard stone on the first day of the run**), drying rack (ventilated, not in direct sun).
3. **Sequence and control points**: gut (**a cut gallbladder downgrades the whole fish; the bile-contaminated section must be removed**) → split along the back and flatten, **backbone intact** → salt **0.5–1 day** (if using salt) → rack and dry → weigh down. Control points: **gallbladder removal** and **backbone left intact** (a broken backbone will not flatten, and an unflattened fish will not dry through).
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: dryness = **30–35% of fresh weight** (calibrate by weighing 10 fish through the full process on the first run and filing the record); drying period **5–10 days** (governed by the dryness criterion, not the calendar); salt 5–10%; salt-free band when daytime is below 10 °C.
5. **Observations to record**: per rack — fresh weight / salt used / dried weight off the rack / drying days / weather — **every rack into the ledger, with the yield ratio (dry ÷ fresh) computed**.
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = **backbone snaps when folded, no damp fingerprint on the flesh, no mold, no maggots**; rework = damp and soft (rehang 2–3 days); scrap = continuous mold or maggots (discard the rack, never mix it in).
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) racks go tacky in bulk → (a) hanging too close for air movement → (b) rain-damp and not taken in → (c) fish held beyond 2 h out of water (salt the next batch); (2) dries hard outside, wet inside → rack in direct sun, move to ventilated shade; (3) mold → humidity high or drying period short; check whether the yield ratio was met; (4) abnormally low yield ratio → fish not fresh and already dehydrated (held too long).
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one acceptable dried fish kept as a sample from the first rack; L2 = three consecutive racks at ≥8/10 pass rate; L3 = a full-run yield-ratio table plus a winter consumption review.
