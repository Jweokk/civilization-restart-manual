## Chapter Objectives

Gives the complete reconstruction execution plan for desert conditions: water resource survey → five-stage route → special-risk sections. This chapter introduces no new technology; it does only **reordering and desert adaptation**; the core proposition is "process basket selection" under the doubly tight constraint of the water ledger and the fuel ledger.


## Capability Boundaries

**Can do**: the four water-source steps (survey–store–convey–conserve) and the qanat; low-water-use buildings and wind towers; solar-heat-assisted kilns and the salt–nitrate chemistry line; the anti-sand machinery system; adjudicating the industrial basket with the water unit-consumption table.
**Explicitly cannot**: large-scale supply of non-mineralized fresh water (distillation covers drinking only); high-water-use industries (papermaking / wet chemistry at restricted output); photovoltaics (a far-generation item); changing sandstorms and drought (only contingency plans possible).
**External dependencies**: fuel (the vegetation regrowth rate is the production ceiling — trading for fuel is the norm, not a failure); the annual groundwater ledger.

### 52.1 Stage One: The Water Resource Survey

- **Three water-source surveys**: ① oasis yield (measured by day-and-night pumping + dry-season recheck); ② groundwater (digging under dry riverbeds, seepage at the mountain-front gobi — plant indicators: shallow water beneath reed/tamarisk clusters); ③ rainy-season flash floods and lowland ponding (collection windows);
- Water storage: underground cisterns (Chapter 13 mortar + bitumen/clay sealing) — **cover slab + soil cover against evaporation** (an open pool in the desert is a sieve);
- **The qanat (underground-channel conveyance)**: mountain-foot groundwater is led by gravity through a gently descending underground channel to the oasis — evaporation loss far below that of an open channel; excavation in segments via vertical shafts (Chapter 16 surveying + Chapter 13 masonry); yield registered by the Chapter 25 volumetric method;
- Water-saving discipline: every process registers its unit water consumption (first place in the desert version of the Chapter 23 ledger); washing water reused in grades (clean–grey–foul, three grades);
- Solar distillation (a starter device for brackish-water desalination): Chapter 24 glass/transparent sheet + a black-bottomed shallow pool; seawater/brackish water evaporated and the condensate collected — low yield but zero fuel; serves as the drinking-water bottom line (**enough to sustain life, not enough to support industry** — an honest positioning).

[Card 52-01 Three Water-Source Checks and Qanat Yield] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: three candidate water-source points (① oasis ② dry riverbed / mountain-front gobi seepage ③ rainy-season flash-flood and lowland ponding windows); the qanat outlet (completed test section); a water-point ledger. **No large group moves in before the annual water survey is done** — this card is the gate on settlement.
2. **Tools required**: a measuring vessel (Chapter 25 volumetric method), pacing bow and rope-and-line (Chapter 3), test-digging tools (for probing shafts), a dedicated tasting spoon (salinity, one person's own). Calibration source: yield is accepted only from consecutive volumetric measurements, never from "it looks enough".
3. **Operation sequence and control points**: ① oasis yield — day-and-night pumping measured + dry-season recheck; ② groundwater — dig under dry riverbeds and at mountain-front gobi seepage, reading the plant indicators (shallow water beneath reed/tamarisk clusters); ③ rainy-season flash floods and lowland ponding — register the collection windows; ④ the qanat outlet measured by the volumetric method **three consecutive days**; ⑤ ten-day tasting of salinity / turbidity / flavor into the ledger. Control points: **both the day and the night readings are required** (daylight-only measurement overstates the yield); the **dry-season recheck** is the one that decides "can we settle"; missing one of the three days means measuring again.
4. **Experiential parameters and calibration method**: warning line = **down twenty percent for two consecutive ten-day periods → investigate silting/collapse**; inspection shafts **one per 60 paces**, **ten-day siltation checks** in the sandstorm season, one major cleanout a year; water quality tasted **every ten days** (salinity / turbidity / flavor, dedicated spoon and taster); the qanat is depreciated over fifty years. These are the chapter's master-table and Section 52.11 values; local yield is re-calibrated yearly by the volumetric method.
5. **In-process observation items**: per point record date / day-and-night pumped volume / dry-season recheck value / water-struck depth in the test shafts (the basis for the water-table trend) / salinity, turbidity, flavor / the ten-day value and the two-period decline.
6. **Accept / rework / scrap criteria**: accept = all three surveys complete, three-day flow stable, no consecutive rise in salinity; rework = down twenty percent over two ten-day periods (investigate silting: ten-day checks locate the shaft section for desilting); scrap = the well site is irreversibly salinized or a collapsed channel section cannot be repaired (that well or section is retired and the backup source opened).
7. **Common failures and troubleshooting order**: ① ten-day yield decline → check silting first → then collapsed sections → finally the water table (the annual water-level ledger assigns responsibility); ② water turning salty period by period → check for over-abstraction → cut withdrawal and open the backup; ③ test digging strikes no water → plant indicators misread or the water-table trend mis-set (back to 52.11 route selection); ④ water in the rainy season, dry in the dry season → only one survey was done, the dry-season recheck was skipped.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one complete triple survey plus the qanat outlet's three-day measurement; L2 = a full year's ledger including the dry season, with the warning line triggered at least once and a disposition recorded; L3 = a second person independently re-measuring the same source per this card, the two volumetric readings agreeing.

### 52.2 Stage Two: Low-Water Materials and Building

- Building: thick adobe walls (thermal inertia — absorbs heat by day, releases it at night, a time-lag regulator) + high small windows + inner courtyard; **wind tower** (wind catching + evaporative cooling: air crosses a wet curtain carrying moisture into the rooms — desert air conditioning); arches and domes (large spans in unreinforced adobe — the desert specialization of Chapter 13 masonry);
- Stone and gypsum: sandstone cuts easily (extractable with Chapter 5 stone tools alone); gypsum calcination (Chapter 23) for plastering and molds;
- Wind and sand control: lattice sand fences (fences of upright shrub branches that pile drifting sand into ridges); double-layer doorway porches (entering the house first passes through a dust-settling vestibule — the same principle as the permafrost cold-porch, intercepting a different intruder).

[Card 52-02 Anti-Sand Day Procedure] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: the day's sandstorm warning (yellow dust / black wind, two levels); machinery (bearings / guideways / gearboxes), optical surfaces, electrical insulators, water pools; grease (dust sealing), rags, boxes and covers.
2. **Tools required**: blow-off device (air blower / bellows), soft cloth, grease gun, box-type storage containers, pool covers. Calibration source: whether the blow-out is done shows in the oil colour and the running noise, not in "I swept it once".
3. **Operation sequence and control points**: grade the day in the morning (yellow dust = stop outdoor machinery + cover optical surfaces; black wind = everyone indoors, doors and windows sealed) → **daily blow-out** of machinery on dust days (machine by machine) → optical surfaces by the three steps "**blow – wipe – cover**" (wiped and boxed immediately after use + blow-off with the air blower) → clear dust off insulators at once, **special patrol immediately after rain** → remove dust from pools and cover them. Control points: **blow before wiping** (wiping straight away = grinding the mirror with sand grains; a scratch is irreversible); **graded actions follow the warning level** — no improvising up, no discounting down.
4. **Experiential parameters and calibration method**: daily blow-out (dust days) + weekly oil change; optical surfaces wiped and boxed immediately after use; insulators patrolled immediately after rain (dust meeting water = a leakage eruption point); routine blow-out is 0.5 man-hour per day on the duty post; sandstorms run the two-level yellow-dust / black-wind plan, and once warned, high-place and outdoor machinery work stops.
5. **In-process observation items**: daily record wind force and warning level / number of machines blown out / oil colour / optical-surface disposition / insulator patrol result / pool dust removal.
6. **Accept / rework / scrap criteria**: accept = every machine blown out and signed for that day, no scratched optical surface, no leakage on the post-rain patrol; rework = grey oil or raspy running noise (precursor of dust ingress) → change the oil and check the dust seal; scrap = a seized bearing, or a scratched optical surface (irreversible).
7. **Common failures and troubleshooting order**: ① raspy running noise, temperature rise early → dust ingress (seal failed; check the dust seal and the grease); ② scratched optical surface → the "blow – wipe – cover" steps were not all done; ③ leakage after rain → insulator dust not cleared, patrol not done; ④ pool water green and salty → not covered, dust not removed.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one sandstorm season of daily blow-out records; L2 = a whole season with zero seized shafts, zero scratched optics, and no gap in the post-rain patrols; L3 = a second person independently running one day's procedure per this card with every item signed.

### 52.3 Stage Three: Heat Energy and Basic Chemistry

- Kiln strategy under the tight fuel constraint: ① **solar preheating** (materials sun-dried through the day before charging, saving over one tenth of fuel; experiential value, must be field-calibrated); ② doubled kiln-body insulation (Chapter 12 thermal insulation) + miniaturization (match daily output, not scale vanity); ③ a shrub-firewood quota system (annual regrowth = the cutting cap; the same discipline as Chapter 50);
- **The salt chemistry line (desert specialty)**: salt pans and salt lakes → table salt + Glauber's salt + (where present) nitrate-bearing soil layers (identification per Chapter 23); **nitrates (Chile-saltpeter-type deposits)** are a natural treasury of gunpowder and nitrogen feedstock — **the desert may leap at a bound into a "chemistry-rich zone"** (coexisting with fuel poverty; record this contradiction truthfully);
- Direct-sun processes: evaporative crystallization (the zero-fuel version of Chapter 26 crystallization), sun-curing of salt, partial pre-calcination of gypsum;
- Glass: good quartz sand is often at hand (the desert = a glass raw-material bank) — but melting consumes fuel, clashing with the tight fuel constraint: **glass in the desert is a candidate for "export in exchange for fuel," not a local consumer good** (route economics, adjudicated by Chapter 29 accounting).

[Card 52-03 Fuel Quota] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: measured shrub regrowth (quadrats), the cutting ledger, quota tags (to household and to kiln), measured cutting-radius records.
2. **Tools required**: quadrat rope and pacing bow (Chapter 3), scales, the reconciliation ledger (Chapter 38). Calibration source: regrowth is accepted only from quadrat measurement, never from last year's consumption.
3. **Operation sequence and control points**: measure regrowth by quadrat → set the annual cutting cap = regrowth × 0.8 → issue quotas to households and kilns (tagged) → monthly reconciliation → measure the cutting radius → a 10 percent expansion = yellow warning → cut quotas or trade for fuel. Control points: **the cap is computed from measured regrowth, not from last year's usage**; **the cutting radius is the most sensitive gauge** (expansion = the vegetation is being eaten through).
4. **Experiential parameters and calibration method**: cutting cap = regrowth × 0.8; quotas to households and kilns; radius expanding 10 percent = yellow warning (Chapter 42 linkage); monthly reconciliation + radius measurement; the vegetation regrowth rate is the production ceiling — **trading for fuel is the norm, not a failure**.
5. **In-process observation items**: per season record quadrat regrowth / the annual cap / each household's and kiln's quota and actual use / cutting radius / warning dates and dispositions.
6. **Accept / rework / scrap criteria**: accept = annual use within the cap and the radius not expanded; rework = radius expanded 10 percent (cut quotas + trade for fuel, and re-measure regrowth); scrap = quota breach stopping the kiln line (review and reset the cap — eating the vegetation bare in three years is the desert version of a historical failure).
7. **Common failures and troubleshooting order**: ① kiln fuel exhausted → quota breach (activate the red line, trade for fuel); ② cutting radius expanding year by year → the cap was set too high (regrowth mismeasured, or the cap set from usage); ③ damp wood, weak fire → a dry-storage problem, not a quota problem; ④ quota disputes → quotas never reached household and kiln (no tags, no ledger).
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one year of quota and reconciliation ledgers; L2 = two consecutive years with no radius expansion and no fuel-driven stoppage; L3 = a second person re-measuring the same quadrats and independently computing the cap, with consistent results.

### 52.4 Stage Four: Metals and Machinery

- Ore-source determinism (Chapter 16 general survey first): desert-type deposits (residual/slope-wash, placer, open oxidized zones) are **easy to mine, hard to smelt** (fuel and water lacking) — smelting schedules are capped by the "fuel transport radius";
- A closed water loop for metallurgy: reuse pools for quench and ore-wash water (brine concentrated by evaporation handled separately — **every liter of water must have its destination registered**);
- Machinery: dust is the killer of bearings and guideways (the desert version of the Chapter 33 three protections) — **sealed bearings + grease dust seals + daily blow-out**; fully enclosed gearbox design takes priority over open belts (better low efficiency than ingress of sand).

### 52.5 Stage Five: Power Generation and Electronics

- The early phase unchanged: waterwheels (qanat flow is small, often enough only for small machines) or animal power / a little steam (constrained by the fuel ledger);
- **Wind as the mainstay**: the desert's day–night thermal convection winds plus perennial wind belts — windmill + elevated reservoir (the Chapter 29 wind–pool pairing) is the desert's standard powerplant;
- Solar power generation (registered as a long-range item): the heat-engine route (concentrator-made steam driving a Chapter 28 engine type — **the concentrator mirror = the Chapter 24 mirror process scaled up**, registered as a key-attack item); photovoltaics belong to the far generation of the Chapter 34 purity ladder and are not promised;
- Electronics: sand-proof sealed packaging (the Chapter 36 harsh-environment grade) + thermal-cycling qualification (large day–night temperature swings = heat-fatigue accelerated aging; add this page to the failure atlas).

### 52.6 Special Sections of the Desert Version

1. **How water limits industrial scale**: the "water unit-consumption table" for every class of process decides the industrial basket — high water use (papermaking, wet chemistry, steam) is restricted; low water use (dry chemistry, machinery, optics) has priority;
2. **The applicable boundary of solar heat**: preheating and sun-drying processes are zero-risk; concentrated high temperature demands tracking and safety (the Chapter 24 focused-ignition risk); **"solar abundance" does not equal "high-temperature processes for free"** — collection, storage, and temperature control are independent engineering problems (the executed version of the Chapter 29 warning);
3. **The effects of dust on machinery / optics / electrics**: optical surfaces (lenses and mirrors) are never stored exposed (box storage + unboxed only for use); dust on electrical porcelain skirts turns conductive (special patrol after rain — in the desert "after rain" is the high season for leakage);
4. **Water storage and chemical operations under high evaporation**: open chemical pools are forbidden (concentration runs away); reagents are made up "at the moment of use";
5. **A system minimizing dependence on external transport**: a spares localization-rate metric (the desert weighting of the Chapter 36 inventory) — long supply lines themselves consume water and fuel; **"make it rather than buy it" is, in the desert, a survival algorithm and not merely an economic one**.

### 52.11 The Qanat Process Card (Card 52-04)

The qanat is the desert's lifeline works — an underground channel carries mountain-foot groundwater by gravity, with evaporation loss a small fraction of an open channel's:

- **Route selection**: where the mountain-foot water table sits high (probe for water with shafts: test-dig at 50-pace intervals until water is struck, recording the **water-struck depth** — joining the points traces the water table's trend; lay the conveyance channel along the water-table contour with a gentle descent);
- **Longitudinal gradient**: the channel floor drops **1–2 finger-widths per 10 paces** (same method as the Chapter 13 open channel — too steep scours the floor and caves it in; too shallow silts up and cuts the flow); each shaft serves both as an excavation opening and later as an inspection and ventilation opening;
- **Shaft spacing**: one shaft every 20–30 paces during excavation (ease of spoil removal); after completion **half the shafts are sealed** (inspection shafts kept at one per 60 paces + an outlet shaft at the end — sealed-shaft covers guard against sand and against people falling in);
- **Cross-section**: channel height and width each 2.5–3 chi (the minimum section in which one person stooping can desilt); lining supports only in loose sections (dry-laid cobble or adobe arch, Chapter 13);
- **Excavation organization**: driving from both ends + segmented multi-face excavation via shafts (Chapter 37 shift scheduling: 2 persons per shaft face × rotating shifts); **direction control** — straightness inside the channel is held by the three-lamp alignment method (a lamp at each end + a lamp in the middle; three lamps in one line = straight);
- **Yield acceptance**: outlet daily flow measured by the volumetric method for three consecutive days (Chapter 25 baseline); **warning line** = down twenty percent for two consecutive ten-day periods → check for silting/collapse (Card 52-01);
- **Maintenance ledger**: one major cleanout per year (silt removal + repair of collapsed sections), monthly inspection of shaft-head covers — the qanat is an "ancestral-estate project"; the books depreciate it over fifty years.

[Card 52-04 Qanat Construction] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: the mountain-foot water-table section (route fixed by probe shafts), masonry materials (cobble / adobe / mortar, Chapter 13), shaft-head covers, three lamps (three-lamp alignment).
2. **Tools required**: test-digging tools, a water meter (Chapter 25 volumetric method), pacing bow and rope-and-line (Chapter 3), masonry tools. Calibration source: gradient and cross-section are measured with rules, not estimated by eye.
3. **Operation sequence and control points**: probe shafts fix the route (**test-dig at 50-pace intervals until water is struck, record the water-struck depth**; joining the points gives the water-table trend; lay the line along the contour with a gentle descent) → excavate in segments via shafts (**one shaft every 20–30 paces**) → channel cross-section **2.5–3 chi in both height and width** → line loose sections with dry-laid cobble or an adobe arch → **hold straightness with the three-lamp method** (a lamp at each end + one in the middle, three in one line) → acceptance by **volumetric measurement over three consecutive days** at the outlet → on completion **seal half the shafts** (inspection shafts one per 60 paces + the end outlet shaft; covers against sand and against people falling in). Control point: **the floor drops 1–2 finger-widths per 10 paces** (too steep scours and caves in, too shallow silts up and cuts the flow).
4. **Experiential parameters and calibration method**: gradient 1–2 finger-widths per 10 paces; shafts every 20–30 paces (excavation) / 60 paces (inspection); cross-section 2.5–3 chi; probe interval 50 paces; acceptance over three consecutive days; warning line = down twenty percent for two consecutive ten-day periods; one major cleanout a year + monthly shaft-cover inspection; depreciated over fifty years; one segment (10 shafts) takes 4–6 persons 30–60 days (experiential).
5. **In-process observation items**: per shaft record water-struck depth / shaft spacing / cross-section dimensions / lined sections / three-lamp alignment result / three-day outlet flow / ten-day siltation check.
6. **Accept / rework / scrap criteria**: accept = outlet flow stable for three consecutive days, gradient and cross-section on spec, three-lamp alignment with no deviation; rework = insufficient flow or fast silting (re-measure the gradient, desilt and re-align); scrap = a collapsed section beyond repair or a salinized well site (seal that section, open the backup well).
7. **Common failures and troubleshooting order**: ① ten-day yield decline → silting → collapsed section → falling water table; ② the two headings fail to meet → the three-lamp check was skipped or the gradient ran away; ③ floor scoured and caved in → gradient too steep; ④ channel silted and flow cut → gradient too shallow; ⑤ someone fell down a shaft → the shaft cover was missing.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one 10-shaft segment completed + the three-day acceptance record; L2 = the whole line supplying water stably for a year with no gap in ten-day checks; L3 = a second person independently driving one segment per this card and meeting the heading by three-lamp alignment.

### 52.12 The Solar Still Device Card (Card 52-05)

Positioning: **the drinking-water bottom line** — enough to sustain life, not enough to support industry (daily yield 1.5–3 L per square meter, experiential value, must be field-calibrated):

- **Construction**: shallow pool (stone-built + black bottom — pot-bottom soot mixed into a paste and painted on; the key to heat absorption) + transparent cover (glass plate / mica sheet, built as a 25–30° pitched roof) + collection trough (along the lower edge of the slope); pool water depth 2–3 finger-widths (too shallow and it dries out fast; too deep and it warms slowly — 2 fingers is the compromise);
- **Operation**: charge water in the morning (brackish or turbid water both fine — what distills out is clean water), collect water in the afternoon; before charging, clear the salt crust off the pool bottom (a crust insulating the black = efficiency drops sharply);
- **Efficiency self-check**: at noon, beads of water streaming down the cover's inner wall in strings = normal; condensing but not streaming = the cover is too hot (shade and ventilate one corner with a straw mat to cool it, or switch to a double cover);
- **Winter / sandstorms**: cover the pool for heat retention (at night, prevent condensate dripping back and contaminating); after a sandstorm, clear the sand before running again;
- **Upgrade path**: multiple pools in series (stage one's waste heat feeds stage two) — yield can rise fifty percent (experiential value); scheduled as a second-batch project.

[Card 52-05 Solar Distillation] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: brackish or turbid water (what distills out is clean water), a stone-built shallow pool with a black bottom (pot-bottom soot mixed into a paste and painted on), a transparent cover (glass plate / mica sheet, 25–30° pitched roof), a collection trough.
2. **Tools required**: collection vessels, a measuring vessel (Chapter 25), a straw mat (shade cooling), a pool cover (for night). Calibration source: yield is calibrated by on-site measurement; the experiential value in this chapter is only a starting point.
3. **Operation sequence and control points**: charge water in the morning (pool depth **2–3 finger-widths**, 2 fingers the compromise) → collect in the afternoon → **clear the salt crust off the pool bottom before charging** (a crust insulating the black = efficiency drops sharply) → noon efficiency self-check → after a sandstorm clear the sand before running. Control points: **beads of water streaming down the cover's inner wall in strings** = normal; **condensing but not streaming** = the cover is too hot (shade and ventilate a corner, or switch to a double cover); **never leave the still unattended** (dry-boil bursting).
4. **Experiential parameters and calibration method**: daily yield **1.5–3 L per square meter** (experiential value, must be field-calibrated); pool depth 2–3 finger-widths; cover pitched 25–30°; multiple pools in series can raise yield by fifty percent (experiential value, a second-batch project). Positioning stated honestly: **enough to sustain life, not enough to support industry**.
5. **In-process observation items**: daily record water charged / water collected (converted to yield per square meter) / salt crust cleared / noon cover behaviour (strings or mere condensation) / pool-water salinity.
6. **Accept / rework / scrap criteria**: accept = water streaming in strings at noon and daily drinking output meeting the ledger; rework = condensing but not streaming (check cover temperature and shading, clear the salt crust); scrap = the black layer has come off or the cover is broken (recoat / replace the cover).
7. **Common failures and troubleshooting order**: ① yield collapsing → check the salt crust first → then the black bottom → then sand on the cover and its sealing; ② condensing but not streaming → cover too hot (shade it, double cover); ③ water at night but none by day → night condensate dripping back, the pool was not covered; ④ cracked pool → unattended dry boiling (the safety-boundary red line).
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = daily yield records for one bank of pools; L2 = a full season of yield accounting that covers the daily drinking bottom line; L3 = a second person independently building one bank of pools per this card and reproducing the same yield.

### 52.13 Salt-Evaporation Ponds and Glauber's Salt Separation (the Civil-Works Face of the Salt Chemistry Line)

- **Evaporation ponds**: level gentle-slope ground into cascading pond groups (3–4 stages, a one-finger-width drop per stage) — brine moves stage by stage, thickening stage by stage: stage one clarification pond (sand settling), stage two evaporation pond (main sunning), stage three crystallization pond (salt precipitates out). Pond bottoms compacted + mortar render (Chapter 13 hydraulic formulation) against seepage;
- **Harvest rhythm**: harvest only when the crystallization pond's salt layer is 2–3 finger-widths thick (early harvests carry mud); harvest by "scrape first, then wash" — the surface salt is scraped off and rinsed once with concentrated brine (removes mud and bitterness);
- **Glauber's salt separation (the winter nitrate harvest)**: brine cold-precipitates in winter — **Glauber's salt (sodium sulfate) solubility falls steeply with temperature; in winter it grows out of the brine by itself**, while table salt stays in solution (the reverse side of the same coin as the hot-filtration principle of Section 23.9.2: hot filtration strips salt from saltpeter; winter cold precipitates Glauber's salt by itself); haul Glauber's salt in winter, sun table salt in spring and summer — **one pond, two seasons, two products**;
- **Product destinations**: table salt enters the food and chemical ledgers (Chapter 38); Glauber's salt for papermaking / glass flux / soda feedstock (the local feedstock for the Section 23.9.5 Leblanc line).

### 52.14 Wind Tower Construction Parameters

- **Tower body**: a square vertical shaft (3×3 chi clear inside), standing 2–3 zhang above the ground — the higher, the steadier the wind (no vortex at the top);
- **Orientation**: the shaft mouth opens toward the prevailing wind (or four-way openings + movable guide panels adjusted with the wind direction);
- **Cooling fittings**: water pan at the tower base + wet curtain (layers of hemp cloth hanging into the pan, kept moist by capillary uptake — incoming air crossing the wet curtain cools by evaporation); the wind, cooled in passing down the tower, enters the corridor outside the living quarters;
- **Criterion**: at noon the tower-base outlet air is 5–8 ℃ below ambient (hand-feel grade comparison; experiential value, must be field-calibrated); a dry, white wet curtain = the pan has lost its water (daily refill is a duty item);
- **Limits, stated honestly**: on windless days the wind tower is out of action — thick-wall thermal inertia (52.2) is the desert's first line of cooling; the wind tower is the second.

### 52.16 Drought-Resistant Agronomy: Date Palm as the Frame, Water Runs in Pipes

- **Seed selection: the Chapter 10 three standards with desert weighting** — the general three standards (drought tolerance / storability / yield) are re-ranked in the desert as **drought tolerance > storability > yield**: the yield champion dies first in drought years (count its loss at one hundred percent); the storability champion is famine-year reserve grain; only the drought-tolerance champion is year-in year-out rations. Execution: candidate crops (barley, millet, sorghum, forage — three lines each) split across two comparison plots — Plot A under conventional irrigation, Plot B on a sixty-percent water ration; for two consecutive seasons register the five columns "emergence date — maturity date — yield per mu — thousand-grain weight — water actually applied"; **selection criterion = Plot B's per-mu yield down no more than thirty percent, and thousand-grain weight down no more than ten percent** (two indicators guard against "yield puffery" — a line propped through one season by one-time deep roots will surely be exposed in the second). Selected lines are harvested and stored separately, with duplicate seed samples kept (one in use, one in reserve; wax-sealed in pottery jars).
- **Date palm as the skeleton**: alkali-tolerant, heat-tolerant, fruit high in sugar and storable, trunk usable, fronds and palm fiber weavable — five uses from one tree, the load-bearing wall of desert agriculture. Planting row spacing is set by the shadow: the noon shade radius of an adult crown is experientially 1.5–2 zhang. **Calibration method**: pick three local adult trees, measure the noon shadow length three days running around the summer solstice, and average (values differ with latitude; copying from books is forbidden).
- **Shade intercropping**: tree rows run east–west — inside the shade band plant forage (the alfalfa family) and shallow-rooted vegetables; in the full-sun band outside, plant millet and sorghum. The evaporation advantage under shade must be measured and registered: two identical shallow basins each charged with equal water, one set in shade and one in full sun for half a day; the water difference is the half-day evaporation difference (experiential value: shade loss is roughly thirty to fifty percent of full-sun loss; local calibration required); if measured shade loss still exceeds sixty percent, the row orientation or spacing is wrong — adjust by transplanting next year.
- **Clay-pipe seepage irrigation (a native method of drip/seepage irrigation)**: unglazed clay pipes (the Chapter 13 pottery line; inner diameter 2–3 finger-widths, segment length 1.5–2 chi) are buried beside the root zone; the pipe head connects to the field ditch, the pipe tail is plugged, and water seeps through wall pores into the root zone — no wet surface above ground, and evaporation is only a small fraction of basin irrigation (comparison method: one basin-irrigated bed and one seepage-irrigated bed given the same measured quantity of water; on the third day dig a profile and compare wetting-front depth; enter it in the ledger);
  - **Burial depth**: cereals 0.5–1 chi (the upper edge of the main root layer); young date palms 1–1.5 chi (experiential; recheck against local soil with a test-seepage profile — if the wetting front has not reached the root layer, deepen by half a chi and retry);
  - **Seepage-radius calibration**: bury a single pipe section as a test, pour in one measured bucket of water, and after 24 hours dig a profile perpendicular to the pipe; the farthest horizontal reach of the wetting front is the radius r — wet sand is dark, dry sand light, and the boundary is discernible; ◎ practice method: before going to the field, train the eye ten times on paired trays of dry and wet sand; compensation for misjudgment is probing with bamboo skewers (a skewer pulled out carrying damp sand = past the wetting front); **pipe spacing = 2r−1 finger-widths** (leaving a one-finger dry-soil band, preventing cross-seepage between lines and keeping one cracked pipe from waterlogging the whole band);
  - **Head–seepage table**: for each one-finger-width raise of the pipe-head water head, record one hour's seepage output by the volumetric method (Chapter 25); post the small table in the well house — irrigation is timed by the table, never by feel.
- **Salt-alkali pioneer-crop observation registration**: newly cleared salt-patch land is forbidden to go straight into food crops — drill three observation strips in salt-tolerance order: saltbush/salt-sage strip → barley strip → (once seedlings take) date-palm strip; every ten days register "emergence rate — seedling death rate — plant height," for three consecutive periods. **Promotion criterion: barley-strip emergence above fifty percent and plant height above one chi**; failing that, the plot is kept salt-suppressing for another year, and leaching water may only be return water (the 52.17 account), never fresh water. Observation strips are "using crops as the geological survey party" — cheaper than digging soil for assay and more honest than guessing.

### 52.17 The Irrigation Network: A Three-Tier Ledger from Well Head to Root Zone

- **The main distribution pool and proportional weirs**: qanat output first enters the main distribution pool (doubling as a sand settler — two compartments rotated for desilting), then water is distributed in two tiers: the main pool's weir boards carry large apertures cut per plot, the field-ditch heads small apertures per work team. Principle of the proportional weir: at equal head, discharge is approximately proportional to weir-mouth width (the thin-plate weir empirical formula), so the "division ratio" is realized as a "weir-width ratio" — **but for it to hold, every weir must be calibrated once by the volumetric method**, recording a "weir width — head — output per shichen" table; an uncalibrated weir is mere decoration. Disciplinary red line: **privately altering a weir mouth = stealing water** — a ten-day water fine plus registration; any weir change must pass the three steps "report — recalibrate — replace board."
- **The rotation irrigation timetable**: open irrigation in the desert happens only in low-evaporation windows — **open at sunset, close at sunrise**; the timetable is arranged by night: at each ten-day period's start, deduct from the total water in the order "human and livestock drinking → salt-alkali observation strips → food-grain fields → forage fields"; the remainder is converted, by "area × crop grade," into time slots filling all seven nights. The execution trio: the duty officer calling the change (open/close irrigation times reported into the book), the bamboo-scale head reading before each weir, and estimating return water at the drain. **Linked with the Chapter 37 shift system**: irrigation-night duty and workshop night shifts hang on the same scheduling wall — one person, one post, one signature — preventing "two night shifts each assuming the other is watching the water."
- **Field anti-evaporation**: mulch as soon as irrigation closes. Stone mulch — gravel spread between plants and along both sides of the field ditches, 1–2 finger-widths thick (dew condenses among the stones at night, vapor is blocked by day, and day–night soil temperature swings under stone are smaller too); straw mulch — date-palm fiber or dry grass spread around the tree disks, 2–3 finger-widths thick (grass sources are tight; young trees get priority). **Acceptance criterion: the morning of the second day after irrigation the surface soil is still damp**; daytime drying with cracking = mulch insufficient — thicken by one finger-width and retry (local values are found by gradient trials; do not copy books).
- **Collection and reuse of irrigation return water**: the drain ditches at each plot's lowest point feed a return-water pool (lidded against evaporation — the same discipline as the 52.1 water storage); return water is booked in three grades: ① residue not over 2 g/L (evaporate one liter of return water to dryness and weigh; an experiential line; recheck: scorched leaf edges three days after irrigation = drop a grade) → re-irrigate the salt-alkali observation strips; second-pass water is just right for the salt-tolerant crops; ② over the line but still usable → into the dedicated salt-leaching account; ③ ◎ salinity judged by taste: a dedicated spoon and a dedicated taster practice taste judgment ten times each ten-day period against reference samples weighed after evaporation to dryness; compensation for misjudgment = the weighing always prevails — the over-concentrated goes into the salt-evaporation ponds as brine (linking to 52.13). Every drop of water passing two pairs of hands is the baseline courtesy of the desert water ledger.

1. **Input materials**: the well head's ten-day average flow (Chapter 25 volumetric method), each plot's area and crop water-demand grade, wooden tags or ceramic tallies for per-household identification.

### 52.18 Trade Routes and Camel Caravans: Walking the Desert into a Road Network

- **The camel load standard**: a single-camel load is experientially 120–150 kg (±10 kg). **Calibration method**: a new camel starts its first season at 100 kg and adds 10 kg per ten-day period, watching two signs — unable to rise after lying down, and footpads worn through — if either appears, drop back one grade (individual variation exceeds breed variation, so files are kept per camel and grades are not set per breed). Three loading principles: heavy low, light high; water forward, cargo aft (water skins go ahead of the hump so they do not chafe against the cargo); every load carries a quick-release tie (in danger, one knife cut drops the load and saves the life). ◎ load-balancing practice method: time three rounds of loading and stripping an empty pack, and check saddle skew with a plumb line; compensation for misjudgment = weigh the two sides of the pack separately on a steelyard; a difference over 5 kg means reload. Schedule: the pack pace covers 4–5 shichen (two-hour periods) of travel per day, about 25–35 li (registered and revised line by line according to terrain).
- **The water-and-feed carrying ledger**: humans drink 4–6 L per day (experiential; carry 8 L per person in the hot season); camels drink 20–30 L per day and eat 3–5 kg of feed per day (dry grass / date residue); **carrying quantity = schedule × daily consumption × safety factor 1.5**. The factor is not a fixed number: on each return leg settle "actual water remaining ÷ planned water carried"; if the five-trip average runs above 1.3 over the long term, lower the factor back to 1.3 — spare water becomes dead weight that breaks camels down, and a padded ledger is more dangerous than no ledger. On segments with mowable pasture along the way, feed counts at half quantity (pasture grade registered into the water register).
- **Watering-point spacing and the "water register"**: before opening a new route, dispatch a pathfinding party and register all watering points along the line into an **inter-point schedule** (trade jargon calls it the "water register"): for each pair of points, five lines — mileage, days of travel, whether shade exists en route, pasture grade, spare-water factor. **New-route rule: no more than two days between waters** (a green route with two-day water gaps may not carry loaded camels). The water register is revised once at each season's end — in the dry season sources shrink, in the rainy season flash floods move them; a past-its-season register harms more than no register at all.
- **Caravan signals and the night-camp system**: by day, smoke talk (three short rising puffs = halt; one long straight column = move; twin columns = water found); by night, lamp talk (a lantern swung left and right = danger; held high and circled = assemble); the horn's three calls (rising call = load up; level call = move slow; falling call = strike camp). All three signal sets are rehearsed by the whole party before departure; **those who have not mastered the signals may only follow the local camel drivers and may not lead off alone with the whistle**. Night camp: camels formed in a ring (cargo stacks centered, camels' heads facing outward); paired sentries posted at diagonal corners (with a single sentry, one man's sleep stakes every life in the party on his eyelids); camp on the waist of a dune's lee slope (wind is savage at the crest; sand piles at the foot and buries cargo); the campfire sits downwind and no nearer than three paces to the cargo stacks.
- **Value-density ranking of trade goods** (the desert borrowing of Chapter 53 trade thought): the desert caravan's transport account = fuel/grain equivalent recovered per kg of load. Ranked by it: saltpeter, glassware, refined salt (low weight, high value) list as prime cargo; coarse salt and gypsum (high weight, low value) only ride along in passing; ironware and grain are return-leg priority cargo. **Red line: an empty-camel return leg = a double loss** — before every caravan departs, recompute the ranking table against the prices at both ends, enter it in the trade ledger, and re-audit once per quarter.
