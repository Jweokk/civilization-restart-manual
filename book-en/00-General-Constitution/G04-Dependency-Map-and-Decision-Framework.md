## II. Five Critical Chains (Priority Verification Targets for Writing and Auditing)

1. Stone tools → woodworking tools → simple machinery (Chapters 5, 7, 9)
2. Fire → furnaces and kilns → refractory materials → metal smelting (Chapters 6, 12, 16–18)
3. Metal tools → measurement → precision machining → machine tools (Chapters 19–22)
4. Mechanical power → electromagnetism → generators → electric power systems (Chapters 21, 28–32)
5. Glass and chemistry → material purification → semiconductor processing → integrated circuits (Chapters 14, 23–26, 33–35)

**Chains 3 and 5 are the most prone to unexplained tool anachronisms; check them node by node during review.**

## III. Priority Decision Framework

Technically feasible ≠ technologically worth prioritizing. A comparison framework (for discussion, not a computable scientific law):

> Priority = survival contribution × resource availability × repeatable-manufacturing capability × support value for downstream technologies

Each technology must further answer five questions:

1. If it is not built, which downstream technologies will be blocked?
2. Is there a cheaper alternative?
3. How much non-renewable resource does it consume?
4. Does it raise the efficiency of other production steps?
5. When is it worth committing more labor?

**The reader must know not only how to build a tool, but also when not to build it.**

## IV. Environment Assessment Table (Unified Model for the Four Versions of Part Eleven)

| Resource or capability | Question to answer | Technology routes affected |
| ------- | ------------- | -------------- |
| Water | Stable, storable, treatable? | Agriculture, chemistry, metallurgy, cooling |
| Fuel | Sustainably obtainable? | Pottery, lime, glass, metallurgy, heat engines |
| Clay and refractory materials | Can reliable furnaces and kilns be built? | High-temperature processes and metal smelting |
| Metal ores | Are there usable ore sources? | Metal tools, machinery, electrical equipment |
| Quartz and silicon-bearing raw materials | Raw material supply and subsequent purification conditions? | Glass, optics, and semiconductor routes |
| Timber and fiber | Sufficient and sustainable? | Tools, construction, transport, some machinery |
| Available power | Water flow, wind, sunshine, fuel? | Mechanical power and power generation |
| Transport conditions | Can ore, fuel, and equipment be moved? | Industrial layout and specialization |
| Maintenance capability | Can tools and equipment be repaired continuously? | Long-term sustainability of the technology |
| Manpower and time | How many people can take part over the long term? | Route selection, capacity, division of labor |

Core principle: **environmental differences change not only where materials come from, but the entire developmental order of the civilization.** The four environment versions share technological knowledge, but they do not share the same technological sequence.

## V. Technology Boundaries: Three Goals That Must Not Be Conflated

Taking the integrated circuit as the example (Chapter 35 enforces this table strictly):

| Goal | Meaning | This book's stance |
| ------ | ------------------------------ | ------------------------ |
| Understanding | Able to explain the working principles and the main manufacturing steps | Delivered continuously throughout the book |
| Manufacturing simple devices | Building and verifying devices of limited complexity on top of established materials, equipment, and measurement | The ultimate challenge of Part Six |
| Rebuilding modern industry | Sustained production of high-performance, complex, reliable, competitive products | **Honestly labeled as beyond the capability of a single volume**, with a list of the required conditions |

Writing the boundary honestly is worth more than inventing a route that looks complete but cannot actually be executed. Chapter 36, "Which modern technologies cannot yet be rebuilt", is part of the book's credibility, not a defect.
