> Part 2 · Survival, Fire, and Primitive Materials. This chapter establishes the first batch of "chemical capabilities": clay evaluation, ash alkali, pigments, binders, and — more important — the methodology of small-scale materials testing and recipe record-keeping. No pottery, no glassware: all liquid operations use gourds, shells, wooden troughs, and hide bags.

- Establish the materials-testing system of "standard specimens + single-variable trials + record sheets" — this is the common starting point of the chemistry lineage (Lineage 5) and the information lineage (Lineage 6).
- Deliverables: a clay test archive, the first tar glue and bond-strength data, and a recipe record system.

### 8.1 How to Find and Identify Clay

**Prospecting locations** (in priority order): inner point bars of river bends (slowing water deposits fine grains); floodplains; lake and marsh margins; slope wash below springs; fresh-cut faces of landslides; subsoil turned up by termite mounds and animal burrows (fine-grained, sticky when wet).

**Outcrop appearance criteria**: sticks to shoes and hands when wet and takes a fingerprint when squeezed; on drying the surface shrinks into polygonal cracked blocks; the fracture is fine and grit-free; gray, yellow-brown, red-brown, or blue-gray color are all acceptable (color is used only for labeling, never for selection).

**Three field tests (run in order; record every one)**:

| Test | Method | Pass criterion | Rejection criterion |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lick test | a soybean-sized bit of soil — **touch to the tongue tip, then immediately spit it out and rinse** (no chewing, no swallowing — guards against pathogens and grit wearing the teeth) | fine and slick, no grit | clearly gritty — sand content too high; pan it or change sites |
| Coil test | knead with water into a 6 mm diameter coil, roll it out to 15–20 cm | does not break, no transverse cracks; if it can further be rolled to 3 mm and bent into a ring without breaking, plasticity is high | snaps as soon as it is rolled — plasticity insufficient |
| Shrinkage test | knead a 4 cm clay ball, shade-dry 24 hours | visible shrinkage but only hairlines on the surface | deep cracks — shrinkage rate too high; temper must be added |

**Settling method for sand content (glassware-free version)**: fill a gourd, large shell, or fire-carved wooden trough with water, drop in an equal volume of soil sample, stir, and let stand 1–2 hours. Read the layers: bottom coarse sand, middle silt, top suspended fine clay. A clay layer more than 50% of the total thickness is excellent (rule of thumb, must be field-calibrated). Carefully pour off the top slurry — what you pour off is the initially panned fine clay.

**Registration**: for every source site, record location, description, three-test results, estimated workable quantity, and test-block numbers in the Chapter 2 resource-map format. Bring back a representative sample of 1–2 kg for the Section 8.2 and 8.6 tests.

### 8.2 Clay Plasticity, Shrinkage, and Cracking

**Moisture-by-feel ladder**: dry powder → damp (will not ball) → **plastic zone** (balls without sticking to the hands or cracking; feels like an earlobe) → soft mud → slurry. The plastic zone holds about 18%–28% water (rule of thumb, must be field-calibrated). All forming operations happen only in the plastic zone.

**Wedging**: before use, knead and press repeatedly (spiral kneading) for 10–15 minutes; purpose: homogenization + expelling air bubbles. Air bubbles will blister and crack during later drying and firing.

**Shrinkage-rate measurement**: make a standard clay bar (12×2×2 cm), scribe a 10 cm standard line on its surface (Chapter 3 scribed rule), and measure the line after shade-drying. Dry shrinkage = (10 − measured length)/10. Common range 5%–10% (rule of thumb, must be field-calibrated). The shrinkage rate of a given clay batch is the basis for later pottery layout scaling.

**Temper (inclusions against shrinkage cracking)**:

| Temper | Grain size | First-use ratio (by volume) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Washed quartz sand | 0.5–1 mm | 10%–20% | general purpose |
| Crushed shell powder | coarse-sand grade | 10%–20% | good thermal-shock resistance |
| Crushed old pottery | — | — | unavailable in the pre-pottery period; activated after Chapter 11 |

Temper gradient test: make one set of test bars at each of 0%, 10%, 20%, 30% (per the Section 8.6 system), and select by "no cracks after drying + highest strength."

**Four-cause crack triage**: drying too fast (sun exposure, drafty spots) → slow the drying; inhomogeneity (bubbles, dry or wet lumps) → re-wedge from scratch; temper excessive or too coarse → lower the ratio; abrupt thickness change → thickness ratio ≤2:1 with a transition.

**Drying procedure**: shade-dry in a cool place under grass or large leaves; full-dry criterion: the bar lightens in color, feels cool-free against the lip, and its weight no longer drops. Record all data in the test sheet.

[Card 08-02 Clay: Three Field Tests and Settling Grading] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: 1–2 kg of soil from candidate deposits (a representative sample, surface soil scraped away); water; one control lump of known-good clay (for hand-feel reference only). **Take samples from at least three spots per deposit** — river-bend sediment is layered even within one site.
2. **Tools required**: a stone flake (to cut a fresh test face), a wooden trough or gourd or large shell for settling (must show layers visibly), scored rule (Chapter 3), record sheet. Calibration source: rule scored against the Chapter 3 standard rod; settling fractions read by eye, no instrument.
3. **Sequence and control points**: lick test → ribbon test → shrinkage test → settling test for sand content, in that order, recording each. Control points: **after the lick test spit it out and rinse — do not chew, do not swallow** (pathogens and grit); **the shrinkage test must run a full 24 hours** — reading early mistakes "not yet dry" for "no cracking".
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: lick test passes = smooth and soapy, no grittiness; ribbon test = roll a 6 mm coil out to 15–20 cm without breaking or transverse cracks, and a 3 mm coil that still bends into a ring indicates high plasticity; shrinkage test = 4 cm ball air-dried 24 hours, fine surface checking only — deep cracks mean excessive shrinkage and call for temper; settling = equal volumes of soil and water, stir, stand 1–2 hours and read the layers (coarse sand at the bottom, silt in the middle, fine clay on top).
5. **Observations to record**: per sample — deposit ID and spot / all three test results / settling fractions / estimated workable quantity / sampling date.
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = all three tests pass (smooth, unbroken coil, fine checking only); rework = noticeable grittiness, wash and retest; scrap = deep shrinkage cracks that persist after tempering at 10–20% (that deposit is for coarse ware or brick only).
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) coil breaks at once → plasticity short, check sand content from the settling test first, then consider blending in a more plastic clay; (2) deep shrinkage cracks → excess shrinkage, retest with 10% and 20% washed quartz sand (0.5–1 mm) temper; (3) settling layers unreadable → not stood long enough or water too turbid; (4) heavy grittiness on the lick test → high-sand deposit, use as temper or brick clay only.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one complete three-test plus settling record; L2 = records for three deposits that allow ranking them by data; L3 = a second person retests the same sample from this card and reaches the same conclusions (if not, the criteria in this card are still too soft and need rewriting).

### 8.3 Wood Ash, Limestone, and Other Basic Feedstocks

**Wood-ash composition (qualitative description + engineering uses)**: mainly potassium carbonate, calcium and magnesium carbonates, phosphates, silicates, and traces of sodium. Hardwood ash and softwood ash differ in their potassium/calcium ratio; grass-straw ash is silica-rich. Ash is the only alkali source obtainable at scale in this stage.

**Lye (ash water) leaching**:

- Ratio: ash:water = 1:3 to 1:5 (by volume, measured with the Chapter 3 standard wooden bucket).
- Process: suspend the ash in a wooden trough or hide bag to steep 2–4 hours (rule of thumb, must be field-calibrated), stir twice, let settle, pour off the clear liquid; a second leach can raise the concentration.
- Criterion: a slippery feel = strong alkalinity (the feel test is qualitative — it judges presence, not concentration).
- **Egg-floating concentration grades (completed in v2: upgrading "slippery" into comparable grades)**: put a fresh egg into the lye — **sinks to the bottom = weak (about the 5% grade and below); hangs half-suspended upright = medium (about 5–10%); floats with the exposed top about the size of a thumb = strong (about the 10–15% grade)** (an empirical correspondence for potassium carbonate solutions, must be field-calibrated: mix your own solutions of known grades and measure this egg's corresponding postures — egg size affects posture, so each team calibrates with its own eggs). Use the medium/strong grades for soapmaking and the weak grade for leaching and re-leaching; record egg posture and use together, batch by batch, to build the team's "egg-posture–use" table. Eggs washed after use remain edible (brief lye soaking is harmless).
- Uses (at this stage): cleaning greasy tools and degreasing hides; small trials of hemp retting to assist degumming; the alkali source for later saponification soapmaking (formal saponification in Chapter 23).
- Warning: lye corrodes skin and eyes; rinse after handling; keep it off wounds; its containers are dedicated and marked (Section 8.4 pigment marking).

**Limestone identification (this chapter only identifies and stocks it; calcining is in Chapter 13)**:

- Appearance: gray-white, fine-grained, dense, usually bedded; hardness about 3 (a bone flake barely scratches it; quartz scratches it easily).
- Acid test (natural acid source): drop sorrel-leaf juice or soured fruit pulp on a freshly scratched face; fizzing = carbonate (calcite). Powder that fizzes slowly may be dolomite — note the distinction when registering.
- Distinguishing from quartzite: quartzite is hardness 7 and quartz cannot scratch it; limestone takes a white scratch from quartz and fizzes.
- Registration: site location, estimated quantity, apparent purity (how many impurity vein lines), into the resource map.

**Other basic feedstock registration checklist** (location, estimated quantity, verification test, intended use): salt (salt springs, salt earth, sea salt — taste test and evaporation residue); sulfur (yellow crystalline blocks in volcanic areas; blue flame and acrid fumes when burned — register, do not mine; handled in Chapter 23); trona (white frost on alkaline lakes); bitumen/petroleum seeps (black, sticky, flammable); quartz sand (abrasive and Chapter 14 glass feedstock; grain criteria: rounded grains, silt-free, 0.1–0.5 mm); ochre ore (8.4); graphite (black shiny scratch mark; register).

[Card 08-01 Lye Extraction and the Floating-Egg Scale] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: wood ash (**hardwood ash preferred** — higher potassium-to-calcium ratio, stronger lye; softwood ash is weak — never mix and record them as one); water; one fresh egg (for the floating test; wash and reuse); a dedicated, clearly marked lye vessel (red stripe, see 8.4). Ash must be stored dry; once it cakes from damp, extraction yield drops.
2. **Tools required**: standard wooden measure (Chapter 3, for the volume ratio), wooden trough or hide bag for steeping, stirring stick, **a dedicated marked lye container**. Calibration source: ratio measured by volume with the same measure every time; strength graded by the floating egg, no instrument.
3. **Sequence and control points**: ash into vessel → add water at the volume ratio → steep 2–4 hours → stir twice midway → stand → decant the clear supernatant → second extraction if a stronger liquor is needed. Control points: **steeping time and stir count**; decant only after standing — liquor carrying grit etches unevenly and clogs.
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: ash-to-water 1:3 to 1:5 by volume; steep 2–4 hours (empirical, calibrate against your ash on site); stir twice. **Floating-egg scale** (upgrades "feels slippery" to a comparable grade): fresh egg in the liquor — sinks = weak (below about 5%); stands half-suspended = medium (about 5–10%); floats = strong. **The hand-feel test only tells presence, not strength, and cannot replace the egg scale.**
5. **Observations to record**: per batch — ash type (hardwood / softwood / mixed) / volume ratio / steeping time / stir count / egg grade / intended use (cleaning, degreasing, retting, soap-making feedstock).
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = egg grade meets what that use requires; rework = grade too low, run a second extraction or steep longer and retest; scrap = no soapy feel and the egg sinks flat (ash spent or not wood ash — start over with fresh ash).
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) lye too weak → check ash type first (softwood ash is low in potassium), then steeping time; (2) turbid liquor → insufficient standing or unfiltered grit; (3) large strength difference between two extractions from the same batch → incomplete records, go back and record ash type and time; (4) **corrosive injury** → lye burns skin and eyes; rinse after handling, keep away from wounds — this belongs at the head of the card, not in a footnote.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one batch plus egg-grade record; L2 = one hardwood and one softwood batch with the grade difference documented; L3 = a second person makes the same grade from this card alone, and both judge the egg consistently.

[Card 08-04 Plant Acid: Extraction and Use in the Carbonate Test] (eight fields)

> **Scope note**: this card covers only extracting an acid liquor for the limestone test, all of it supported by the body text. The "fruit-mash acidification" in the original index entry has no established process in the text, so it is not written here — better a gap than an invention. A proper vinegar process would need its own chapter.

1. **Input materials**: sorrel leaves (or any plant leaf with a clear sour taste) or soured fruit mash, either one; a freshly cut face on the rock being tested (**it must be freshly cut — a weathered face will not fizz and will make limestone look like quartzite**).
2. **Tools required**: stone mortar (crushing), cloth (wringing), reed or thin bamboo tube (for dropping, more controlled than a finger), record tablet.
3. **Sequence and control points**: gather leaves → crush in the mortar → wring the juice through cloth into a small vessel → drop one drop onto the fresh face with the tube → watch for fizzing. Control points: freshness of the cut face; drop size just enough to bead and not run off.
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: fizzing = carbonate (calcite group, i.e. limestone); **slow fizzing** may be dolomite and must be recorded separately; no fizzing = not a carbonate. Distinguishing from quartzite requires both checks: quartzite has hardness 7 and quartz will not scratch it; limestone is scratched white by quartz **and** fizzes.
5. **Observations to record**: per site — acid source (sorrel juice / fruit mash) / position of the cut / fizzing speed / whether the hardness test agreed.
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = acid and hardness tests agree and clearly separate limestone from quartzite; rework = acid too weak (change source or concentrate) or face weathered (recut); scrap = the two tests contradict and no verdict is possible — do not list that site as limestone for now.
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) nothing fizzes anywhere → check acid strength first, then whether the face is weathered; (2) fizzes but slowly → possibly dolomite, record as "undetermined" rather than using it as pure limestone; (3) quartzite mistaken for limestone → the fizzing was read without the hardness test; always run both.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = acid and hardness record for one site; L2 = three sites, at least one judged non-carbonate, with records that reproduce the judgment; L3 = a second person judges the same batch from this card alone with the same conclusions.

### 8.4 Natural Pigments and Surface Treatment

**Pigment mineral sources and color families**:

| Color | Source | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Red | hematite (reddish-brown streak) | streak tested by scratching on a coarse stone slab |
| Yellow-brown | limonite/goethite | — |
| Black | manganese ore; carbon black (collected from resinous-fire smoke) | carbon black is easiest to obtain and the most stable |
| White | chalk, kaolin | distinguish from limestone: kaolin does not fizz with acid |
| White (substitute) | shell powder | burned, then ground fine |

**Processing workflow**: smash → dry-grind on a stone anvil → **water-settling classification**: stir the fine powder into water in a trough to cloud it, let stand briefly, pour the suspended upper layer into a second trough, and after settling keep the bottom — this yields the fine pigment fraction; repeat 2–3 times for finer grades. After settling, fineness passes when "rubbed between the fingers it feels grain-free."

**Mixing**: pigment slurry + binder (gum solution 8.5, animal fat, or egg white), starting from 2:1 by volume (rule of thumb, must be field-calibrated). Paint a stripe on a sample board (a planed board or stone slab) for every batch and number it; record recipe and date.

**Uses** (the three formal uses at this stage):

1. Tool and container marking: ownership, numbering, hazards (red stripe on lye containers) — the first carrier of the information lineage.
2. Test specimen numbering: one-to-one correspondence with the Section 8.6 record sheets.
3. Wood surface treatment: many thin rubs of animal fat (waterproofing); hot-melt beeswax coating (in beekeeping regions); pine-resin coating (8.5); smoke-blackening (insect repellence, decoration).

### 8.5 Resins, Plant Gums, and Natural Binder Materials

**Pine resin collection**:

- Living-tree cuts: with a stone-flake knife cut a slanted downward V-groove into the trunk, stopping 1–2 cm below the cambium; **girdling is strictly forbidden** (girdling kills the tree). ≤3 cuts per tree per season.
- Collecting: fit a birch-bark gutter at the lower end of the cut and collect every 1–2 weeks; also gather hardened resin lumps from old tree scars.
- Processing: warm fresh resin until it thins and use it at once; long storage volatilizes and hardens it — it can be restored with gentle heat (low fire, do not boil; near flames there is flash-fire risk).

**Birch-bark tar (the first high-strength glue; pottery-free method)**:

1. Roll birch bark (scrape off the hard outer rind, use the inner layer) into a tight solid cylinder 5–8 cm in diameter.
2. Bury the bark cylinder upright in a dirt pit, tamp soil firmly around it, leave a small hole at the top, and bank a charcoal fire around the pit.
3. After 1–2 hours of sealed pyrolysis (rule of thumb, must be field-calibrated), withdraw the fire; once cool, dig it out and scrape the glossy black tar from the cylinder's inner wall and core.
4. Pass criterion: draws threads while warm, still slightly tacky and plastic when cold; brittle and hard = overburned; thin and runny = underburned.

Principle: oxygen-starved pyrolysis converts betulin and other constituents of birch bark into sticky tar; the dirt pit is the "pottery-free dry-distillation retort." Yield is low (a few grams to a few tens of grams per cylinder), but the cost per run is extremely low and it can be repeated indefinitely.

**Hafting glue recipe (parts by volume)**: tar 2 + fine charcoal powder 1 + dry grass fiber or crumbled moss 0.5. Apply warm to the blade–handle mating faces, assemble and bind while hot; it sets on cooling.

**Plant gums**: dry gum lumps exuded by acacia and Prunus-type trunks, dissolved in water into a paste — low strength, used for pigment binding and light-duty joining; root-starch paste gelatinized in hot water — light adhesive for the pre-paper era.

**Hide glue (gelatin precursor; stone-boiling bypasses the container bottleneck)**:

- Feedstock: hide offcuts and scraps, minced fascia.
- Process: fill a hide bag with water and the stock, drop in fist-sized fire-heated cobbles (stone-boiling), and swap stones to hold 60–80°C (by feel, "hot but not touchable for long") for 4–8 hours (rule of thumb, must be field-calibrated); reduce the liquid until it coats a spoon; it sets to a jelly on cooling.
- Limitations: make fresh and use at once; spoils easily in hot humid weather; dried gel slabs store well and rehydrate for reuse (empirical method — register and verify). Scaling up awaits Chapter 11 pottery (direct boiling becomes possible).

**Bond-strength test (rough quantification)**: standard lap specimens — two planed wood strips lapped 3×3 cm; after the glue cures 24 hours: first break by hand (three force grades: opens with a light pull / opens with a hard pull / will not open), then hang weights in increasing steps until it separates; record the separation load and the failure face (glue-line failure = weak glue; wood tear = glue stronger than the wood). n≥3 per recipe.

**Storage**: tar lumps wrapped in leaves and kept in a cool place; gum lumps stored dry; lye and glue kept in separate zones.
[Card 08-03 Birch-Bark Tar and Hafting Glue] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: inner birch bark (outer crust scraped off, inner layer only); fine charcoal powder (sieved from the Chapter 6 bed); dry grass fiber or chopped moss (cut under 5 mm); soil (for sealing the pit). **Peel and roll the bark fresh** — dried inner bark goes brittle, will not roll tight, and yield drops sharply.
2. **Tools required**: flake knife (scraping the outer crust), earthen pit (or a spent clay pot after Chapter 11), receiving vessel (bark roll or broad leaf). Calibration source: no thermometer — judge the fire by the color and consistency of tar seeping from the wall; pyrolysis time calibrated on site against roll diameter and weather.
3. **Sequence and control points**: roll bark tight into a solid cylinder 5–8 cm across → stand it upright in the pit, backfill and tamp the soil → leave a small hole at the top → heap charcoal around the pit → pyrolyse 1–2 hours → pull the fire, let cool → scrape the glossy black tar from the inner wall and core. Control points: **the backfill must be tamped** (leaks burn the charge to ash) and **the top hole must not be omitted** (it is the vent; plugged, the roll can burst).
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: roll diameter 5–8 cm; pyrolysis 1–2 hours (empirical, calibrate on site); **three acceptance signs** — draws a thread when warm and stays slightly tacky and workable when cold = good; brittle and non-tacky = over-fired; runny = under-fired. **Hafting glue (parts by volume)**: tar 2 + fine charcoal 1 + dry grass fiber 0.5, applied warm to the blade-handle interface, assembled and lashed while hot, set on cooling.
5. **Observations to record**: per roll — bark quantity / diameter / pyrolysis time / yield in grams / consistency grade (threads / brittle / runny) / weather.
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = draws a thread and stays tacky when cold; rework = under-fired and runny, return to the pit in 20-minute steps, checking between; scrap = over-fired, brittle and non-tacky (still usable as charcoal powder, so not true waste).
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) very low yield → check whether the roll was tight first, then whether the soil seal leaked; (2) tar brittle → fire too hot or too long (over-fired); (3) tar runny and will not glue → under-fired, return to heat; (4) joints crack after gluing → too much charcoal or fiber in the mix, or assembly was not done while hot; (5) **ignition** → tar is flammable near flame; clear a flame-free radius around the retort, the same discipline as the fire rules in 6.8.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one roll of acceptable tar plus consistency record; L2 = five consecutive rolls with comparable yield and grade, and at least one tool glued with the hafting mix that passes a load test; L3 = a second person produces the same grade from this card alone, with yields within 50% of each other (a wider gap points to technique or bark source differences).

### 8.6 How to Run Small-Scale Materials Tests

**Five principles of testing**:

1. Single variable: change one factor at a time (recipe, water content, drying method, firing temperature).
2. Control: every group must have a baseline group (the variable left unchanged).
3. Repetition: n≥3 per group; take the median result.
4. Standard specimens: unified dimensions — clay bar 12×2×2 cm, clay ball 4 cm, glue lap 3×3 cm, cord sample 50 cm.
5. Full records: failed specimens are kept and recorded like any other; failure data carry the same weight as success data.

**Standard specimen library**: mark finished specimens with scribed numbers (bow-drill tip or stone-flake marks) + pigment color dots (8.4); store them dry on racks off the ground; re-inspect once a month (moisture uptake and warping, cracking, mold spots).

**Record-sheet template (wooden tablet)**:

| Field | Example |
|---|---|
| Number/date | T-011 / before the rainy season of Restart Year 3 |
| Objective | compare the dry-crack rate of East Slope clay with 20% sand temper |
| Materials | East Slope clay batch 2, river sand sieved 0.5–1 mm |
| Recipe | clay 4 : sand 1 (volume, by bucket) |
| Process parameters | wedged 12 minutes; shade-dried 48 hours |
| Environment | overcast; humidity by feel "damp" |
| Result | 1 of 3 pieces with hairlines; dry shrinkage 7% (10 cm line → 9.3 cm) |
| Conclusion | hairlines acceptable; proceed to firing trial |
| Next step | make fire-baked test pieces at the same recipe |

**Worked example — preliminary design for "the local best pottery recipe"**: 3 clay sources × 3 sand ratios (0/10/20%) = 9 groups × 3 pieces = 27 pieces, with unified drying and bake trial (15 minutes on a charcoal bed), graded (no crack / hairline / cracked / shattered). This design yields the starting recipe for Chapter 11.

### 8.7 How to Record Recipes and Process Parameters

**Record carriers (pre-paper stage)**:

| Carrier | How made | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Wooden tablet | planed softwood board + charcoal pencil (a charred, pointed charcoal stick) | day-to-day test records; can be scraped and rewritten |
| Birch bark | large sheets stripped and flattened; written in charcoal pencil | duplicate copies, carrying |
| Notched stone/wood | symbols cut with a stone flake | permanent key data (baselines, recipe numbers) |
| Knots + pigment marks | — | memory aids; never the primary archive |

Storage: dry and off the ground, rodent-proof; **two copies stored in different places** (one fire must not destroy the whole archive).

**Measurement discipline (Iron Law: "add to taste" is forbidden)**:

- All ratios use volume standards: self-made wooden buckets (verified against the Chapter 3 baseline), mussel shells (dedicated after their capacity is calibrated), the standard rod (per the Chapter 3 length units).
- Water content is described by the "feel ladder + coil criteria" (8.2), never as "add some water."
- Temperature is described by fire position + the state of a standard specimen (6.7), never as "burn for a while."

**Record-field discipline**: date, objective, materials (source + batch number), recipe, process, environment, result, conclusion, next step — a missing field voids the record.

**Version management (implementing the Chapter 4 rules)**: a recipe change must take a new number (T-011 → T-011b) and never overwrite the old version; failed recipes are kept with the cause of failure noted.

**Inheritance test**: a novice independently reproduces the result once from the records; a dimensional deviation >15% counts as a gap in the records — review the process for holes and backfill the record. The final acceptance of an archive is not "it is all written down" but "someone else can follow it and succeed."

- Lime calcining (Chapter 13) and mortar: this chapter only completes limestone identification and stock preparation.
- High-purity reagents and glassware (Chapters 14, 26): the precision ceiling of all liquid chemistry is constrained by container material.

## Safety Boundary

- Wear dry-leaf glove pads for lye work and guard against splashes into the eyes; containers are always marked with a red stripe and never mixed with tableware.
- Do not process pyrite or sulfur ore samples near fire (burning gives off an irritant gas).
- While the tar pit smolders, no one may bend over the vent hole; do not open the pit before the fire is withdrawn and it cools.
- Swap stone-boiling stones with wooden tongs or a hide pad; never grab hot stones with wet hands.
- Keep the specimen library away from fire and rodent runs; discard moldy specimens and record it.

## Acceptance Criteria

- **L1 proof-of-principle**: complete clay test bars passing the three tests; one successful birch-bark tar run that bonds a lap joint of wood securely.
- **L2 repeatable manufacture**: 2 or more clay sites pass the three tests and are archived; tar succeeds 3 times running with bond-strength test data on file (at least 1 case at wood-tear grade); ash lye leaching reproducible; the 9-group "best recipe" pre-study completed with clear conclusions.
- **L3 sustainable production**: tar feedstock (birch) registered and harvested within the cut quota; monthly-output and loss accounts kept for glue, pigments, and lye; the record system independently reproduced and verified by a second person on ≥1 item; specimen-library monthly inspection in execution.
