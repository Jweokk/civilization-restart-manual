### 5.1 How to Identify Stone Suitable for Knapping

Five criteria for knappable stone; all must be satisfied before test-knapping:

1. **Homogeneous**: color and grain size uniform across the fresh fracture; no banding, mottling, or interlayers.
2. **Fine-grained**: no crystal grains detectable by eye or fingertip. The fracture surface should look like "solidified syrup", not "granulated sugar".
3. **Conchoidal fracture**: a freshly knocked-off fracture shows a curved surface, often with concentric ripples; the edge is naturally sharp.
4. **Isotropic**: striking from any direction produces the same conchoidal fracture; no fixed splitting plane (unlike the bedding of slate and shale, or the cleavage planes of feldspar-class crystals).
5. **Fresh**: "rotten stone" with whitened weathered surfaces and powder that scrapes off under a fingernail is usually riddled with microcracks; forbidden.

Four-step field procedure:

* Step one, **find**: prefer river-bank gravel (the water has already done the sorting; weak stones were eliminated by transport and abrasion); second, nodules in limestone hills (lumpy masses along the limestone bedding planes); third, conglomerate cementation zones and volcanic slope deposits.

* Step two, **knock**: knock a corner off with a stone in hand and inspect the fresh fracture; listen at the same time: a crisp "ring" means dense and crack-free; a dull thud suggests internal cracks or porosity.

* Step three, **scratch**: grade by mutual-scratch testing (against Mohs hardness: fingernail ≈ 2.5, bone splinter ≈ 3, quartz ≈ 7). Qualified edge material should be scratched by quartz and yet able to scratch bone — that is a siliceous rock of roughly Mohs 6–7.

* Step four, **record**: location, lithology, fracture description, and test-knapping results, registered into the resource map (Chapter 2 format).

Exclusion list:

| Excluded | Characteristics | Reason |
| -- | -- | -- |
| Shale, slate, schist | Splits into sheets when struck; bedding planes flat | Bedding controls the cracking; a controlled cutting edge cannot be made |
| Coarse-grained granites | Grains visible to the eye | Spalls at grain boundaries; edge discontinuous |
| Strongly weathered stone | Whitened surface; powder scrapes off with a fingernail | Internal microcracks well developed; collapses entirely when struck |
| Porous volcanic rock (except pumice, which has other uses) | Many gas vesicles | Cannot form a continuous edge |

### 5.2 Flint, Cherty Rocks, and Other Knappable Stones

| Stone | Common occurrence | Fracture quality | Edge quality | Main limitation | Priority |
| -- | -- | -- | -- | -- | -- |
| Flint/chalcedony/jasper | Limestone nodules, gravel | Conchoidal, flat | Best; edge about 0.01–0.05 mm thick | Only in sedimentary-rock regions | First choice |
| Obsidian | Volcanic glass slope deposits | Flattest and glossiest | About an order of magnitude thinner than flint; sharpest of all | Extremely brittle; collapses under chopping; cutting only | Use if available |
| Quartzite | Metamorphosed sandstone, river gravel | Disturbed by grains, rough | Oversized edge serrations, short service life | Thickened edges only | Second choice |
| Siliceous limestone nodules | Limestone regions | Near-flint | Good | Often interlaced with white calcite veins; cracks along the veins | Test nodule by nodule |
| Hornfels, dense basalt | Contact-metamorphic zones, igneous regions | Tough, not brittle | Unsuitable for knapped thin edges | Take the grinding route (Section 5.5) | First choice for ground tools |
| Rhyolite/felsite | Pyroclastic regions | Moderate | Moderate | Limited occurrence | Backup |
| Quartz crystals | Vein quartz | Cracks run along healed fissures and grain boundaries (no cleavage; fracture discontinuous; often milky/smoky inclusions) | Uncontrollable | Use only as a battering hammer | Excluded as edge material |

Search strategy: river gravel first, limestone nodules second, obsidian slope deposits in volcanic regions third. Marker of ancient quarry sites — large piles of sharp stone debris on slopes that natural transport cannot explain.

**Alternative-route boundary (Iron Law 5)**: in flint-free environments the realistic order is quartzite/siliceous limestone (knapped) + hornfels/basalt (ground). Performance losses must be stated: edge angles need thickening by 10°–20°; cutting life on the same task is roughly halved (rule of thumb, must be field-calibrated); the knapping reject rate rises and long thin flakes become hard to obtain. Conclusion: the alternative route does not affect the validity of later chapters' technical chains; it only lowers efficiency and yield.

### 5.3 Flakes, Cores, and Sharp Edges

**Principle — the Hertzian cone**: a hard object striking a stone surface propagates stress downward in a cone (cone angle about 136°, an empirical constant). When the strike point lands at the edge of the core's striking platform and the platform angle (the angle between the platform and the side face to be flaked) is less than 90°, the cone intersects the core edge and the crack runs along the predetermined path "platform edge — core side face", detaching a flake with a sharp edge. At a platform angle ≥ 90°, the impact energy turns into local crushing, and what you get is debris, not a flake.

Three geometric parameters (measured with the Chapter 3 angle board):

| Parameter | Definition | Control range |
| -- | -- | -- |
| Platform angle | Angle between platform and flaking face | Flaking requires < 90°; ideal 70°–85° |
| Edge angle | Angle between the two faces of the flake's edge | Set by use (see Section 5.5) |
| Length-to-thickness ratio | Flake length/thickness | About 2:1 with hard-hammer flaking; 3:1 and above with a soft hammer |

Flaking procedure:

1. **Select the core**: fist to two fists in size, no visible cracks, at least one flat face usable as a platform.
2. **Dress the platform (hard hammer)**: with a 300–600 g dense-gravel hammer, knock a continuous flat band along the core edge, 10–20 mm wide.
3. **Detach flakes (hard hammer)**: hold the core in the left hand (always padded with hide or thick bark) and strike 5–10 mm in from the platform edge with the right-hand hammer in short snapping blows (swing amplitude 30–40 cm). Accurate placement outranks force.
4. **Fine flaking (soft hammer)**: switch to an antler section or hardwood bar (200–400 g) and "brush-strike" close against the platform edge to detach longer, thinner flakes.
5. **Blades (upgrade item)**: dress the core into a prismatic shape and detach parallel long blades in sequence along the ridges — the most economical use of material and the straightest edges.

Failure troubleshooting (expanded to five patterns in v2): ① all debris → strike point too far from the platform edge or platform angle too large; re-dress the platform; ② flake splits lengthwise → internal cracks in the stone; discard the material; ③ hammer slips → platform edge too rounded; knock a sharp ridge out again; ④ **step (hinge)** — the flake breaks midway and leaves a stepped shelf on the core → insufficient strike energy or placement too deep; restart the next flake from the outer edge of the shelf; do not force blows directly from the base of the shelf; ⑤ **over-half edge collapse** — more than half the ring of the edge has spalled away → hammer blows are standing in for pressure flaking; switch to light soft-hammer flaking.

**The three lashings of the stone axe and load testing (completed in v2; connects to the quadruple attachment of Section 9.1)**:

| Knot | How tied | Used for | Characteristics |
| -- | -- | -- | -- |
| Slip loop (slip knot) | Wrap the cord one turn and pull tight to lock | First positioning of edge to handle | Fast; must be re-tightened under load |
| Cross-wrapping knot | Figure-eight wraps, 6–10 turns, at the "edge–handle" crossing; finish with a square knot | Main load-bearing lashing | Shear-resistant |
| Stick-twist tensioning knot | After lashing, insert a short stick and twist the cord 2–3 turns; press the free end in | Prevents handle-end slip | Tightest; can be loosened and readjusted |

**Load test (mandatory after every hafting; current lashing material = temporary binding of vine/wet rawhide strips; cord and knots are a Chapter 7 preview — backfill the formal lashing once Chapter 7 is unlocked)**: swing the axe into bare wood 20 times — lashing displacement < 2 mm and no creaking is a pass; then re-check with 1.5x impact (5 full-force blows into hardwood). If displacement exceeds the limit, twist it tighter and retest first; if still loose, re-lash with a longer binding section. Lashings are consumables: **bindings soaked by rain or immersion must be retested** (wet expansion and dry shrinkage change the binding force).

[Card 05-01 Platform Preparation and Hard-Hammer Flaking] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: a core from fist- to double-fist size (no visible cracks, at least one flat face usable as a platform); a dense cobble hammer, 300–600 g; one piece of hide or thick bark as a hand pad. **Reject any blank with a visible crack without test-striking it** — a cracked blank shatters ten times out of ten and wastes hours.
2. **Tools required**: hard hammer (inspect its striking face once every half-day; on any penny-shaped spall, turn it or replace it), angle gauge (Chapter 3, for platform angle), anvil block (keeps the core from rolling). Calibration source: hammer weight balanced on the Chapter 3 balance into the 300–600 g band; angle gauge self-checked by the 3-4-5 cord method.
3. **Sequence and control points**: select core → dress the platform (strike a continuous flat band 10–20 mm wide along the core edge) → flake (strike 5–10 mm in from the platform edge, 30–40 cm stroke, short snapping blow) → inspect the flake. **Two control points: the platform angle must be <90° (measured with the gauge; ideal 70–85°); the strike must land 5–10 mm from the edge — placement beats force.**
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: platform angle 70–85° (eyeballing is unreliable; always use the gauge — with no gauge, cut a 75° template by the 3-4-5 cord method and compare); hammer 300–600 g (balanced); stroke 30–40 cm (about one-third of arm length); length-to-thickness target 2:1 (before calipers exist, judge by pinching the flake between two fingers; measure directly once Chapter 20 gauges appear).
5. **Observations to record**: per core — measured platform angle / hammer weight / whether the strike drifted / number of good flakes / share of debris / failure-mode number (field 7).
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = five or more consecutive flakes from the same core, length-to-thickness ≥1.8:1, edge flat with no step; rework = a step has formed, restart the flake from the outer edge of the step (**never hammer straight into the step floor**); scrap = the blank splits lengthwise (internal crack) or three consecutive strikes yield only debris (wrong stone).
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) all debris → first check whether the strike is too far from the edge, then whether the platform angle is ≥90°; (2) lengthwise split → internal crack, discard the blank, do not blame technique; (3) hammer skids → platform edge too rounded, dress a sharp ridge; (4) hinge → insufficient force or strike too deep; (5) edge crumbling past halfway → switch from hard to soft hammer (go to Card 05-02).
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one acceptable flake plus one record sheet; L2 = ≥60% success over ten consecutive cores plus per-core records; L3 = a second person reaches L2 from this card alone, and the two batches' platform-angle distributions can be compared.

[Card 05-03 Stone Axe Hafting and Load Test] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: a knapped axe head (bifacial, tough stone 15 cm or longer); a straight dry handle (diameter matched to the hafting groove — too thin it twists, too thick it will not bind); vine or wet rawhide strip (**temporary binding only in this chapter; the proper cord and knots arrive in Chapter 7 and are back-filled**); one short stick for tightening; a wooden tablet for records.
2. **Tools required**: stone knife (trimming vine ends), wooden wedge (opening the groove), scored tablet (displacement datum). Calibration source: no dedicated gauge; displacement read against scored lines to about 1 mm.
3. **Sequence and control points**: slip knot to locate (blade to first pass on the handle) → cross wrapping (figure-eight 6–10 passes at the blade-handle crossing, finished with a flat knot) → toggle-stick tightening (2–3 turns, end tucked in) → **load test (mandatory after every hafting, never skipped)**. Control points: wrap count and tightening turns are written into the record, not left to "looks about right".
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: 6–10 cross wraps (fewer and shear strength fails); 2–3 tightening turns (more snaps the vine); test standard = 20 swings into empty air, binding displacement <2 mm, no creaking; recheck = five full-force blows into hard wood (1.5× impact). Displacement judged against scored lines; failure means rework.
5. **Observations to record**: per axe — wrap count / tightening turns / measured displacement after 20 swings / any creaking / whether it got wet.
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = displacement <2 mm after 20 swings and no creaking; rework = displacement over limit, tighten and retest first, and if still loose, lengthen the binding and re-haft; scrap = the hafting wood splits or the axe groove chips (change the head).
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) large displacement → check wrap count first, then tightening; (2) creaking → knot not seated, or the vine is too dry (switch to wet rawhide); (3) **after rain or soaking the binding must be retested** — swelling and shrinkage change the binding force, and this is the main cause of a flying axe head; (4) loosens after a few swings → binding section too short, load concentrated.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one hafted axe plus test record; L2 = five passing axes, at least one with a post-rain retest record; L3 = a second person hafts independently from this card and passes the test, with both batches' displacement data comparable.

### 5.4 Stone Hammers, Bone Tools, and Wooden Aids

**Two classes of stone hammer**:

* Hard hammer (direct percussion): dense rock (quartzite, flint nodules), 300–600 g, egg-shaped, one end slightly narrowed for aiming. Inspect the striking face every half day of use; when biscuit-shaped impact scars appear, switch faces or replace.

* Soft hammer (fine flaking): antler section or hardwood bar, 200–400 g. Why the soft hammer works: local deformation of the contact surface absorbs the impact spike, and the crack starts inside the core rather than at the hammer mark, so the flakes come off thinner and longer; the cost is fast wear.

**Bone tools** (raw material from collected bones of naturally dead animals and scavenger leftovers):

| Bone tool | Making | Use |
| -- | -- | -- |
| Pressure flaker | Antler tip ground round and blunt | Pressure-flaking and edge retouch (Section 5.5) |
| Bone awl | Long bone split lengthwise into strips; tip wet-ground on a sandstone slab | Holes in leather goods, aid to cordage work |
| Bone needle | Fine bone strip ground to shape with water on a fine sandstone slab, 1–3 h per needle (rule of thumb, must be field-calibrated) | Sewing and net making (awaits Chapter 7 fibers and Chapter 15 textiles) |
| Bone scraper blade | Shoulder-blade edge ground to an edge | Scraping residual flesh from hides (more durable than stone blades) |

Bone preparation: air-dry fresh bone to half-dry before working; too wet and it gums up the grinding stone, too dry and it chips and snaps. Fatty bone smells after long storage but does not affect use.

**Wooden aids**: wooden anvil (a short stout block to pad the core and stop it rolling), bark or hide hand-guard pad (mandatory under the hand holding the core), spare wedge stock (for the Section 5.7 placeholder).

Every tool in this section can be rebuilt from "collection + stone hammer + grinding stone"; the lineage is self-sustaining, meeting the requirement.

### 5.5 How to Make Cutting, Scraping, and Drilling Tools

Specification table for the four tool classes:

| Tool | Material/shape | Edge angle | Use boundary |
| -- | -- | -- | -- |
| Flake knife | 8–15 cm straight-edged flake; ridges and corners in the grip zone knocked blunt to prevent cuts | 20°–40° | Cutting meat, hides, grass stems; no chopping |
| Scraper | Thick flake with end or side edge blunted | 45°–70° | Scraping hides and wood surfaces; only a large edge angle withstands vertical scraping force — a blunt edge is a design requirement |
| Pointed tool (drill-point blank) | Short stout point pressure-flaked symmetrically on both sides | Point angle 60°–80° | Drilling and incising; drill points for Chapters 7 and 9 |
| Hand axe/chopper | Bifacially knapped; tough stone (quartzite, hornfels) 15 cm or larger | 60°–90° | Chopping wood, cracking bone; never for prying |

**Pressure-flaking retouch** (turning "can cut" into "works well"): press the antler pressure flaker against a spot 2–3 mm above the edge at about 45° to the stone surface (rule of thumb), pushing down with combined arm and chest force to pop off small flakes 2–5 mm wide; work segment by segment around the edge to produce uniform serrations shallower than 1 mm. Uniform serrations = uniform loading = no local edge collapse. After retouch the edge shows a regular ripple rather than uneven notches — this is the conforming visual criterion.

**Serrated flake (the embryo of the saw)**: select a long straight flake and pressure-flake continuous serrations with a 2–3 mm tooth pitch along one edge. This chapter makes only the blank for a hand reciprocating saw; the formal bow saw, mounted in a frame, is in Section 9.2.

**Grinding placeholder**: hornfels and basalt blanks can be given an edge by wet grinding on a sandstone slab — time-consuming (on the order of hours) but the edge body is tough; this is the proper route for felling tools. The complete grinding process unfolds after tooling-up in Section 9.2.

All outputs of this section are numbered in one scheme and registered into the tool archive (wooden tablet, Chapter 3 record format).

[Card 05-02 Pressure Flaking and Edge Retouch] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: a struck flake blank (edge cuts but is ragged); an antler billet or hardwood billet, 200–400 g, as soft hammer; an antler tip ground blunt as pressure flaker; coarse and fine sandstone slabs; hand pad.
2. **Tools required**: soft hammer, pressure flaker, sandstone slabs, angle gauge. Calibration source: billet weight balanced on the Chapter 3 balance; pressure-point position by eye plus marker (measured once Chapter 20 arrives).
3. **Sequence and control points**: soft-hammer "stroke" along the platform edge (hug the edge, do not strike air — yields longer, thinner flakes) → seat the pressure flaker 2–3 mm above the edge at about 45°, press with arm-and-chest weight, removing 2–5 mm chips → work once around the edge. Control points: 2–3 mm from the edge and about 45°; deviation in either causes edge collapse.
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: soft hammer 200–400 g; pressure point 2–3 mm from the edge (empirical, calibrate to local stone); pressure angle about 45° (empirical); chip width 2–5 mm; finished serration depth <1 mm. **How to establish "45°"**: cut a 45° template by the Chapter 3 3-4-5 cord method and hold it against the edge face; do not rely on feel.
5. **Observations to record**: per pass — chip width / serration depth / collapse locations and count / whether the step spacing stayed even.
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = edge shows an even wave (not a jagged notch), serration depth <1 mm, still cuts after 20 strokes; rework = local collapse, retouch from both sides of the notch inward, do not patch only the notch; scrap = edge worn below 3 mm (reclassify as scraper or discard).
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) edge collapse → check whether the pressure angle is too steep, then whether force is excessive; (2) flakes will not come off → flaker too blunt, or seated too far in (>3 mm); (3) uneven serration → uneven step spacing, switch to counting steps; (4) edge getting thinner and thinner → chips too wide, back off to 2 mm.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one retouched knife edge plus an edge photo (or rubbing); L2 = five consecutive passes plus serration-depth records; L3 = a second person reaches L2 from this card alone with a scrap rate <20%.

[Card 05-04 Edge Grinding and Bevel-Angle Verification] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: dulled flake or ground blank (tough stone such as hornfels or basalt); coarse and fine sandstone slabs; water; hand pad.
2. **Tools required**: sandstone slabs (coarse for shaping, fine for establishing the edge — never interchange), pressure flaker, angle gauge, wooden tablet. Calibration source: bevel angle measured with the Chapter 3 gauge; slab flatness verified by rubbing two slabs face to face and checking for light gaps (rough precursor to the Chapter 20 three-plate method).
3. **Sequence and control points**: coarse sandstone to shape (remove notches, true the edge line) → fine sandstone with water to establish the bevel → pressure-flake the serration (go to Card 05-02) → verify bevel angle. Control point: **the bevel angle must fall inside the use band** — flake knife 20–40°, scraper 45–70°, awl blank 60–80°, axe 60–90°; anything outside is rework.
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: the four use bands above (from the 5.5 specification table); grinding takes hours (empirical, varies with local stone hardness, must be calibrated); grind with a single-direction push, never pull back (pulling rounds the edge).
5. **Observations to record**: per session — measured bevel angle / edge-line straightness (check for light gaps) / grinding time / any one-sided bias.
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = bevel in the use band, no light gap along the edge line, still cuts after 20 strokes; rework = bevel out of band or biased, re-true and regrind; scrap = body thinner than 3 mm or a through-crack in the edge line.
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) will not cut → bevel too large (blunt), return to coarse grinding to reduce it; (2) chips on first use → bevel too small (brittle), increase it or switch to pressure flaking; (3) growing asymmetry → one side ground too long, alternate sides by count; (4) edge shiny but will not bite → over-ground into a round bevel, return to pressure flaking for serration.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one acceptable edge plus measured bevel record; L2 = one of each of the four tool types with per-item bevel records; L3 = a second person reaches L2 from this card alone, with the two people's same-use bevel measurements within 5°.

### 5.6 How to Check Whether a Stone Tool Suits Its Intended Use

**Visual inspection (100% inspection)**:

1. Edge serrations uniform, serration depth < 1 mm.
2. Rotate slowly against the light; the edge's reflected highlight must be continuous — an interruption is a microcrack or nick.
3. Flick the back of the stone with a finger: a crisp sound means no internal cracks; a dull sound means cracks — downgrade or scrap.
4. No through cracks, and no radiating hidden cracks near the bulb of percussion.

**Functional testing (sampled, on standardized workpieces)**:

| Test | Standard | Pass line (rule of thumb, must be field-calibrated) |
| -- | -- | -- |
| Cutting | 50 consecutive strokes through hide or fresh meat; one stroke = one 10 cm pass | ≤ 1 nick and ≤ 2 mm |
| Scraping | 30 reciprocating scrapes on a wet hide, 5 cm per stroke | No nick > 2 mm; edge angle increase < 5° |
| Drilling | Pointed tool ground and rotated on hardwood for 20 minutes | Point does not break off; no ring-shaped cracks |
| Chopping | Hand axe chops hardwood 20 times | Crushed zone < 10% of edge length |

**Blunting and edge collapse must be distinguished**: blunting is uniform rounding of the edge, normal wear; it can be reworked by pressure flaking and counts within service life. Edge collapse is local spalling, pointing to material microcracks, a wrong platform angle, or use beyond the tool's purpose (e.g., taking a cutting knife to chopping); find the cause first, then rework.

**Scrap criteria**: edge body too thin after 3–5 cumulative reworks; edge length loss over 1/3; a through crack appears. Scrapped pieces are converted into battering-hammer cores or pressure-flaking stock.

Every test and rework goes into the tool archive: number, stone material, date, test data, rework count, scrap reason. This archive format is the record template for the book-wide tool lineage (Chapter 4).

### 5.7 How to Go from Stone Tools to Composite Tools

**Why haft at all**: it multiplies the lever arm (with the same swing force, edge velocity ≈ arm speed × (arm length + handle length) / arm length); the hand leaves the impact zone; strike kinetic energy concentrates along the edge line.

**This chapter's boundary**: cord (Section 7.4), adhesive (Section 8.5), and bow drill (Section 7.6) have not appeared; this chapter does two "placeholder validations" only:

* Option one (semi-composite): split the end of a short wooden handle along the grain, seat the flake blade in the split, and wrap temporarily with fresh bark strips or rawhide strips. Drawback: it loosens as it dries and must be re-wrapped before each use. This option only validates blade-to-handle shape matching; it is not a formal tool.

* Option two (fire-burned groove, to be implemented after the Chapter 6 stable fire): first scorch the planned groove line of the handle into a char line, then scrape deeper with a flake. Hot working replaces precision working, dropping the groove's precision requirement by one grade.

**Placeholder acceptance**: swing the hafted hand axe empty 20 times → lightly chop a wooden block 20 times; lashing displacement < 2 mm and no blade-body shift passes the placeholder.

**Pre-registration of dependencies for the formal composite tool** (to prevent later-chapter tool anachronisms):

| Step | Dependency | Solved in |
| -- | -- | -- |
| Cutting the handle groove | Charring + scraping; rows of drilled holes with the bow drill | 6.5; 7.6 |
| Lashing | Cordage, rawhide strips, sinew | 7.3, 7.4 |
| Bonding | Birch-bark tar/pine resin glue | 8.5 |
| Locking | Wooden wedges | 7.5 |

The complete hafting procedure is given in one place in Section 9.1.

* Knapping depends on individual skill; the reject rate is unquantified — to be backfilled after the Section 9.5 capacity ledger is established.

## Safety Boundary

* During knapping, the 3 m fan-shaped zone in front of the strike point is off-limits; observers may only stand to the knapper's side and rear.

* The hand holding the core must be padded with hide or thick bark; gripping a small core bare-handed for hard blows is forbidden — always use the wooden anvil.

* Check edges with a light sweep of the back of the hand; stroking along the edge with a fingertip is forbidden.

* Obsidian debris is extremely sharp and hard to see; it must be swept up and collected at knock-off.

* Cracked stone is forbidden as hammer stock — it will burst apart and fly in mid-strike.

## Acceptance Criteria

* **L1 proof-of-principle**: knock off at least one sharp flake with a conchoidal fracture that can cut through a grass stem and strip bark.

* **L2 repeatable manufacture**: following the archive-recorded selection–flaking–retouch process, complete 1 set (knife + scraper + point + hammer) on each of 3 different collection days, all passing the Section 5.6 functional tests; per-piece time and reject rate recorded.

* **L3 sustainable production**: stone sources registered in the resource map ≥ 2; hard/soft hammer wear rates known with replacement sources; tool archive holding ≥ 20 items; rework procedure executed ≥ 5 times; novice reproduction success rate from the archives ≥ 50% (empirical criterion, assessed within the team).
