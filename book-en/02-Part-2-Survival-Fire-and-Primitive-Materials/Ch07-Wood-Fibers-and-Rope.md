### 7.1 How to Identify Wood Suitable for Working

**Field three-level hardness criteria** (without relying on any metal tools):

1. Fingernail pressed into the sapwood: a deep mark with slow rebound = softwood; almost no mark = hardwood.
2. Cross-cut scoring with the stone-flake knife: a smooth cut face and flaky chips = fine, dense, good stock; a rough, torn cut face = coarse or slanted grain.
3. Heft and tap pieces of the same size: heavy with a crisp ring = dense; light with a muffled thud = loose or porous.

**Four structural checks**: straight grain (the grain lines do not twist when followed along the length); few and live knots (dead knots have a black ring around them and drop out); no cracks (especially radial splits on the end face); no wormholes (reject pieces where holes and powder appear together).

**Moisture state**: freshly felled wood is wet-heavy; fine surface checks and a clearly lighter weight = it has entered the air-dried state.

**Species selection by use table**:

| Use | First choice | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Tool handles, levers | Oak, locust, elm hardwoods | Hard and tough, bending-resistant |
| Bow drill board/shaft | Willow, poplar, linden | Soft, fine friction powder, easy hole formation |
| Wedges | Straight-grained Mongolian oak, hawthorn | Strong in compression along the grain, does not splinter |
| Containers (chiseled out) | Whole softwood sections | Easy to burn-hollow |
| Kindling/charcoal | See 6.2 | — |

Register bark appearance in the resource map (Chapter 2): for each woodland, record species, diameter class, quantity, and distance. Felling rules: take fallen trees and standing dead wood first; cut living trees during the cambium-active season (when bark peels easily), and only fell — never girdle.

### 7.2 Drying, Splitting, and Shaping Wood

**Drying**: green wood moisture content is about 30%–60% (empirical value; must be field-calibrated); the usage target is air-dried to 12%–18% (empirical value; must be field-calibrated).

Procedure:

1. Debark (protects the sapwood from borers).
2. Seal the ends: wrap the end grain with wet mud or bark — wood ends evaporate fastest; unsealed ends crack.
3. Stack raised off the ground with gaps between pieces; air-dry in a shady, ventilated place; avoid direct sun (differential surface-to-core shrinkage causes cracking).
4. Estimate air-drying time for thick boards at "about 1 year per 2.5 cm of thickness" (empirical value, dry climate; must be field-calibrated); small-section tool handles are usable in about 1–2 months (empirical value).

**Splitting**: splitting along the grain is the most efficient woodworking method — the crack tracks the wood grain automatically and needs no cutting force.

Procedure: read the grain direction on the end face → with the hand axe or hard hammer, drive a stone wedge in on the end-face grain line → once the crack starts, follow with the second and third wedges in sequence, advancing down the crack line → boards result. Twisted grain will not split into straight boards; such stock can only be used whole.

Radial-split boards (through the pith) are dimensionally more stable than tangential-split boards: tangential shrinkage is about 1.5–2 times radial shrinkage (empirical value; must be field-calibrated), so tabletops and ways use radial-split boards.

**Shaping**: rough-hew with the hand axe (cut from the far end toward the body; cutting against the grain always tears) → fine-shave with the stone adze (see 7.8) → smooth with the scraper → fire straightening (wet the bent section, bake it near the fire until hot to the touch, then press and hold the shape while it cools).

**Crack-prevention management**: rough-shape large pieces before drying (a cracked rough blank is a small loss); cut holes and grooves only after drying; keep thickness ratios ≤2:1 at thick-thin transitions.

### 7.3 Bark, Vines, Grass Fiber, and Animal Fiber

**Plant fiber table**:

| Source | Harvesting | Processing | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|---|
| Linden and paper-mulberry inner bark | Strip during the spring–summer cambium-active season (take only 30 cm segments of cut per tree, never a full girdle) | Rett 7–14 days (empirical value; shorter in warm water) → pound soft with a wooden mallet → comb and wash the fiber bundles out in water | Long and strong; the cordage mainstay |
| Nettle stems | Cut in autumn | Dry until they snap brittle → crush the outer skin → draw out the inner-core fiber | High strength, but prickly to harvest |
| Thatch grass, sedge, cattail leaves | Cut in the dry season | Pound soft → comb straight into bundles | Quick coarse rope, weaving |
| Fresh bark strips | Take as needed | Use immediately | Turns brittle when dry; temporary tying only |
| Vines | Harvest live | Soak soft, remove knobby growths | Whole-length tying, good elasticity |

**Animal fibers**:

- Sinew (hind-leg tendon, back strap): dry to half-dry and shred into bundles. It wraps wet and shrinks dry — after binding a stone edge it dries and self-locks; the first-choice hafting binding material (9.1).
- Rawhide strips: spiral-cut a hide into strips; wet-bound, dry-shrinking, as above.
- Horsehair and down: aids for braiding and sewing.

**Selection criteria**: fiber length ≥10 cm is preferred; pull test by hand — a single bundle pulled repeatedly 20 times without breaking; no mold spots or black specks. Storage: hang dry; wet-moisten before use to restore suppleness.

### 7.4 Rope Twisting, Weaving, and Basic Joints

**Mechanics of reverse twisting**: once a single strand is twisted, the fibers inside it grip through friction and helical geometry; but a single strand has an untwisting tendency — let go of one end and it falls apart. The solution is **reverse plying**: twist the two strands each in the Z direction, then ply the two strands around each other in the S direction. The strands' untwisting tendency is exactly canceled by the ply's winding — the whole is in torsional equilibrium, and the rope neither kinks nor loosens. This is the fundamental difference between a usable rope and "a handful of grass twisted once," which is not.

**Retting time vs. water temperature (v2 completion; linden/paper-mulberry inner bark)**: retting is microorganisms digesting pectin; **each roughly one-step rise in water temperature ("warm" against "cool") about halves the time** (empirical value; must be field-calibrated):

| Water-temperature class (Chapter 3 anchors) | Retting period (empirical starting point) | Mid-course check |
|---|---|---|
| Cool (<15 ℃) | 14–20 days | Day-7 spot check: can the outer skin be separated from the woody core? |
| Neither cold nor hot (15–25 ℃) | 7–14 days | Spot check every other day |
| Warm (25–30 ℃) | 4–8 days | Daily spot check — over-retted fiber turns brittle (snaps like paper when pulled) |

**Fiber wet-strength comparison (v2 completion; quick species reference; comparative terms)**:

| Fiber | Dry strength | Wet strength | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Linden/mulberry inner bark | High | **Higher** | Cordage mainstay: gets stronger when wet |
| Nettle | High | High | Fine cord and sewing thread |
| Dry grass stems | Medium | **Plummets** | Dry-state tying and fuel only |
| Sinew | High | Medium | Softens when wet; for seams only (7.3) |

1. **Input materials**: linden or paper-mulberry inner bark (ring-cut in spring-summer when the cambium is active; only a 30 cm strip per tree, never a full girdle); retting water.
2. **Tools required**: wooden mallet (pounding soft), clean water (combing out), temperature anchor card (to judge the water grade), record board. Calibration source: water temperature by the Chapter 3 feel grades (cool / neither cold nor hot / warm), no thermometer; time anchored to the day.
3. **Sequence and control points**: strip the bark (30 cm per tree) → ret in water → set the retting period by the water-temperature class → mid-course spot checks (does the outer skin separate from the woody core?) → pound soft with the mallet → comb the fiber bundles out in water → store hung dry. Control points: **take it out early rather than over-ret** — over-retted fiber snaps like paper when pulled.
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: cool (<15 ℃) 14–20 days, spot check on day 7; neither cold nor hot (15–25 ℃) 7–14 days, check every other day; warm (25–30 ℃) 4–8 days, check daily; **each roughly one-step rise in water temperature about halves the time** (empirical value — must be calibrated to local conditions). Criteria: outer skin separable from the woody core; fiber surviving 20 repeated pulls.
5. **Observations to record**: species / stripping date / water-temperature class / retting start and end / spot-check dates and results (separable or not, feel, smell) / wet strength (whether it actually improves when wet).
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = the fiber combs out into bundles and survives 20 pulls; rework = not fully separated — extend the ret and check daily; scrap = over-retted (snaps like paper), discard the whole batch.
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) fiber retts into mush → over-retting: first check whether the schedule was set by the highest temperature class (the warm class needs a daily check), discard the batch and shorten the next; (2) the outer skin will not peel → not the cambium-active season; collect in another season; (3) fiber is brittle and snaps → first check over-retting, then whether it dried too fast (dry storage must be hung in the shade); (4) strength collapses when wet → the wrong fiber was used (dry grass stems plummet when wet); switch to linden/mulberry inner bark.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one batch retted and combed into bundles; L2 = one batch at each of two water-temperature classes, with retting periods and spot checks that line up; L3 = a second person rets a batch from this card alone with the same fiber yield and strength feel.

**Rope-making procedure**:

1. Take two bundles of equal thickness (the bundle diameter is half the finished rope's diameter).
2. The right hand twists each strand tighter in the same direction (Z twist) while the left hand, keeping pace with the twisting, winds the two strands around each other in the opposite direction (S ply).
3. Rolling on the knee or thigh is least tiring; two-person teamwork: one feeds and splices material, one twists and plies.
4. Splicing: stagger the two strands' splice tails ≥10 cm apart, roll the overlap into the strands, and keep the joint no thicker than the rope body.
5. Finishing: tuck-braid the end three turns or whip it dead with fine cord.

[Card 07-02 Reverse-Twist Rope Making] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: two bundles of equal-thickness bast fiber (the bundle diameter decides half the finished rope diameter); fine cord (for whipping the ends).
2. **Tools required**: bare hands (rolling on the knee or thigh); a stone-flake knife for cutting rope; known-weight stones and water bags (for the 07-03 calibration). Calibration source: rope diameter estimated at the standard rod's sub-unit grade; load steps only from known-weight objects.
3. **Sequence and control points**: take two equal bundles → twist each strand tight in the same direction (Z twist) → with both hands in step, wind the two strands together in the opposite direction (S ply) → on splicing, stagger the two splice tails ≥10 cm and roll the overlap into the strands → tuck-braid the ends three turns or whip them dead with fine cord. Control points: **single strands Z, plying S, uniform across the whole team** (a reversed direction will kink the rope).
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: bundle diameter = half the finished rope diameter; splice overlap ≥10 cm, staggered, joint no thicker than the rope body; ends tucked three turns; diameter classes 3/6/10/16 mm with static breaking loads 30–60 / 80–150 / 200–350 / 500–800 kgf (empirical values — must be calibrated to local conditions).
5. **Observations to record**: fiber species / batch number / bundle diameter / twist direction (Z/S) / rope diameter / splice positions / maker.
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = the rope neither kinks nor back-twists, the ends do not unravel, joints no thicker than the body; rework = the rope kinks into loops (plying direction reversed) — unply and re-ply with S/Z swapped; scrap = fiber over-retted and brittle (snaps when pulled) — replace the whole batch.
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) the rope will not hold and falls apart as soon as it is released → first check whether the twist is insufficient, then whether the two strands are uneven (match thicknesses); (2) the rope kinks into loops → the plying direction is reversed; unply, swap S/Z, re-ply; (3) breaks always at the splice → first check whether the overlap is shorter than 10 cm, then whether the two splice tails sit at the same place (they must be staggered); (4) strength plummets when wet → dry-grass-type fiber was used; switch to bast fiber.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one length of rope that neither kinks nor unravels; L2 = 10 m made in each of the three diameter classes and filed; L3 = two or more people can make rope to the file independently, with the same diameter and twist direction.

**Rope diameter vs. breaking load table (empirical values; must be field-calibrated; based on three-strand linden-bark/nettle rope)**:

| Rope diameter | Static breaking load (order of magnitude) | Use |
|---|---|---|
| 3 mm | 30–60 kgf | Tying, sewing, bow drill string (4–6 mm steadier) |
| 6 mm | 80–150 kgf | Tool hafting, general hanging |
| 10 mm | 200–350 kgf | Load lashing, tripods |
| 16 mm | 500–800 kgf | Load hoisting (with pulley sets) |

**Calibration methods (mandatory for every batch)**:

- Static-load test: suspend the rope and load it in steps (stones/water bags of known weight), holding each step for 1 minute, and record the breaking step. The breaking value divided by 5 is that batch's working-load ceiling; divide by 10 when lifting people.
- Impact test: drop a weight equal to half the static breaking load in free fall from 1 m onto a tensioned rope; surviving 10 drops qualifies it as a lifting sling.

[Card 07-03 Static-Load Stepping and Calibration] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: the rope batch to be calibrated (sampled from the batch); known-weight stones or water bags (stepped load); a fixed suspension point (crossbeam or tree limb) and slings.
2. **Tools required**: hanging cord, water bags and stone set (stepped ballast), wooden caliper or standard rod (to measure diameter), record board. Calibration source: load comes from known-weight stones or water bags (Chapter 3 weighing), not from eyeballing.
3. **Sequence and control points**: suspend the rope → load in steps → hold each step 1 minute → record the breaking step → divide by the safety factor for that batch's working load → impact test (half the breaking load, 1 m free fall, 10 drops). Control points: **every step must be held 1 minute** (loading fast misjudges); for lifting people divide by 10 instead.
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: safety factor — working load = breaking value ÷ 5, ÷ 10 for lifting people; the impact test drops half the static breaking load from 1 m, surviving 10 drops qualifies as a lifting sling; step increments keyed to the diameter class's empirical breaking magnitudes 30–60 / 80–150 / 200–350 / 500–800 kgf, to be overwritten by on-site measurement.
5. **Observations to record**: batch number / rope diameter / load and hold result at each step / breaking step / break location (rope body, splice, knot eye) / impact drops and result / calibrator and date.
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = the breaking step is not below that class's empirical magnitude and the rope survives 10 impact drops; rework = the break is at a splice — lengthen the overlap, stagger it, re-splice and recalibrate; scrap = specimens from one batch scatter widely in breaking step (batch inhomogeneity — the batch is not used for load-bearing).
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) breaking steps scatter within a batch → first check whether specimens came from the same splice section, then whether fiber batches were mixed; (2) breaks always at a knot eye → first check whether that knot passed the three load-bearing checks, then whether the rope surface is fuzzed (a fuzzed knot drops to 50% load); (3) the rope stretches visibly and will not recover → wet rope or over-twisted; dry it and retest; (4) it parts on the first impact drop → first check whether the drop weight exceeded half the breaking load, then whether the rope was wetted and dried again (strength already lost).
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = a stepped-loading record for one batch; L2 = one calibration table per diameter class stating the working load; L3 = the table in use for real hoisting, with a recorded working-load ceiling per batch and refusal above it.

**Basic knots (function-oriented)**:

| Knot | Function | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| Square knot | Joining two ropes of equal thickness | Both ends loaded together, no slip |
| Sheet bend | Joining ropes of different thickness | The thin rope wraps the thick and locks |
| Bowline | Fixed loop, no slip, no jam | Still untieable after loading |
| Clove hitch | Fast pole lashing | Tighter the harder it pulls; can be tied one-handed |
| Cross lashing | Binding two crossed poles (scaffolds, tool handles) | 3 turns each in the order horizontal–vertical–horizontal–vertical, then add frapping coils to cinch dead |

### 7.5 Wooden Wedges, Mortise-and-Tenon Joints, and Mechanical Connections

**Wooden wedges**:

- Stock: straight-grained hardwood (Mongolian oak, hawthorn), grain parallel to the load direction.
- Wedge face angle 8°–12°: too steep and vibration pops it out; too shallow and it splits the workpiece (empirical value; must be field-calibrated).
- Leave a 2–3 mm blunt edge at the head to prevent mushrooming when driving.
- Drive dry wedges into wet holes: as the hole dries and shrinks it locks the wedge dead — tighter with use.

**Mortise-and-tenon (pre-chisel-era techniques)**:

- Two ways to cut mortises: burn-chiseling — char it through locally with coals → scrape away the char layer with a stone flake → repeat to depth; drill-and-enlarge — the bow drill (7.6) drills a continuous row of holes along the mortise line → break through → scrape and true the walls (efficiency multiplies once tooled up in Chapter 9).
- Specifications: tenon width = 1/3–1/2 of the handle width; tenon length 2–3 times its width; fit a dry tenon into a wet mortise with 0.5–1 mm of interference (empirical value; must be field-calibrated); once the tenon protrudes through, drive in a cross-grain wedge to swell it dead (through-wedge lock).
- Three anti-crack rules: hole centers at least 2 hole-diameters from the end of the wood; no holes along split-sensitive directions; for large holes, burn a small hole first and enlarge stepwise.

**Joint lineage (strength and reversibility)**:

| Level | Joint | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Lashing (rope) | Fast, removable, needs periodic re-lashing |
| 2 | Wedge + lashing | Semi-permanent |
| 3 | Mortise-and-tenon + through wedge | Permanent, load-bearing in bending |
| 4 | Tar glue + wedge + lashing | Strongest; depends on 8.5 |

Acceptance: apply a static load of 1.5 times the working load to the joint and hold for 1 minute; displacement <2 mm with no cracking or noise passes (record working loads by use).

### 7.6 Simple Bow Drill and Rotary Tools

**Full bow drill parameter table**:

| Component | Specification | Material/source |
|---|---|---|
| Bow arm | Length 60–80 cm, straight-grained elastic wood; strung, the arc stands about 5 cm high | 7.1 species selection + 7.8 shaping |
| Bowstring | Rope diameter 4–6 mm, wraps the drill shaft 1.5–2 turns | 7.4 rope making |
| Drill shaft (for boring) | Length 20–25 cm, diameter 2–2.5 cm, round straight hardwood; stone drill point fitted at the end | Stone point = Chapter 5 pointed flake |
| Drill shaft (for fire lighting) | Diameter 2–2.5 cm softwood, rounded end | See 6.3 |
| Hearth board | See 6.3 | — |
| Bearing cap | Hardwood block or a stone with a socket; grease the socket with animal fat to reduce drag | Chapter 5 toolworking |

**Operating parameters**: stroke length 25–35 cm; 50–70 strokes per minute; speed about 200–400 rpm; downward force 5–10 kg (empirical values; must be field-calibrated). Solo operation: left hand presses the cap, right hand works the bow; two-handed cooperation (one presses, one saws the bow) for deep holes and hard stock.

**Boring procedure**:

- Wood: scribe a cross to mark the hole position (Chapter 3 datums) → the drill point starts a locating dimple → steady pressure, slow rotation → lift the drill to clear the chips every 20–30 seconds. A hole through 2 cm thick hardwood takes about 5–15 minutes (empirical value; must be field-calibrated).
- Deep-hole anti-runout: drill from both faces toward the middle; correct the meet-up deviation with the angle board.
- Bone and stone: switch to a hollow bone-tube/bamboo-tube bit + wet quartz-sand abrasive — grinding rather than cutting; see 9.2 for details.

**Fire-lighting reuse**: swap in a softwood shaft and a hearth board and it becomes the bow drill upgrade of 6.3, with a success rate and efficiency several times the hand drill's (empirical value).

**Maintenance**: restring as soon as the string loosens (wrap count unchanged); dulled drill point → retouch by pressure flaking (Chapter 5); bearing-cap socket worn deep and slipping → re-cut the cap face.

6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = the point traces a circle in free spin, holes do not skew, and a 2 cm hardwood hole is done in 5–15 minutes; rework = restring a slack string (wrap count unchanged), retouch a dull point by pressure flaking, re-cut a worn socket; scrap = the bow arm cracks or loses its spring — replace the stock.
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) the point slips without biting → first check whether the point is dull (retouch by pressure flaking), then whether downward force is short (the 5–10 kg grade), last whether the stock is too hard (change stock or switch to abrasive drilling); (2) holes skew → first check for a starting dimple, then whether the cap hand is adding lateral force, last whether the stock face is tilted; (3) the string keeps going slack → first check whether the wrap count changed, then whether the string is under 4–6 mm; (4) speed will not come up and it only smokes without cutting → first check whether the stroke is too short (25–35 cm), then whether the bearing cap lacks grease.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one assembled bow drill that bores one hole; L2 = 5 aligned holes in hardwood, drilled from two faces with deviation <2 mm; L3 = bow, string, shaft, and wrap count on file, and a second person reproduces a bow drill of the same speed from the file.

### 7.7 Levers, Pulleys, and Simple Load-Moving Devices

**Lever**: three elements — fulcrum, effort point, load point. Effort saved = distance from fulcrum to load ÷ distance from fulcrum to effort. Practice: when prying a heavy stone, the packing stone acts as the fulcrum and sits as close to the load as possible; the longer the effort arm, the more effort is saved, but the larger the stroke.

**Rollers**: place equal-diameter logs under the load and feed them in one by one from the front. The rolling friction coefficient is about 0.05–0.1 versus about 0.4–0.6 for sliding (empirical values) — effort saved 3–5 times. Key points: keep the path level; roller spacing 50–80 cm; fan the rollers out when turning.

**Inclined plane**: an earth ramp with a 1:4 grade saves effort down to 1/4 in theory (ignoring friction). Chock behind the load on the way up to prevent backsliding.

**Rope-and-pulley arrangements (limited by having no metal axles)**:

- Turning a line over a tree limb or smooth log: efficiency <50%, heavy rope wear; for direction changes only.
- Simple wooden pulley block: hardwood with a grooved wheel + wooden pin axle, efficiency about 60%–70% (empirical value; must be field-calibrated); a single movable pulley saves nearly half; a one-fixed-one-movable combination saves more than half, but descent speed and stroke double.
- Tripod hoisting: bind three equal-length hardwood legs at the top (cross lashing + through wedge) and hang the pulley set; used for loading carts, lowering into pits, and erecting members.

**Rafts and sledges**:

- Raft: buoyancy = displaced volume × water density. Assessment: dry wood density about 0.5–0.7 (empirical value); a log's displacement minus its own weight is the gross load capacity; halve that again for the safe load.
- Sledge: for hauling over snow and mud; hardwood runners underneath; lash the load with cross lashing.

All load-moving devices undergo 1.5-times static-load verification before use (method of 7.4).

### 7.8 How to Make Reusable Woodworking Tools

**First batch of composite-handled woodworking tools** (hafting uses joint lineage level 2 of 7.5: wedge + lashing; level 4 awaits the 8.5 glue):

| Tool | Edge | Haft angle | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stone adze | Ground stone edge (hornfels/basalt) or a thick flake | Edge to handle about 60°–75° | Transverse shaving, gouging channels |
| Stone axe | Bifacially knapped or ground | Edge line perpendicular to the handle axis | Felling, splitting rough stock |
| Wood chisel/graver | Small flake bound to a short handle, edge angle 30°–50° | In line with the handle | Gouging channels, truing holes; cutting and prying only — hammer strikes forbidden |
| Scraper/flat blade | Thick flake end edge 45°–60° | In line or slightly angled | Surfacing, bark removal |
| Wooden mallet | Large-diameter short hardwood section, ends flat | One piece | Driving wedges, truing shape; spares the edge |

Auxiliaries: marking gauge (hardwood point + charcoal stick for scribing); whetstone set (one coarse and one fine sandstone slab); plumb line and angle board (Chapter 3) carried with the toolbox.

**Grinding process** (the proper route for adze and axe edges): grind the blank on a coarse sandstone slab with water to remove excess → medium-grind to shape → polish with fine sand + leather. Keep the original edge angle constant throughout: consistent grinding-striation direction, parallel to the edge, is the evidence the angle has not changed. Grinding one stone axe takes 3–8 hours (empirical value; must be field-calibrated).

**Maintenance regime**:

1. Wipe clean after use and coat thinly with animal fat against moisture.
2. Re-sharpen as soon as the edge dulls, angle unchanged (checked against the grinding striations).
3. Re-lash immediately when lashings loosen — follow the standard lashing pattern (7.4 cross lashing); no temporary wrappings.
4. After 3–5 regrinds a stone-edged tool's blade is too thin and is scrapped; the edge blank is recycled for re-knapping (back to 5.5).

**Wear ledger**: for every tool, record manufacture date, labor hours, regrind count, and scrap date. Summarize the wear rate monthly to feed capacity planning in Section 9.5.

### 7.9 Knot Atlas: Ten Knots and the Three Load Checks

The five knots of 7.4 cover "joining and lashing"; this section completes the ten-knot atlas and gives a unified **slippage risk grading**: low = self-locks even under pulsating loads and vibration, may be left unattended overnight; medium = self-locks under constant load but backs off under pulsating loads, must be checked the same day; high = for temporary positioning or attended use only, untie immediately after use.

| Knot | One-line tying method | Use | Slippage risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clove hitch | Wrap the rope twice around the pole, the second wrap trapping the first wrap's end | Lashing starting point on poles; opening stage of the tripod top joint | Medium (stable under constant load, backs off under pulsating load; finish with a half hitch) |
| Figure-eight knot | Wrap the rope end around the standing part into a figure "8" and pass back through | Rope-end stopper against pull-through (safety end before threading holes) | Low (tightens the harder it pulls; the cost is being hard to untie after loading) |
| Rolling hitch | The short rope takes three snug turns around the standing rope facing the load side | A temporary power-grip handle on a tensioned rope (rope-tightening work) | Medium (rigged backwards it slips: the wrapped face must face the load direction) |
| Bowline | The rope end goes up through to form a loop, around the back of the standing part, and down back into the loop | Fixed loop: lifting people, around stakes, starting netting | Low (no slip, no jam, untieable; tuck the tail back through into a double bowline to finish) |
| Sheet bend | The thin rope end passes through the thick rope's bight, folds back around the bight's bottom and traps itself | Joining a thick and a thin rope | Medium (thin-end tail ≥10 cm; tie it doubled at critical points) |
| Double fisherman's knot | The two rope ends each wrap the other's standing part twice, then pull against each other to tighten | Permanent joining of equal thin ropes; closing loops | Low (extremely self-locking; cannot be untied after loading — permanent joins only) |
| Sheer lashing | With two poles side by side, the rope takes several connected turns around both, then fraps tight horizontally between the poles to close | A-frames, shears legs (set the angle before closing) | Medium (the close-off backs off under vibration; re-tighten daily) |
| Carrying-pole lift hitch | Once the load sling loop is set, the carrying pole's end passes through the loop, traps the sling root, and takes one more back turn | Two-person carrying: joining the carrying pole to the load sling | Low (vertical load presses it ever tighter; must be re-closed after setting the load down) |
| Ladder hitch | The short rope wraps the standing rope one turn and passes through to form a self-gripping loop; rungs are lashed horizontally between two loops | Rope-ladder rung fixing | Medium (slips unloaded: re-tighten rung by rung before climbing) |
| Anchor hitch | The rope end takes two turns around the ring or post, finished with a half hitch | Fixed ends between rope and rings, posts, anchors | Low (self-locks under constant load; switch to three turns for stiff, thick rope) |

**Three checks for load-bearing knots** (any knot that will take a load must be checked after tying; miss one, do not use):

1. **Check the load direction**: does the knot's locking face square with the actual pull direction? A rolling hitch or sheet bend rigged backwards is as good as untied; the bowline's loaded side must be the standing part, not the end.
2. **Check the finish**: tail ≥10 cm; required re-threading done and required half hitches added; a figure-eight knot pre-tied on a rope end must not be stripped for convenience.
3. **Check the wear point**: pinch the knot's eye (the tightest bend) and twist-inspect — fuzzing = outer fibers cut; a cut-through feel = the core is about to fail. A fuzzed knot drops to 50% load; a cut-feel knot is retied at a new spot immediately.

◎ Hand-feel marker: whether the finish is tight enough is judged not by eye but by "two fingers clamping the tail cannot pull it out, and a knuckle tap on the knot gives a muffled sound." Practice method: everyone ties each of the ten knots 20 times with 3 mm rope (about 2 hours total, empirical value), then ties each 5 times blindfolded; thereafter, each week 3 knots are drawn at random and re-tied against the clock, with scores entered in the rope ledger.

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| Input materials | Calibrated rope (7.4, any of the 3–10 mm classes); wooden poles or ring posts; practice usage about 50 cm of rope per knot per run |
| Tools required | Bare hands for the most part; stone-flake knife for cutting rope (Chapter 5); known-weight stones for verification (Chapter 3 weight records) |
| Operation sequence and control points | Tie per the atlas → **run the three checks for load-bearing knots** → register item by item. Control points: failing either the load direction or the finish forbids hanging any load |
| Empirical parameters and calibration | Tail ≥10 cm; calibration: run 3 pull-to-failure tests per knot type and record "slippage load ÷ breaking load"; a knot type with a ratio <0.7 is banned for that use (empirical criterion; must be field-calibrated) |
| In-process observation items | Knot type, rope diameter, load step, slip amount (mm), fiber state at the knot eye; enter in the rope ledger |
| Pass / rework / scrap | Displacement <5 mm under working load with no slip = pass; slips then re-locks = reworkable (add a half hitch or retie doubled); slips out = that knot type is scrapped for that use |
| Common failures and check order | Slip: (1) check whether the load direction is rigged backwards (2) check whether the finish lacks a half hitch (3) check whether the rope surface is too slick (newly twisted rope lacks fuzz; pre-fuzz it before use) |
| L1/L2/L3 evidence | L1: one demonstration of each of the ten knots; L2: 3 pull-test records per knot on file; L3: knot-type vs. use ledger + quarterly retraining records |

[Card 07-05 Knot Atlas] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: calibrated rope (7.4, any of the 3–10 mm classes, about 50 cm per knot per practice run); wooden poles, rings, stakes to tie onto.
2. **Tools required**: bare hands for the most part; stone-flake knife for cutting rope (Chapter 5); known-weight stones (for the slippage-load ratio calibration). Calibration source: diameter and breaking load from the 07-03 calibration table; load applied in known-weight stone steps.
3. **Sequence and control points**: tie per the atlas → run the **three checks for load-bearing knots** (load direction, finish, wear point) → register item by item in the ledger. Control points: failing either the **load direction** or the **finish** check forbids hanging any load.
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: tail ≥10 cm; run 3 pull-to-failure tests per knot type and record "slippage load ÷ breaking load"; a knot type with a ratio <0.7 is banned for that use (empirical criterion — must be calibrated to local conditions); practice load: each of the ten knots 20 times with 3 mm rope (about 2 hours), then 5 times blindfolded, thereafter 3 knots drawn at random each week against the clock.
5. **Observations to record**: knot type / rope diameter / load step / slip amount (mm) / fiber state at the knot eye (fuzzed or cut-feel) / tier and date.
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = displacement <5 mm under working load with no slip-out; rework = it slips then re-locks (add a half hitch or retie it doubled); scrap = it slips out — that knot type is scrapped for that use; change knot type.
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) it slips → first check whether the load direction is rigged backwards (a rolling hitch or sheet bend rigged backwards is as good as untied), then whether the finish lacks a half hitch or the tail is under 10 cm, last whether the rope surface is too slick (newly twisted rope lacks fuzz; pre-fuzz it); (2) it cannot be untied after loading → that knot belongs to permanent joins only (double fisherman's type); switch to an untieable knot; (3) the knot eye fuzzes → first check whether the tightest bend is taking the load (move the knot), fuzzed knots drop to 50% load; (4) slip amounts differ widely between two runs → first check whether the rope is the same diameter class, then whether the tying followed the atlas step by step.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one demonstration of each of the ten knots; L2 = 3 pull-test records per knot on file; L3 = a knot-type vs. use ledger plus quarterly retraining records.

### 7.10 Net and Mat Weaving

**Starting a net**: first tie a row of base loops on the spreader bar (bowlines, loop spacing = one mesh size); the loop count is the net's mesh count across the width. Weave row by row: take the cord and tie two consecutive half hitches around the two mesh posts of each mesh in the row above and tighten (interlocking knot); rows interlock into a sheet.

**Decreasing and increasing meshes row by row**:

- Decrease (narrowing the net): at the row end, combine two adjacent meshes of the row above into one and tie a single knot.
- Increase (widening the net): tie two knots on the same mesh post.
- Rule: the number of decreases and increases per row must be left-right symmetric, or the net draws up skewed (the whole sheet twists into a trapezoid).

**Mesh gauge (sizing board)**: one hardwood board, width = mesh side length × 2, thickness 3–5 mm, edges rounded off. Cast the starting loops around the board so every mesh comes out the same width; mesh tolerance is judged by "the gauge slides through the full row with uniform snugness." Fishing-net mesh 30–50 mm (start from half the target fish's body height; one live fishing run calibrates it); cargo-net mesh 80–120 mm (sized so cargo cannot slip out).

◎ Weaving-tension hand feel: tighten each interlocking knot to the degree that "mesh posts do not twist and the gauge slides in and out without binding." Practice method: weave three 10 cm × 10 cm test pieces first; when the third matches the first row by row in mesh count, you have graduated (empirical value; must be field-calibrated). Misjudgment compensation: run the gauge through the full row — rows it will not pass are too tight and rows it falls through freely are too loose; correct the hand-feel record against the gauge results.

| Item | Specification | Labor hours (empirical value; must be field-calibrated) | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fishing net | 1 m², mesh 30–50 mm, 3 mm bast rope | 6–10 h | Blocking streams, drag fishing |
| Cargo net | 1.5 m × 1.5 m, mesh 80–120 mm, 6 mm rope | 8–12 h | Hoisting and load binding, hanging storage |
| Grass mat (plain weave) | 1.8 m × 0.9 m | 3–5 h | Sleeping mat, underlay |
| Grass mat (twill) | Same as above | 4–6 h | Rain cover (twill is denser and seeps more slowly) |

**Two mat weaves**: plain weave alternates one-over, one-under — fast, flat surface; twill advances two-over, two-under in offset rows — diagonal texture, denser weave. Pound the grass soft and comb it straight first (7.3 processing); press each column tight as you weave — "finish a column, press a column."

**Selecting cord**: nets use bast rope that does not lose wet strength (linden/paper-mulberry inner bark; 7.4 wet-strength table); mats can use dry grass, but it must be stored hung dry to prevent mildew.

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| Input materials | 3–6 mm bast rope (wet-strength-retaining type, 7.4); one spreader bar; stock estimate = net area × mesh density plus a 15% allowance |
| Tools required | Mesh gauge (self-made in this section); stone-flake knife for cutting rope (Chapter 5); verification: the gauge passes through the full row |

| In-process observation items | Mesh count per row, tension hand feel (◎ criterion), rope batch number; stop the row for inspection on any mismatch |
| Pass / rework / scrap | Mesh deviation <10% with no skipped knots = pass; two consecutive rows deviating the same way = rework (unravel two rows, reweave, and re-check the gauge); rope stock over-retted and brittle (snaps when pulled, 7.4 criterion) = scrap and replace the stock |

[Card 07-06 Net Weaving] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: 3–6 mm bast rope (wet-strength-retaining type: linden/mulberry inner bark); one spreader bar; stock = net area × mesh density plus a 15% allowance.
2. **Tools required**: mesh gauge (hardwood board, width = mesh side × 2, thickness 3–5 mm, edges rounded, self-made); stone-flake knife for cutting rope (Chapter 5). Calibration source: mesh size = gauge width ÷ 2; wet swelling passes as long as the gauge is not jammed.
3. **Sequence and control points**: cast a row of base loops on the spreader bar (bowlines, spacing = one mesh) → interlocking knots row by row (two consecutive half hitches around the two mesh posts of each mesh, tightened) → decrease/increase meshes per plan (**left-right symmetry is mandatory**) → finish edges with three tucked turns. Control point: count the meshes as each row completes; a miscount unravels that row.
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: mesh size = gauge width ÷ 2; fishing net 30–50 mm (start from half the target fish's body height, calibrated by one live fishing run); cargo net 80–120 mm; mesh deviation <10% passes; weave three 10 cm × 10 cm test pieces first, and graduating means the third matches the first row by row in mesh count (empirical value — must be calibrated to local conditions); labor 1 m² fishing net 6–10 h, 1.5 m × 1.5 m cargo net 8–12 h.
5. **Observations to record**: mesh count per row / mesh size (gauge full-row test) / tension hand feel (◎ mesh posts untwisted, gauge neither binding nor slack) / rope batch number / mesh re-measured wet.
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = mesh deviation <10% with no skipped knots; rework = two consecutive rows deviating the same way — unravel two rows, reweave, re-check the gauge; scrap = rope stock over-retted and brittle (snaps when pulled) — replace the stock.
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) the net draws skewed into a trapezoid → first check whether decreases/increases are piled on one side (they must be left-right symmetric), then whether the interlocking knots vary in tightness, last whether the gauge has worn narrow; (2) mesh size wanders → first check whether every starting loop was cast on the same gauge board, then whether wet rope was re-measured (wet swelling); (3) a skipped knot inside a row → add it when counting that row; never carry it into the next row; (4) the cord snaps mid-weave → first check for over-retted stock (switch to linden/mulberry inner bark), then whether the diameter is under 3 mm.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = a 10 cm × 10 cm test piece kept as a reference; L2 = one fishing net survives one launch without losing meshes; L3 = net output, losses, and wet-strength spot checks on the ledger.

### 7.11 Splitting Strips and Rattan-Bamboo Work

**Fixed-blade strip-splitting method**: stand the bamboo tube upright on the ground with a shallow cross kerf pre-cut in the end face; set the blade (stone axe edge or thick-backed flake) into one of the kerf lines; from then on **the blade position does not move** — press down on the blade spine with both hands or strike the spine with the wooden mallet, letting the tube's own weight and the strikes drive it downward in one split to the end. The fixed blade is the key to equal-width strips: move the blade midway and the strips are sure to come out uneven.

**Splitting order**: first split crosswise into four blocks (through the pith) → split each block into strips → scrape the inner diaphragms clean (diaphragms are hard and brittle; left in, strips snap mid-weave).

**Strip grading**:

| Grade | Position | Characteristics | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Green strip | The outer layer with the green skin | High strength, over 2 times the yellow strip's (empirical comparative; calibration: hang weights on equal-width strip pairs and compare breaking steps) | Load-bearing warp strips, basket bottoms, bending members |
| Yellow strip | Inner layer | Low strength, soft | Weft winding, filling woven walls |

Strip width specifications: warp strips 6–8 mm with faces free of split wounds (a wounded strip is a future break point); weft strips 3–5 mm. Moisten dry strips in water for about 10 minutes before weaving; the brittle-snap rate drops markedly (empirical value; must be field-calibrated).

**Rattan working**:

- Straightening: bake bent sections near the fire until hot to the touch (same fire-straightening method as Chapter 6), press straight, and cool naturally; twice per spot is the limit — a third baking scorches the rattan skin and it is discarded.
- T-joint: the horizontal bar's split end clamps the upright; close with cross lashing; used for frame tops and tool racks.
- Splicing (lengthening): split each rattan end into three strands; interweave the strands into one another; whip the outside with fine cord to close. Joint strength is about 60%–70% of the parent stock (empirical value; must be field-calibrated: hang loads on spliced and whole sections and compare breaking); critical load points must avoid joint positions.

◎ Strip-splitting blade hand feel: the along-the-grain criterion is "the blade spine sinks evenly and the sound runs continuous without popping"; a popping sound = hitting a node or going against the grain — stop and withdraw immediately and re-open the kerf. Practice method: split 20 strokes on a scrap bamboo tube; ≥80% equal-width strips means graduation (empirical value). Misjudgment compensation: compare strips two by two, side by side; pull out any with a width deviation >2 mm for re-splitting or demote them to weft strips.

### 7.12 Rope Bridges and Hoisting

**Twin-cable suspension bridge** (design span limit 10 m, empirical value; must be field-calibrated):

- Structure: 1 main cable (10–16 mm, passing static-load calibration) + 2 handrail cables (6–10 mm) + the tread set.
- Main cable sag: 8%–12% of the span (empirical value; must be field-calibrated) — too tight and the anchor-point loads run to several times the bridge load; too loose and every step is a trap. After tensioning, verify by "hanging 75 kg at mid-span and the extra sag stays <50 cm."
- Anchoring: wrap the main cable three turns around a living trunk + anchor hitch (7.9), or bury ground stakes (hardwood ≥15 cm in diameter, buried ≥1 m deep, with a cross-bar on top of the stake). Handrail cables anchor at chest height at both ends.
- Tread lashing: short timbers (40–50 cm long, 6–8 cm diameter) hang from the main cable with clove hitches, spaced 30–35 cm, each tread end given a half hitch against sliding; every 3–4 treads, run a hanger rope from a handrail cable diagonally to the main cable, forming triangles against sway.
- Verification: before carrying people, hang 1.5 times the rated load (water-bag ballast) and let it stand 10 minutes; anchor displacement <5 cm passes; thereafter inspect the anchor hitches and tread half hitches weekly.

**Three-point hoisting**:

- Center-of-gravity judgment: the test lift is the measurement — once the sling lifts the object, it rotates until the center of gravity hangs directly below; if it tilts, set it back down, shift the sling loops, and try again until it lifts level.
- Sling layout: two-point slings + one top point make three points. With the two legs at an angle <90°, each leg carries <0.75 times the weight; at 120°, each leg carries the full weight; **lifting is forbidden at angles >120°** (empirical criterion; measure on site with the Chapter 3 angle board).
- Test-lift procedure: lift 10 cm off the ground and hold 1 minute → observe the tilt and the slip at every knot → set down and adjust → second test lift, level → formal hoist. No one stands below at any point during the lift; lifting and lowering follow the called-command system of this chapter's safety boundaries.

**Stringing a rope across a river (leader-line method)**:

1. Tie a stone or a piece of floating wood to the fine leader line's end (3 mm rope), throw it across, or carry it while swimming over.
2. Pull the leader line taut across the river and anchor it temporarily on the far bank.
3. The leader line drags the middle rope (6 mm), and the middle rope drags the main cable (10–16 mm) — **scale up step by step, each step a rope-diameter ratio of about 1:3**; if any step will not budge, stop and check for snagging; throwing the main cable across directly is forbidden.
4. Anchor and tension the main cable to the twin-cable bridge specification; run the 1.5-times static-load verification first, then use it as a crossing bridge or a traverse line.

| Symptom | Candidate causes (by probability) | Remedy |
|---|---|---|
| Rope will not hold together, loose | Insufficient twist / uneven strand thickness | Add twist and re-ply; match the thicknesses |

## C. Unresolved Dependencies

- No adhesives: the haft-strength ceiling is limited by lashing (tar glue in 8.5 completes it).

## Safety Boundaries

- When splitting, no one within 3 m of the wedge ejection direction; follow-up wedges must be driven in one by one in sequence — hammering a single wedge hard is forbidden.
- Retting water carries putrefactive bacteria: wash hands after processing fiber and keep wounds away from retting water.
- The bow drill string wrapping around fingers is a major injury source: string length and bow grip follow the 7.6 specifications.
- Tripods, pulley sets, and rafts must pass 1.5-times static-load verification before use; rafts carrying people run at half load.
- Swing the stone axe through empty air and confirm no one is behind before working (called-command system, see 9.5).
