## Capability Boundary

**What can be done:**

- Control engineering for the six exposure source classes: wet work, enclosure and off-gas absorption, ventilation and wind-direction positioning, site isolation, shift time limits—all implemented with materials already in this book.
- The occupational exposure registry system: per-post files with four fields (post / exposure source / weekly hours / protection status), connected to Chapter 51's record discipline; the annual examination observation items table (nails, gums, wrist, tremor, breathing, hearing, skin, body weight—all observable, no instrument dependence).
- Disposal chains for the four waste categories: on-site classification, mutual neutralization of the two wastes, the three-bucket system and deep-burial records for heavy-metal residue, staged wastewater settling, wet stacking and covering of tailings.
- The acute poisoning field response sequence plus mercury-spill and acid-spill emergency plans, including an annual drill system.

**What cannot be done (declared honestly):**

- **Definitive medical diagnosis of occupational disease.** Blood lead, urinary mercury, urinary arsenic, lung function, audiometry—none of it can be measured (the Chapter G01 exclusion table). The "detection" in this chapter has only two steps: symptom and sign registration + removal from exposure. On finding wrist-drop, the response is "transfer out of the lead post + register + recheck," not saying "he has lead poisoning," let alone treating it. The boundary of diagnostic language is exactly the same as Chapter 51: write only syndromes and findings, never disease names.
- **Occupational medicine treatment.** This chapter has no antidote or treatment means of any kind; specific antidotes are on the C list. The medical destination for those with abnormal signs is Chapter 48 (emergencies) and Chapter 50 (symptomatic care and dose discipline); what this chapter governs is "no further exposure."
- **Quantification of chronic damage.** We do not know how much poison has accumulated in a given craftsman's body—we can only register exposure hours and annual-examination signs as a proxy curve. This curve is a trend dashboard (same sense as Section 47.7), not an individual diagnosis sheet.
- Continuous monitoring of fugitive exhaust emissions and chemical testing of slag-yard leachate—only the salt tracer method (Chapter 45) can judge connectivity, and pin maps (Chapter 47) can show clustering.

**External dependencies (counted on the C list):** an occupational medicine and toxicology testing system (blood and urine quantification, lung function and hearing measurement); qualified respiratory protection equipment (filter dust masks, acid-gas masks); specific antidotes; water testing for heavy metals. Recovery conditions match the Chapters 44–46 boundary—**[Background/Outlook] Occupational medicine**: on the day the microscope, aseptic technique, and quantitative chemistry return, this chapter's exposure registry and annual-examination sign tables become a ready-made data mother-lode for that generation of medics—first the cohort with exposure histories, then the diagnoses with disease names; the reverse order can never answer "whose disease came from which post."

## 49.1 Acute Injury Kills a Person; Chronic Damage Kills a Trade

### 49.1.1 The Fundamental Difference Between the Two Classes of Injury

The safety boundaries of the preceding fifty-two chapters were almost all aimed at acute injury: burns, cuts, flying stock, explosions, drowning—injury on the spot, cause and effect before the eyes, pain itself the alarm. Acute injury kills one person, and the settlement reacts immediately. Chronic exposure is a different thing; the contrast is fixed as follows:

| Dimension | Acute injury | Chronic exposure damage |
|---|---|---|
| Speed of visibility | On the spot to same day | Three to twenty years |
| Cause and effect | Everyone can see it (the hammer hits the hand) | Impossible to pin down (dust inhaled fifteen years ago) |
| Pain alarm | Violent pain, instinctive avoidance | **No pain**—the lungs do not cry out; the nerves are slowly ruined |
| Attribution of responsibility | The person involved bears a share | Often written off as "getting old" or "bad luck" |
| Reversibility | Most heal | Silicosis, lead encephalopathy, mercury tremor, arsenic neuropathy, hearing loss—**essentially irreversible** |
| What is killed | One person | **One trade**: the old master falls, and the craft and the master–apprentice line (Chapter 43) break with him |

The last line is the reason this chapter exists. When a settlement loses ten able-bodied craftsmen, what is lost is not ten lives converted into work points (Chapter 37 standard), but the eyes that read fire color at the furnace, the hands that feel forging heat, the rhythm of drawing glass—once these unledgerable things break, reconnecting takes a generation. **The review said "chronic damage is what actually kills craftsmen on a twenty-year scale"; this chapter engineers that sentence: the failure of chronic-exposure control is not written in accident records, it is written in the empty cells of the skill matrix twenty years later.**

### 49.1.2 Three Features of Chronic Damage: Silent, Cumulative, Irreversible

1. **Silent.** None of the routes poisons take into the body carry an alarm: silica dust goes unnoticed until it chokes one into coughing blood; lead passes hand to mouth without taste; mercury vapor is colorless and odorless (Chapter 33's red-line wording); arsenic's garlic odor is smellable only at high concentrations; CO is colorless and odorless (collapsing at the furnace face is often the first signal); noise-induced hearing loss first takes the high frequencies—voices merely sound "a bit muffled," without pain. **There is only one countermeasure: do not wait for the body to sound the alarm; make the paper sound it—this is the entire rationale of the Section 49.3 registry system.**
2. **Cumulative.** Dose stacks linearly with work years: one mercury spill is not fatal; spill once a month with part of it evaporating each time, and after three years the air of the work room is itself the poison source; one day of ore-breaking "toughed out" passes, but ten years of it is a fibrotic lung. **Countermeasures: register exposure by the hour, set tenure caps and rotation by work years (Section 49.3.4), keep the toxin account by the income-outlay difference (Section 49.4.3).**
3. **Irreversible.** Silica dust deposited in the lungs cannot be taken out; dead nerves do not grow back; lost hair cells (hearing) do not regenerate. An acute injury, once healed, can return to post; for a person positive for chronic damage, the only effective response is **transfer while he is still healthy**—which is why this chapter writes "transfer" as a process step, not a personnel arrangement.

## 49.2 The Six Exposure Sources, Source by Source

Each source is written in a fixed six items: where it comes from / acute manifestations / chronic manifestations / engineering controls / personal protection / exposure registry points. **Reading rule: engineering controls always come before personal protection—dust that wet methods can eliminate is not carried on a mask; slag that can be isolated is not dodged by people. Personal protection is the last layer, never the first.**

### 49.2.1 Dust (Silica Dust)

**Where it comes from:** Mining and ore crushing (Chapter 16), dry screening in ore dressing, dry grinding of abrasives and pigments, casting-cleanup sandblasting (dry sand blasted at the surface), dry mixing of ceramic glaze materials (Chapter 14), stone cutting and chiseling. A rough standard for identifying "high-hazard dust": **rock-class dust (quartz-type, ore-vein country rock) is far more toxic than soil-class dust**—dust containing free silica (ore veins, sandstone, flint) is the source of silicosis; wood shavings and grain dust are not.

**Acute manifestations:** Same-day irritant dry cough, sputum, itchy eyes, nose, and throat—these are the body self-cleaning, not the injury itself; the real injury is depositing silently.

**Chronic manifestations (silicosis, by observable stages):**

| Stage | Observable criterion (just follow it; no instruments needed) |
|---|---|
| Early | Morning cough with sputum habitual for over a month; **unable to speak in full sentences after 100 brisk steps on flat ground** (the sentence-speaking test, same origin as Chapter 47's three signs of shortness of breath); monthly weight loss <5% |
| Middle | Must stop midway on the slope-walk test (about 20 steps of steep slope); large morning sputum volume; short of breath after just 100 brisk steps |
| Late | Wheezing at rest, sputum with black in it, obvious wasting—register and care for per Section 47.3.4 chronic wasting lung disease |

**Differential interface (to Chapter 51):** When registering a Section 47.3.4 chronic wasting lung disease (consumption-like) case, **the case's dust work-years file must be checked**—those with ten or more years of dry crushing / dry grinding work years are first registered as silicosis and immediately removed from dust posts, then registered and managed as consumption-like; the two diseases are handled differently: consumption requires infection control, silicosis requires exposure cutoff.

**Engineering controls (four items, ordered by effectiveness):**

1. **Wet work**—the most effective item. Change ore breaking, screening, and dry grinding to wet grinding: sprinkle the work surface with water until soaked through. ◎Water-quantity hand-feel criterion: grab a handful of material and squeeze it into a ball that holds, does not fall apart on dropping, and **no dust cloud visible** where the hammer lands—then it is wet enough. Practice method: on the waste-rock pile run a dry-vs-wet comparison of 20 hammer blows each and observe the dust plume under the hammer; misjudgment compensation: take "floating dust still visible in the air one breath after the hammer lands" as failing—sprinkle again; decide not by hand feel but by the dust plume.
2. **Enclosure and settling**—processes that truly need to be dry (such as dry screening) get three-sided baffles with the opening downwind; dust travels upward: **open high windows to exhaust dust, take in air low** (same standard as Section 45.6.1's low-in-high-out); workstations are not placed in the falling path of the dust plume.
3. **Wind-direction position**—open-air crushing points are placed at the settlement's downwind end per the prevailing wind, at ≥100 paces from dwellings and the cookhouse (same class as the arsenic site, see Section 49.2.4).
4. **Workstation layout onto drawings**—the three items above are completed into a **workstation plan** (Chapter 42): dusty workstations, wind-direction arrows, and spacing dimensions drawn clearly; new workshops are accepted against the drawing, not on a craftsman's "find an empty spot and start hammering."

**Personal protection:** Multiple layers of wet cloth over mouth and nose. **Honest declaration: wet cloth blocks only coarse dust, not respirable fine dust—it is the last layer, not a permit.** For dry work continuing while wet work is down, work hours are halved and registered. After work, wash hands and face and change out of work clothes before eating or returning to the residential area (same discipline as lead posts, below).

**Exposure registry points:** Per post, weekly "dry-work hours" (target is zero; if any, record the true number); the annual-examination slope-walk test and morning-sputum records go into the examination file (Section 49.3.3).

### 49.2.2 Lead

**Where it comes from:** Galena smelting (Chapters 16–17), lead-copper alloy (Section 17.8 route three), lead-glaze pottery (Chapter 14 glaze materials), lead-tin solder, lead-white pigment (paints), lead ware casting and repair. Lead's **main route into the body is hand-to-mouth**: lead dust on the hands enters with food, smoking, or licked fingers—the existence of this route determines that most of the control measures below are hygiene institutions rather than masks.

**Acute manifestations:** Lead colic—paroxysmal cramping pain around the navel, constipation for days, nausea; a metallic-sweet taste in the mouth. **Chronic manifestations (four signs at the annual examination, all observable):**

| Sign | How to check (criterion) |
|---|---|
| Wrist-drop | Both arms extended straight forward, palms down, held for 30 breaths (about 1 min): if the wrist cannot stay flat and the fingertips hang drooping = positive (early radial nerve palsy) |
| Gum lead line | Lift the upper lip and look in a mirror at the gum line: a blue-black line-like deposit = positive (more visible with poor oral hygiene) |
| Anemic earthy-gray complexion | Gray-earth face, pale palpebral conjunctiva, easy fatigue—compare against same-village same-age people, not on a single impression |
| Mees' lines | White transverse lines on the nails, appearing at the same height on multiple nails (more typical of arsenic, see Section 49.2.4; also seen in lead) = positive |

**Engineering controls:** ① A high window or draft hood above the lead-melting pot, so lead fume is drawn upward; ② **never overheat pouring**—a smoking lead pot is overheated; reduce the blast and cool it; smoke is the visible proxy of lead vapor; ③ lead powder and lead white are always ground wet (same method as Section 49.2.1); ④ lead work areas are separately enclosed, children barred, livestock excluded.

**Personal protection and hygiene system (the main force for this source):** ① **Soap washing** before shifts, before meals, and after the toilet (Chapter 45 soap); ② **no smoking, no eating, no snacks in the work area**—written into the workshop wall rules; ③ dedicated work clothes, **never worn back to the residential area**, with a changing spot at the work-area door; ④ leather gloves with cloth liners (Chapter 15), replaced as soon as torn.

**Two hard red lines:** ① **Pregnant women are barred from the post**, and lead-post rotation for women of childbearing age is strict—lead crosses the placenta and the milk to damage the offspring's brain development, harming a child no one can see; ② children are barred from lead work areas (children's hand-to-mouth frequency and absorption rate are higher than adults'; the same exposure dose harms them several times over).

**Exposure registry points:** Weekly lead-melting hours; spot checks of the hand-washing system (the foreman randomly checks 3 people's after-work hands each week, entered into the file); the four annual-examination signs go into the examination file.

### 49.2.3 Mercury

**Where it comes from:** All mercury operations of the Chapter 33 electronic-device line—mercury displacement pump evacuation, McLeod gauge readings (raising and lowering mercury), the mercury storage jar, plus leftover mercury from starting point B; upstream is cinnabar roasting and distillation to make mercury (Chapters 16, 12). **Characteristic: mercury evaporates continuously at room temperature, and the vapor is colorless, odorless, and unalarming—an open cup of mercury is an exposure source running day and night.** Chapter 33 has already set the mercury-discipline red lines; this chapter folds them into the six-source system and adds the two missing rings, examination and waste.

**Acute manifestations:** When mercury is heated, or spilled and unrecovered: same-day choking cough, chest tightness, fever—a chemical-pneumonia-like picture; report to Chapter 48 the same day and leave the mercury room.

**Chronic manifestations (three signs at the annual examination):**

| Sign | How to check (criterion) |
|---|---|
| Intention tremor | **Water-bowl test**: carry a full bowl of water level for ten steps; spilling beyond a rim-width ring = positive; or write your own name three times and compare stroke wobble against last year's specimen |
| Personality change | Family inquiry (fixed question: "This year, are you worse than last year in irritability, insomnia, and causeless timidity?")—a subjective item, used as a hint only, never a verdict |
| Gingivitis with drooling | Red swollen gums that bleed easily, much saliva—distinguish from the lead line: mercury is inflammatory red, lead is a deposited blue-black line |

**[Background]** Pre-industrial fur felting used mercury nitrate; "the mad hatter" (tremor and great personality change) is a historical record of an entire trade poisoned—a trade can be collectively broken by one process line using mercury. The mechanism cannot be verified on this route, but the engineering implications are enforced as usual.

**Engineering controls:** ① **Shallow-tray work**—all mercury operations are performed inside a large shallow tray; any spill lands in the tray (Chapter 33's original red line); ② **a dedicated mercury room**—mercury work shares no building with food and lodging, assembly, or the cookhouse; single room, single door, high cross-ventilation window; ③ **sealed storage**—the mercury storage jar is kept tightly closed with a layer of water over the mercury face as a seal (the water interrupts evaporation); close it as soon as use ends; ④ the McLeod gauge's raising and lowering mechanism moves slowly, preventing mercury impact and splashing (Chapter 33).

**Personal protection and discipline (Chapter 33's mercury discipline incorporated in full):** soap washing after operations; no eating, drinking, or smoking in the mercury work area; work clothes never carried out; **spilled mercury is immediately covered with sulfur powder and collected** (full emergency text in Section 49.6 and Card 49-03).

**Toxin account interface (Chapter 38):** reconcile the mercury balance monthly: last month's stock + newly made (or newly drawn) − current stock − amount deep-buried = **loss**. Empirical criterion: if loss > 2× the monthly ledger average, check floor seams, table seams, and jar seals—**the mercury account is the exposure account: mercury missing from the books has all gone into the air.** (Chapter 33 standard: each electron tube consumes 5–20 g of mercury, entered item by item.)

**Exposure registry points:** weekly mercury-operation hours, number of spills and recovered amounts (item by item), monthly loss.

### 49.2.4 Arsenic

**Where it comes from:** the arsenic-copper line (Section 17.8 route two: arsenopyrite / arsenical copper ore reduced with copper ore in one furnace); the more hidden source is **companion arsenic**—copper, tin, and gold ores often occur together with arsenic minerals; when dressing separation is incomplete (Chapter 16), arsenic follows the concentrate into the furnace, and the roasting fumes contain arsenic. **This chapter adds one universal rule: at any new ore site's first roast, judge the fumes by smelling for garlic odor 100 paces downwind; any garlic odor means manage it as an arsenic ore**—before you confirm the ore's name, defend against the worst case (same standard as Chapter 45's general rule).

**Acute manifestations:** garlic odor on the breath and in the fumes (observable, smellable); after exposure, vomiting, diarrhea, cramping abdominal pain.

**Chronic manifestations (three signs at the annual examination):**

| Sign | How to check (criterion) |
|---|---|
| Mees' lines | **Monthly nail check**: white transverse lines on the nails move outward as the nail grows; appearing at the same height on multiple nails = positive (arsenic's most typical sign)—on appearance, **permanent transfer out of arsenic work** (Section 17.8 original text) |
| Palmoplantar keratosis | Feel and press palms and soles: callus-like spots and punctate bumps beyond the normal callus zone = register |
| Peripheral neuropathy | Questioning + examination: stocking-distribution numbness of hands and feet (a wooden feeling in the sock-wearing area); compare pinprick perception of the instep against the forearm = register |
| Pigmented spots | Raindrop-like dark spots on the skin, mostly on the trunk = register and trace for the record (the Chapter 42 drawing method can trace and keep them) |

**Engineering controls:** ① **A dedicated site**—the arsenic roasting and pouring site sits **≥100 paces downwind** of the residential area (Section 17.8 original text), exclusive, never mixed with other trades; ② the roasting shed opens on three sides toward the downwind side, fumes venting straight up high, not pooling; ③ dedicated people, dedicated clothes, dedicated tools, **no sharing work clothes with the copper shop**; ④ enhanced dressing of companion-arsenic ores (Chapter 16 water separation) cuts arsenic at the source.

**Hygiene system (Section 17.8 original text incorporated):** after roasting, **wash clothes and skin**; after operations, do not directly touch food or children; work clothes never carried out.

**Exposure registry points:** roasting batches and hours; the monthly nail-check record page (each month record "no transverse lines" or "transverse lines, which nails, where").

### 49.2.5 Acid Mist and Chemical Fumes

**Where it comes from:** the green-vitriol dry-distillation sulfuric acid line, the nitric acid retort, the hydrochloric acid three-jar absorption line (Chapter 23); distillation and chlorine work (Chapter 26); metal pickling, bluing, passivation (Chapter 23); saponification and the oils line. Two kinds of fumes: **acid mist** (escaping from acid surfaces and exothermic reactions; a white mist with a suffocating sting) and **chlorine** (Chapter 26; yellow-green, heavier than air, pooling in low spots).

**Acute manifestations:** choking cough, suffocating feeling, tearing and burning eyes (acid mist); yellow-white burn marks on skin, burning eye pain (acid splashes); heavy chlorine exposure causes same-day pulmonary-edema-like shortness of breath (Chapter 47's three signs of shortness of breath are the escalation line; emergency transfer to Chapter 48).

**Chronic manifestations (three items at the annual examination):**

| Sign | How to check (criterion) |
|---|---|
| Dental acid erosion | Look in a mirror at the biting edges of the upper and lower front teeth: transverse grooves in the enamel, thinning and translucency of the edges = positive (a directional sign of acid mist; dentists do not exist—the mirror is the examination instrument) |
| Chronic bronchitis | Morning cough for over a month, worse in winter and lighter in summer; record year-over-year comparisons |
| Diminished smell | Three standard odor items (vinegar, ammonia water (a soap-line byproduct), pine smoke) tested against each nostril, compared at the annual examination |

**Engineering controls (Chapter 23 standards incorporated and extended):** ① **Enclosure + off-gas absorption bottle**—any apparatus producing acid mist or chlorine connects the tail of its condenser to an **ash-lye absorption bottle** to capture the off-gas (Chapter 23 original text: HCl is neutralized by ash lye, with no white mist emission; chlorine likewise passes through ash lye); ② apparatus stands in the open or in a four-side cross-ventilated shed, the operator **positioned upwind or crosswind**; ③ the retort lid is sealed with clay leaving an observation hole; never open the lid to look; ④ on smelling a suffocating white mist, withdraw immediately; ventilate first, then handle; ⑤ **emergency rinse station**—every acid work point has a bucket of clean water (lidded against dust) + a dedicated rinse pot; on an eye splash, rinse 15 min (Chapter 44 interface); ⑥ acid-mixing discipline: **add acid into water, never the reverse** (Chapter 23).

**Personal protection:** apron plus sleeve guards (Chapter 15 leather); eye protection (simple flat glass pieces, Chapter 14); a wet cloth over the mouth blocks droplets only, not gas—gas is handled by ventilation and positioning, not by covering.

**Exposure registry points:** weekly acid-work hours; splash events recorded item by item (amount, body part, response, healed or not).

### 49.2.6 Carbon Monoxide, Heat, and Noise

Three sources out of the furnace zone and forging zone, merged into one section, but their control logics differ: CO relies on ventilation and the two-person rule, heat on rotation and salted water, noise on distance and work hours—**noise has no safe exposure below a threshold; there is only dose control.**

**Where it comes from:** the incomplete-combustion products (CO) of kilns (Chapter 12), blast and forge furnaces (Chapter 18), and the boiler house (Chapter 28); heat radiation at the furnace face and the forge; noise from hammering and the bellows (the bellows exhaust whistle is often overlooked—it is a continuous sound source).

**Acute manifestations and response (to Chapter 48):**

| Source | Acute manifestations | Field red line |
|---|---|---|
| CO | Headache, dizziness, nausea, ringing ears → fainting | **Ventilate before rescuing: the rescuer must not rush into the furnace zone barehanded to carry someone out—double death in a CO zone is the classic accident.** First open doors and windows for forced cross-flow ≥5 min, tie a rope, keep two people outside as watchers, then rescue; wet cloth blocks smoke and dust, not CO |
| Heat | Heat exhaustion: heavy sweat, weakness, dizziness, calf cramps; heat stroke: **hot dry skin without sweat + confusion** = Chapter 48 emergency grade | At heat exhaustion, leave the furnace at once and drink salted water in the shade; heat stroke transfers to emergency care immediately, no "waiting it out" |
| Noise | After a blast (a single enormous report close to forging), tinnitus and deafness lasting over half a day | Register on a single case; ear protection added permanently |

**Chronic manifestations (three items at the annual examination):** CO chronic—regular morning headaches, year-round fatigue, worse in the season of closed winter windows (furnace-zone heating and insufficient dwelling ventilation share the source; check Section 45.6.1 dwelling ventilation at the same time); heat chronic—heat-intolerance exhaustion, summer output and appetite declining year by year; noise—**the 5 m whisper test**: subject facing away, the examiner 5 m away whispers three words for repetition; failure to repeat = register (compare against last year; the curve matters more than a single test).

**Engineering controls:** ① High windows for cross-flow in the furnace zone, low air intake (CO and hot air share the same upward path); ② **two-person watch in enclosed furnace zones**—single-person watch of an enclosed boiler house or a furnace-kiln basement is listed as forbidden (Chapter 37 scheduling interface); ③ continuous furnace-face work capped at **≤45 min per rotation** (empirical value; calibration method: take "short of breath while speaking + sweat not stopping" as the relief signal, measure how long this settlement's own furnace workers take to reach that signal, take 70% of it as the cap, transcribe to the ledger); ④ in summer the furnace-face shift moves up to dawn and dusk (Chapter 37); ⑤ the furnace face gets a shaded rest spot and a **salted-water drinking point** (oral rehydration standard quoted from Section 47.3.3: 1 L water + 3.5 g salt; in hot weather, the full daily amount at the furnace face); ⑥ **forging and bellows stations are concentrated at the workshop's downwind end**, with hammer and bellows positions ≥3 paces apart and the rest spot another ≥5 paces back (the Chapter 42 workstation drawing fixes these three points).

**Personal protection—wax earplugs (◎):** beeswax mixed with cotton flock, kneaded into plugs, inserted into the outer ear. ◎Wearing hand-feel criterion: after insertion, your own speaking voice sounds distinctly muffled. Practice method: each forging apprentice makes, wears, and removes plugs 20 times each, inspected by the foreman; **misjudgment compensation (measurable compensation): when worn properly, a loud shout at 1 pace (about 0.7 m) is needed to get a response—if ordinary speech is heard clearly, they are not in properly; ask not the hand feel but the response.** Declaration: wax plugs reduce sound, they do not block it; real dose control rests on work hours and distance.

**Exposure registry points:** furnace-face hours, hammering hours, and bellows hours listed separately each week; the annual-examination whisper test and morning-headache inquiry.

## 49.3 Occupational Exposure Registry and Examination System

Section 49.1 said it: chronic damage is silent; only the paper can sound the alarm. This section is that paper: one file per post, one examination per person per year, and tracing back from death—three rings connected to Chapter 51's record system.

### 49.3.1 Post Exposure File (One File per Post)

| Field | What to record (criterion) | Common mistake |
|---|---|---|
| Post name | The settlement-wide uniform name (check against the post list table) | One post, several names; the file cannot match the person |
| Exposure source | Six-source annotation (multiple allowed: forger = noise + heat + CO) | Writing only "a bit dirty" |

### 49.3.4 Post Rotation: Spreading Out the Cumulative Dose

Rotation is the active means of reducing any one person's cumulative work-years dose; three rules are fixed:

1. **Dry dust posts**: cap on continuous dry-work tenure of 5 years (empirical value; calibration method: correct against this settlement's annual-examination slope-walk-test positive rate—as the positive rate rises, the cap drops; transcribe to the ledger).
2. **Mercury pump operation**: two fixed people rotate; no one person serves alone for consecutive months (Chapter 33's two-person system, filed).
3. **Forge hammer and bellows**: swap every half day (doubles as skill backup).

**Rotation presupposes skill redundancy**: ≥2 qualified people per post (Chapter 37 skill matrix)—the rotation system in turn forces apprenticeship, which is precisely the direct payoff of the Chapter 47 master–apprentice system on poison posts.

## 49.4 The Four Waste Disposal Chains

The second sentence of the review stands here: "The result of missing a disposal chain is reverse destruction: drinking water, farmland, and craftsman health are damaged in sequence." **The disposal chain has four links: on-site classification → transport to a fixed point → endpoint disposal → ledger records; missing any one link fails the whole chain**—disposal without records equals no disposal (after the next rain, no one knows what was buried where). The four chains follow; tailings and dust wastes go into the mining variant of the slag chain (Section 49.4.5).

### 49.4.1 Furnace Slag and Mine Slag (Chapters 16, 18)

**On-site three-way classification (completed the same shift the slag comes out of the furnace; criteria checkable):**

| Class | How to tell | Destination |
|---|---|---|
| Iron-bearing slag | **Magnet test** (same as Chapter 16's magnetic dressing): pass crushed slag over a magnet; what sticks up plentifully is iron-bearing slag | Pile in centralized storage as remelt feedstock reserve (potential feed for Chapter 18's lean-material re-reduction) |
| Lean slag (building grade) | Magnet does not attract it; light color, light weight, no metallic luster; powder rubbed between the fingers has no garlic odor (arsenic check, Section 49.2.4) | Crushed for road base, aggregate in lime mortar (Chapter 13), rammed foundations |
| Heavy-metal slag (mainly lead and arsenic) | Slag out of lead, copper, and arsenic furnaces is **classed here without question**, no per-piece judgment (defend against the worst case) | Into the Section 49.4.3 heavy-metal chain, **forbidden in building materials** |

**Four siting rules for the pile yard (Chapter 37's siting method applied to slag yards):** ① ≥100 paces from the nearest water-intake point (more than three times Chapter 45's 30 paces for latrines—reason: slag volume is large, it contains metal, it sits for decades); ② not on a runoff line, not occupying a stream bank (rainy-season runoff washing the slag pollutes everything downstream); ③ downwind of the settlement's prevailing wind; ④ ground higher than the intake section, not in the same hollow. **Once the site is drawn, it enters the slag-yard drawing (Chapter 42); stakes mark the boundary.**

**Yard operation:** slag is piled by class with clear separation; an **interception ditch** around the perimeter diverts surface runoff away (same method as Chapter 45 drainage ditches); every cartload of slag is registered by source furnace, date, class, and quantity (estimated weight to ±20%, an empirical value; calibration method: fill a fixed-volume basket three times, weigh, take the average, and thereafter count by basket).

### 49.4.2 Chemical Waste Liquids and Acid Residue (Chapters 23, 26)

**Treating waste with waste (core process: use waste to treat waste):** waste acid is never poured out—the acid line's spent acid and pickle liquor are **mutually neutralized with ash lye (wood-ash water, Chapter 23)**.

| Step | Operation | Criterion |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Collection | Waste acid into a ceramic vat (dedicated vat, dedicated label), never mixed with the lye vat | Vat labeled "waste acid," wooden lid (never open-topped) |
| 2 Mutual neutralization | Ash lye added slowly to the waste-acid vat, stirred with a wooden rod | **Indicator method (Chapter 23): drip in purple-cabbage juice while stirring—red means still acid; keep adding lye until it turns purple near the original color: that is the endpoint**; a warm vat wall is normal; too hot to touch means slow down |
| 3 Settling | Let stand half a day | Mud residue settles at the vat bottom |
| 4 Splitting clear liquid from residue | The supernatant goes into the **waste-liquid pit** (dedicated pit, clay bottom, connected to no ditch); the mud is shoveled out into the **chemical-sludge landfill pit** (same pit spec: clay bottom, capped with compacted soil, marker post) | The pit locations enter the slag-yard drawing |
| 5 Records | Entered item by item into the Chapter 42 account | Waste acid amount (estimated volume), lye used, date |

**Two prohibitions:** ① **Vessel-cleaning water must not enter the drinking-and-drainage system**—water used to wash the acid vat is treated as waste acid into the waste-liquid pit; not one drop enters the household drainage ditch (the Chapter 45 ditch that runs toward the waters below the intake section); ② the saturated ash lye from chlorine absorption bottles is likewise disposed of as chemical waste liquid, not dumped into the stream just because it is "already neutralized"—chloride salts into farmland kill seedlings.

### 49.4.3 Heavy-Metal Slag and Mercury-Bearing Waste (The Red-Line Chain)

**Three-bucket system:** lead slag, mercury waste, and arsenic slag (and all slag and flue dust of the arsenic-copper line) **each get one dedicated bucket, color-banded and lettered** (lead: white band; mercury: black band; arsenic: red band—the color code enters the settlement-wide wall rules). **Mixing buckets is forbidden; mixing into ordinary slag is forbidden**—one bucket of lead slag mixed into building slag scraps the whole batch of building material and forces re-sorting; this is this section's only capital-level red line; violators are handled under Chapter 41's red-line disciplinary procedure.

| Waste | Treatment before leaving the bucket | Deep-burial spec |
|---|---|---|
| Mercury waste (spill collections, scrap mercury, mercury sludge) | **Mixed with sulfur powder until it turns black** (conversion to mercury sulfide, same as Chapter 33's spill response); conversion criterion: no silver-white beads visible | Pit depth ≥1 m, bottom compacted, capped with compacted soil |
| Lead slag | Mixed with lime (Chapter 13), moistened and encased | Same as above |
| Arsenic slag and flue dust | Mixed with lime, moistened and encased | Same as above |

**Four deep-burial rules:** ① the pit location is recorded on the slag-yard drawing **by paces and bearing** (from the marker stake: pace count + bearing per the Chapter 3 orientation method, drawn in per the Chapter 42 drawing method); ② a marker post is erected with carved letters ("lead / mercury / arsenic + year"); ③ **deep-burial spots are permanently barred from cultivation**—no food crops may be planted (safety boundary item 5); ④ check year by year: at the annual slag-account reconciliation, verify against the drawing that the stakes are intact.

**Toxin account closed loop (Chapter 38 interface):** each of lead, mercury, and arsenic gets a balance page: in (mined / purchased) = out (in use + deep-buried + book loss). **Mercury's loss monitoring line is in Section 49.2.3; lead and arsenic follow the same method**—an abnormal book loss means check the pile yard, the bucket labels, and the burial spots.

### 49.4.4 Workshop Wastewater and Settling in Stages

**General rule: workshop wastewater and household drinking water are separate systems.** Chapter 45 has already ruled that household drainage ditches must not discharge upstream of the intake section; this chapter tightens it: **workshop wastewater (slurry water, pickle water, quench water, furnace-zone wash water) does not enter the household drainage ditch; it gets its own separate system.**

**Three-stage settling ponds (in series from high to low):**

| Stage | Function | Spec (empirical + calibrated) |
|---|---|---|
| Stage 1 · grit chamber | Coarse sand and grit sink to the bottom | Depth ≥0.8 m; a baffle at the inlet face slows the flow |
| Stage 2 · settling pond | Fine silt and suspended solids settle | Volume 1.5× stage 1; the overflow between the two ponds passes at a high level |
| Outlet · clarified water | Overflows to a downstream non-intake section | The outlet has a gravel filter layer (same method as the Chapter 45 filter line) |

The desilting cycle is not fixed in days: **empirical criterion = clean when silt reaches one third of the pond depth**; measure this workshop's own days-to-full (survey with a rod once a month in the first season), then enter the measured cycle into the Chapter 43 maintenance table. **Wastewater from heavy-metal workshops (lead, mercury, arsenic) does not enter the three-stage ponds**—it goes alone into a dedicated pond; when that pond silts up, mix with lime and deep-bury per the Section 49.4.3 lead-arsenic slag method.

### 49.4.5 Tailings and Dust Wastes (The Mining Variant of the Slag Chain)

The hazard of dressing tailings (the fine slurry left after water separation) and of dust collected by dry dust removal lies not in a single toxicity value but in **quantity and re-dispersal**: a dry tailings beach at the first wind becomes a secondary exposure source inhaled by the whole village.

| Step | Operation | Criterion |
|---|---|---|
| Wet stacking | The tailings slurry is directed to the tailings yard, the surface kept wet and dark | If the surface dries white, channel more water or sprinkle—**wet stacking is a rule, not a suggestion** |
| Retaining wall | A stone retaining wall (Chapter 13 masonry) on the downhill side holds back slump slides | Check for cracks before the rainy season |
| Covering | Decommissioned yard sections get ≥0.2 m of soil cover | The soil layer is continuous with no tailings showing through |
| Vegetation | Sow Chapter 10's soil-binding fast-growing grasses or shrubs | Green-up the next season is the survival criterion |
| Windy-season patrols | One patrol every ten days in wind season | **Any dust seen means sprinkle at once**; patrols enter the slag-yard ledger |

## 49.5 The Backfire Simulation: How a Settlement Poisons Its Own Water in Five Years

This chapter's "outbreak investigation." Section 47.4's five-step method investigates patients; this section turns the same method on the environment—**the slag yard is the chronic version of the disease source, and the pin map is its X-ray.** The simulation's base case is fictional; every hinge comes from the chains written above.

**Base case: the Baishui settlement, 150 people. In year two it builds a copper-lead smelting shop and sites the slag yard on the gentle slope upstream of the stream—reasoning: "close to the shop, saves the slag-carting labor" (not one of Chapter 37's four siting rules followed). Rain in two seasons year-round. The simulation runs from the shop's founding:**

| Year | What happened | Signal visible at the time | Gate that could have intercepted it (which link failed) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Year one | Lead slag and lean slag heaped together on the slope, no interception ditch; rainy-season leachate ran down the slope into the stream | Nothing—**this is the very shape of chronic poison: in year one nothing can be seen** | Gate 1 · siting (Section 49.4.1's four rules); Gate 2 · classification and interception ditch |
| Year two | The stream occasionally smells off; the downstream catch halves; upstream households say "the water tastes heavy" | Biological signal: fewer fish are the earliest sentinels—earlier than people | Gate 3 · slag account (the mixed heap not caught by reconciliation); Gate 4 · environmental sentinels unwatched |
| Year three | Downstream irrigated fields have weak seedlings, yield down two tenths; scattered abdominal pain and earthy-gray faces among downstream children, taken as "worms stirring" | Children = the most sensitive sentinels: **clusters of child abdominal pain and earthy-gray complexion precede adult occupational cases** | Gate 5 · children's cases not asked about address and water direction (Chapter 47's four-question table lacks "which fields' grain do you eat") |
| Year four | Downstream adults: growing numbers of fatigue, constipation, and colic cases; on the registry "scattered," no one reads them together | The cases are already on the books—**what was missing was pinning the cases onto the map by water direction** | Gate 6 · pin map not done (Section 47.4.2, overlay the water-source edge) |
| Year five | The hygienist, per this section's method, makes a slag-yard-vs-cases pin map + salt tracer: 10 kg of salt into the slag yard's seep pit; on day 4 the downstream intake point tastes salty—connectivity confirmed; the slag yard is sealed, fields moved, the water source changed | With every gate breached, the bill is in the table below | —— |

**One-line gate review: had any single link of the disposal chain held, the simulation stops in that year.** Siting holds (year one), classification holds (year one), reconciliation holds (year two), asking children's cases about water direction holds (year three), the pin map holds (year four)—five gates, each a procedure already written above. **The backfire is not fate; it is a list of skipped procedures.**

### 49.5.1 The Slag-Yard–Case Association Investigation Method (Connecting to Chapter 51's Pin Map)

1. **Pin map with three overlaid edges**: use the Section 47.4.2 pin-map method as-is, but the three edges become **the water system (stream course and intake points) / slag yards and burial spots (Section 49.4 drawings) / case households**. Reading the clustering pattern: cases banding along the downstream water system while upstream households are clean = an environmental source is indicated; cases clustering only in workshop trades = an occupational source is indicated (Section 49.3's exposure files are the differential basis).
2. **The trade four-question table** (a revision of Section 47.4.3's four-question table, run per case): what trade (exposure file) / usual water source / which bank do you live on (upstream / downstream) / **which fields' grain do you eat** (irrigation water direction). All four questions recorded; the common factor shows itself.
3. **Salt tracer to verify connectivity** (Chapter 45's original method): 5–10 kg of table salt dissolved into the slag yard's seep pit at once; then for 7 days taste the intake point daily (boiled and cooled, a dedicated spoon, one mouthful a day); turning salty = groundwater connectivity; seal the slag yard. **This is the only field developer of "invisible leachate," using no reagents at all.**
4. **The child-sentinel rule**: child abdominal pain + earthy-gray complexion + anemia-like clustering, **check the environment before the trade**—children do not enter workshops; their exposure comes only from water and food; the child curve is the environmental line, the adult trade curve the occupational line; draw the two lines separately.

## 49.6 Emergency Plans (Field Response Sequences for Acute Events)

### 49.6.1 Universal Response Sequence (All Acute Poisonings and Exposures)

1. **Leave the exposure** (first action, person before property): move people upwind to fresh air; for skin exposure, **dry-sweep / dry-wipe first**, then flush with large volumes of clean water (lime classes: sweep before flushing—water activates the burn);
2. **Ventilate and isolate the scene**: open doors and windows, stop fans, hang a maintenance tag; clear out unrelated personnel and post one person at the entrance;
3. **First aid** (Chapter 44 procedures connect in): breathing and pulse response, burn flushing, eyes rinsed with clean water ≥15 min with the eyelids everted;
4. **Record**: time, people, substance, response, symptoms—entered into the Chapter 47 case book the same day; poison-involved events also into this chapter's exposure file.

### 49.6.2 Three Specialized Plans

2. **Tools required**: sulfur dispenser (paper packet or shaker); soap wash station (for washing up after withdrawal); timing means (Chapter 3, for the 10 min wait); cordon sign and one person to hold the entrance. Calibration before use: sulfur crumbles freely between the fingers (not caked); the jar lid seals and the marking is legible; the timer is checked against the clock.
3. **Sequence and control points**: **Do first, in this order, no step skipped**: ① **get people out before touching the material**—throttle that area's vent, send everyone not needed 10 min away, leave one person holding the entrance; ② gather the visible beads with the stiff card, pushing inward from the outer edge (no patting, no blowing, no sweeping); ③ **sprinkle sulfur thickly over every visible bead and every floor seam**; ④ after 10 min gather the sulfur-mercury mixture with the stiff card into the lidded red-marked jar; ⑤ sprinkle the seams with sulfur a second time; ⑥ give that area one week of boosted ventilation and rotate the personnel (two-person rotation, no one person month after month); ⑦ register time / location / estimated amount / who cleaned, enter the spill ledger and copy it into the exposure file (Section 49.3), and the same day into the Chapter 47 case register if symptoms appear. **Never do**: sweep with a broom or pat the spill (airflow shatters mercury into microbeads and the evaporating surface explodes); never vacuum, never blow air, never flush it into a drain; never gather it bare-handed; never put the sulfur-mercury mixture into ordinary slag or burn it; never "carry on as usual" after a spill with no registration and no rotation; no eating, drinking, or smoking in the mercury work area. **When you must withdraw**: a second spill at the same post within one year stops that post's work and triggers a process review (Chapter 33 linkage); if an indoor spill cannot be isolated or its vent throttled, withdraw everyone from the area, seal it, and wait for the sulfur; if the cleaner shows red swollen gums with excess saliva, spills past a rim-width in the water-bowl walk, or grows irritable or sleepless, apply the Section 49.3 two-step positive response (off exposure + recheck next month)—move them off the job first, recheck after.
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: people withdrawn 10 min; gather 10 min after the sulfur goes down; one week of boosted ventilation for the area; two spills at one post within a year triggers the process review; mercury work is run by two people in rotation, never one person alone month after month. Calibration: the sulfur dose is judged by "no silver-white glint from any bead or seam" (not by weight); conversion in the jar is judged by "no silver-white bead visible" (Section 49.4.3, sulfur until it turns black).
5. **Observations to record**: location and extent of the spill, estimated amount, number of sulfur passes, whether any silver-white bead remained in the jar, the second sprinkling, start and end of ventilation and rotation, the double entry in ledger and exposure file, and the symptom inquiry for the cleaner.
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = visible beads at zero + jar sealed and red-marked + double entry in ledger and exposure file + rotation executed; rework = a seam still glints silver-white → sprinkle sulfur again and re-gather; scrap (management failure) = sweeping with a broom, flushing with water, or dumping into ordinary slag—registered as a red-line event, that batch of slag quarantined and re-sorted, the event taken into review.
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order** (most frequent first): ① reaching for the broom (commonest and worst) → stop at once, switch to the stiff card, increase the sulfur dose and the ventilation time; ② seams missed → sprinkle sulfur again and re-inspect along the seams in raking light; ③ cannot find every landing spot of scattered mercury → compensate with full-coverage sulfur sprinkling (a UV lamp is a far-term means, not part of this procedure now); ④ work carried on after a spill with no registration and no rotation → check the spill ledger and retrain on this card; ⑤ gathered material mixed into ordinary slag → trace and re-sort per the Section 49.4.3 heavy-metal red line, and quarantine the whole batch of building material.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one drill: 10 min withdrawal + sulfur + gathering into the jar + one ledger page; L2 = one real spill handled to standard, double entry in ledger and exposure file, rotation at that area executed; L3 = a year with no "broom / water flush / ordinary slag" violations + the process review closed after two spills at one post + one person brought up to handle a spill independently.

**Acid spill**: small areas—cover with dry sand to absorb, shovel into the waste-acid vat, neutralize by slow addition of ash lye (stirring, indicator to near neutral); large areas—first build a sand berm to keep it out of the ditches, then as above; **never flush directly with water into the drain** (berm first, then flush).

**Chlorine / acid-mist leak**: all personnel evacuate upwind; close the clamps, stop the reaction; spray an ash-lye mist over the leak area to settle it; those with coughing or stinging go to Chapter 44 observation.

### 49.6.3 Drill System

One comprehensive drill each year (one specialized plan drawn at random + the universal sequence): time it (**leaving the exposure ≤1 min is the pass line**, an empirical value), record it, debrief into the file. **A plan never drilled equals no plan.**

## Safety Boundary

> **Hard gate restated (implementing the part-level declaration)**: where any medical card in this chapter writes a criterion that falls "beyond the boundary," stop at once; the only remaining responses are warming / rehydration / pain relief / attendance / recording (see Chapter 44's part declaration).

- Those handling mercury- and arsenic-bearing waste are **trained before posted**; after work, follow that poison's washing procedure;
- Guard against inhaling sulfur powder; guard against splashes during neutralization (acid into lye, add slowly, stir gently);
- Waste-slag deep-burial pits ≥2 cubits + a lime layer + marker posts—**the location registry is for those who come after**;
- Every measure in this chapter is engineering and institutions, not medicine: suspected occupational disease symptoms are managed under Chapter 47's case system with removal from exposure; [Background/Outlook] specialized occupational medicine awaits a mature medical system.
