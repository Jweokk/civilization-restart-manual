Every treatment technique in this chapter is a combination of four words: **hemostasis, immobilization, cleaning (irrigation and debridement), transport**. Before any action, ask which of the words it belongs to; if it belongs to none of the four, the default is "do not do it".

## Part 9 General Declaration: Medical Boundaries (In Force Jointly for All of Chapters 44–49)

> **The medical content of this manual consists of damage-control procedures for situations of extreme isolation; it is not licensed medical advice.** Three hard boundaries: ① whenever professional care is reachable, seek professional care first — this Part's content fills only the gap where there is "nothing at all"; ② the criteria at the end of every medical card are a hard gate — **for situations marked "stop once beyond the boundary," continuing only kills or cripples faster**, and once stopped, the only treatments left are warmth, fluid replacement, pain relief, accompaniment, and record-keeping; ③ every "effective" judgment in this Part defers to the evidence grading of Chapter 46 (the drug-dosage system); anything below evidence Grade I/II is uniformly labeled "unverified". This Part's positioning is the implementation of Iron Law 7: labor is the primary asset, and the primary asset needs not miracle healers but stable procedures that make no fatal mistakes.


## Capability Boundaries

**CAN (this chapter gives methods and criteria):** surface hemostasis; wound irrigation, three-tier grading, and conservative debridement; suturing of clean lacerations and delayed suturing with open drainage for contaminated wounds; burn depth grading and cold-water treatment; splinting of fractures and multi-person flat-lift transport of spinal injuries; handling of superficial eye foreign bodies and chemical eye exposure; rescue-dragging in CO poisoning and manual airway opening; incision and drainage of superficial abscesses; limited analgesia; amputation (last resort, Section 44.8).

**CANNOT (explicitly "not done"):**

| Procedure not done | Reason | When it can resume |
|---|---|---|
| Laparotomy, thoracotomy, craniotomy — all body-cavity surgery | Internal bleeding is invisible, non-compressible, non-ligatable; no general anesthesia, no transfusion, no anti-infection backstop — doing it means death on the spot, while not doing it still allows observation and transport | An external medical system with anesthesia, transfusion, and a sterile operating room |
| Removal of deep embedded foreign bodies (iron spikes, wooden stakes, large glass shards) | The foreign body is tamponading the very vessel it cut; the moment it is pulled out there is massive hemorrhage with no transfusion available (Section 44.3.3) | Same as above |
| On-site reduction of open fractures | The exposed bone end is already contaminated; pushing it back carries dirt into the medullary cavity; reduction without anesthesia causes secondary vascular and nerve injury (Section 44.5.3) | Same as above |
| Blood transfusion | No route exists for blood typing, anticoagulation, or pyrogen control (General Outline exclusion table) | External system |
| General anesthesia and invasive local anesthesia | No means of calibrating doses, no means of counteracting respiratory depression; listed as subject to the Chapter 46 dosage-trial system; it does not enter the procedures until acceptance is passed | Chapter 46 system + external testing capability |
| Antibiotics, vaccines, biologicals | General Outline "explicitly excluded technologies": require controlled culture, aseptic filling, batch testing, cold chain | Microscope + aseptic technique + strain library + testing and verification system (Chapters 45–46 define the boundary) |

For local pain relief only two limited means are retained: **immobilization** (good fixation is itself analgesia) and **cold water** (Section 44.4); alcohol analgesia is used in restricted fashion per Section 44.9.

The hedges against infection number only four: irrigation volume, debridement quality, open drainage, and isolation with record-keeping. **There is no drug.** This fact dictates that every "do a bit more" impulse in this chapter must be held down: in a world without an anti-infection backstop, doing less invasion and more irrigation saves more lives than the reverse.

**External dependencies (counted in the Class-C list):** legacy medicines and dressings (consumed without replacement; entered in the Chapter 38 accounts); tetanus antitoxin (no solution; Section 44.9.4 states this honestly); prosthetics and other rehabilitation parts (Chapter 7 woodworking can make crude substitutes of limited function).

## 44.1 Occupational Injury Lineage: First-Minute Procedures

The first minute allows only three actions, in fixed order: **① cut the source and stop the machine** (close the steam valve, shut the damper, lay down the tool) — equipment that keeps running will make a second casualty; **② call for help and grab the kit**; **③ do not move the injured except out of a hazardous environment (fire, toxic gas, falling-object zone)**. The lineage below maps line by line to the processes described earlier; copy and post it at the corresponding workshops.

| Process (source chapter) | Common injuries | First minute | Details |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stone-tool knapping (Chapter 5) | Cuts from stone flakes, chips in the eye | No rubbing if in the eye; flush with clean water; cuts get direct pressure | 44.2, 44.6 |
| Woodworking axe, chisel, saw (Chapter 7) | Deep cuts, severed fingers, deep splinters | Press the stump with clean cloth (wrap the severed finger in dry cloth and take it along with the casualty — no replantation route exists; it is kept only for record-keeping and stump inspection); do not pull out deep splinters | 44.2, 44.3.3 |
| Tree felling (Chapter 7) | Trunk crush injuries, falls | Gather ≥3 people to lift the load; for crush lasting over 15 min, be alert for deterioration after release; treat falls as spinal injuries | 44.5 |
| Masonry and work at height (Chapter 13) | Falls, struck by falling objects | Do not sit the person up, do not carry on the back; lay flat, check sensation in hands and feet, then decide transport | 44.5.4 |
| Pottery and brick kilns (Chapters 11–12) | Burns at the kiln mouth, carbon monoxide in the kiln passage | Burns: cold water flush ≥15 min; CO: ventilate first, test with a flame, drag out | 44.4, 44.7 |
| Glass melting and lampworking (Chapter 14) | Molten-glass splash burns, cuts, photophthalmia | Splash: cold water flush; eye inflammation: avoid light, cold compress | 44.4, 44.6 |
| Underground mining (Chapter 16) | Collapse crush, CO and hypoxia, falls | Call for help from outside the drift; never enter alone; ventilate first, rope up, two people; a weakening flame serves only as an auxiliary warning; drag out before treating | 44.7 |
| Iron smelting and casting (Chapter 18) | Molten-iron splash burns, hot-slag burns, CO | If the splash entered clothing, remove the clothing first (molten metal keeps burning through cloth); cold water flush ≥15 min | 44.4, 44.7 |
| Forging (Chapter 19) | Hammer-crushed fingers, iron filings in the eye, burns | Severed finger: pressure and dressing; filings: never rub, flush the eye | 44.2, 44.6 |
| Grinding and cutting (Chapters 19–20) | Iron filings and grit in the eye, entanglement crush injuries | Stop the machine before reaching in; flush the eye | 44.6 |
| Basic chemistry (Chapter 23) | Acid and alkali burns, lime burns | Flush with large volumes of clean water ≥15 min; **with dry lime, brush off all dry powder before flushing** (it releases heat on contact with water) | 44.4, 44.6.2 |
| Steam engines (Chapter 28) | Steam scalds, burst-pipe impact injuries, heat stroke in the engine house | Close the steam valve, get away from the leak; estimate steam scalds one depth grade deeper | 44.4.5 |

The lineage table is used by **copying and posting it in advance**: when an accident happens, read the table and execute — do not improvise on the spot. Quarterly drills draw rows from this table for assessment (Section 44.10.2).

## 44.2 Hemostasis

### 44.2.1 Bleeding Classification and General Principles

| Type | Appearance | Danger | First choice |
|---|---|---|---|
| Capillary oozing | Uniform oozing across the whole wound surface, dark red | Low (mostly stops with pressure) | Direct pressure |
| Venous bleeding | Dark red, steady welling flow | Medium | Direct pressure + elevate the limb |
| Arterial bleeding | Bright red, spurting or welling with the pulse | High (uncontrolled bleeding from a major limb artery can kill within minutes) | Direct pressure; if two rounds fail, go to Section 44.2.3 |

General principle: **for all bleeding, the first action is direct pressure** — where pressure works, no tourniquet; where a cloth can separate hand from wound, never press with the bare hand.

### 44.2.2 Direct Pressure Method (First Choice)

| Step | What to do | To what degree (criterion) | How to recover from mistakes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Cover the wound with a boiled (or cleanest available) cloth pad | Pad extends 20–30 mm beyond the wound edge on all sides | When the pad soaks through, **do not remove the bottom layer**; add new cloth on top and keep pressing — lifting the cloth tears off the clot that just formed |
| 2 | Press the pad with the heel of the palm, sustained force | **Press 10–15 min without releasing or lifting to peek**; simultaneously elevate the limb above heart level (do not elevate if fracture is suspected) | Lifting to peek early = restart the 10 min count |
| 3 | Release pressure slowly and observe | No bright-red pulsatile bleeding; seepage on the outer cloth not expanding | Still bleeding → press deeply one palm-width proximal to the wound for another 10 min; if that fails too → Section 44.2.3 |
| 4 | Pressure bandage: place a cloth roll over the dressing and wrap the bandage in circles | After wrapping, distal nail-bed press refill ≤2 s | Distal end purple or numb → rewrap one notch looser |

### 44.2.3 Cloth-Strap Windlass Tourniquet (When Direct Pressure Fails)

Prerequisites for use (all must hold simultaneously): major bleeding of a limb; two rounds of direct pressure (≥20 min cumulative) have failed; or the limb is already severed; or multiple injuries require freeing both hands to treat elsewhere.

Equipment: cloth strap ≥50 mm wide (tear cloth into strips; **thin cord, metal wire, and rattan are forbidden** — too narrow concentrates pressure and crushes vessels and nerves); one windlass rod (150–200 mm wooden stick, Chapter 7); padding cloth; charcoal pencil.

| Step | Key points |
|---|---|
| 1 Wrap the strap | 50–100 mm proximal to the wound (upper arm/thigh), avoiding the elbow and knee joint surfaces; pad cloth between strap and skin to prevent strap injury |
| 2 Tighten | Tie a slip knot in the strap and insert the windlass rod; **twist until bleeding stops or the distal pulse disappears, and no further** (see ◎ below) |
| 3 Secure | Lock the windlass rod so it cannot spring back |
| 4 Record the time | With the charcoal pencil, **write the application time in two places**: on the casualty's hand/forehead and on the tourniquet itself — omitting this is the most common fatal error of this route (row 2 of the failure-modes table) |
| 5 Loosen | **Loosen once every 30–45 min**: first apply direct pressure to the wound, then release the windlass for 1–2 min; if pressure alone maintains hemostasis, do not re-tighten |

◎ Hand-feel for the tightening degree: twist until the limb distal to the strap shows no refill when pressed and bleeding has stopped. Practice method: a healthy person practices wrapping and knotting on their own leg (without tightening), 5 times per quarter. Misjudgment compensation: substitute observable criteria — "oozing has stopped + nail bed pale with no refill."

**Why intermittent loosening, and its cost (a fundamental difference from a hospital setting)**: in modern protocols a tourniquet, once tightened, is not loosened mid-way, because an operating room waits downstream. This route has no downstream — a limb continuously twisted for over 2 h will certainly necrose, and necrotic tissue absorbed into the blood is equally lethal; therefore intermittent loosening is mandatory, at the cost that every loosening risks re-bleeding. Weighing the two harms: **the tourniquet is a tool for buying time, not a sign that hemostasis is complete**; while it is on, keep preparing to replace it with a pressure bandage.

### 44.2.4 Nosebleed (Epistaxis)

Sitting position, leaning forward (**tilting the head back is forbidden** — blood swallowed into the stomach both masks the true blood loss and induces vomiting); pinch the cartilaginous part of the nose **10–15 min** without releasing; cold compress on the bridge of the nose. Criterion: no active bleeding after release. Boundary: if it recurs more than twice or a single episode lasts >30 min, the medic performs anterior nasal packing: a boiled gauze strip moistened with clean water or oil is packed into the front part of the nose, leaving a tail ≥20 mm outside; retain ≤24 h, then remove.

[Card 44-01 Hemostasis] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: ≥6 boiled cloth pads (each 0.3–0.5 m², Chapter 15); 1 cloth strap (≥50 mm wide, ≥0.8 m long, torn from cloth; **fine cord, wire and rattan are forbidden** — too narrow, pressure concentrates and crushes vessels and nerves); 1 windlass rod (150–200 mm stick, Chapter 7); padding cloth; charcoal pencil. Acceptable substitute: laundered sun-dried clothing (performance loss: lower sterility; downgraded situations only, recorded).
2. **Tools required**: no instrument required. Water clock or sundial (Chapter 3, checked against the noon shadow before use); if none, convert minutes from a resting pulse of 60–80 beats/min; injury record sheet (44.10.3) and charcoal pencil.
3. **Sequence and control points**: **Read the urgency criteria first — in this order, do whichever appears, never skip a step**: (1) **massive bleeding** (bright red, spurting or welling with the pulse, cloth soaking through fast) → no questions, heel of the palm straight onto the wound, elevate the limb at the same time (not if fracture is suspected); (2) **asphyxia / no normal breathing** (unresponsive + no breathing or agonal gasps) → first shut off the source and ventilate, enter roped with a second person outside on watch, drag clear, and only then open the airway and start compressions (44.7.2 — this order may not be reversed); (3) **shock** (pale, cold sweat, thin rapid pulse, altered consciousness) → hemostasis first, lie flat and keep warm, small frequent oral fluids per 44.4.6 if fully conscious, **nothing by mouth in anyone unconscious**. Main sequence: (1) cloth over the wound (pad 20–30 mm beyond the wound edge all round); **(2) heel of the palm, sustained pressure 10–15 min, no peeking**; (3) not stopped → add cloth, press another 10 min, elevate the limb; **(4) still not stopped → strap and windlass (50–100 mm proximal, clear of elbow and knee joint surfaces, padding beneath) until bleeding stops, charcoal-pencil the time in two places — back of the hand or forehead and on the tourniquet**; (5) pressure bandage + nail-bed refill ≤2 s; (6) loosen the tourniquet every 30–45 min (press the wound first, then release 1–2 min); if pressure alone holds it, do not re-apply. Control points: no skipping, no reversing; **a tourniquet with no written time counts as no hemostasis at all**.
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: pressure 10–15 min, strap 50–100 mm proximal, loosening cycle 30–45 min, nail-bed refill ≤2 s — all empirical (parameter table). Calibration: record every case on the injury record sheet (bleeding type, time to hemostasis, total tourniquet time, outcome), compile quarterly, revise this settlement's values and transcribe into the ledger (Chapter 43). Analgesia and fluids follow the boundaries of 44.9.1 and 44.4.6; this chapter gives no dose — follow the Chapter 46 medicine-material cards and trial increments.
5. **Observations to record**: bleeding appearance (oozing/welling/spurting), time to hemostasis, time of strap application and of each loosening, distal nail-bed color, complexion and pulse rate — in the "hemostasis" column of the injury record sheet.
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = bleeding stopped + distal circulation present (nail-bed refill ≤2 s) + time written down. Rework = seepage spreading but still pressable (press again or re-wrap; when a soaked outer layer must be replaced, add cloth and keep the bottom layer). Scrap (treatment failed) = spurting continues after two pressure rounds (≥20 min in total) + one windlass round → switch to continuous bare-hand pressure on the proximal limb during transport; record "hemostasis failed". **Hard gate**: when every hemostatic means is spent, bleeding continues and there is no way to move the casualty — stop; what remains is warmth, fluids, company and records (Part declaration, clause 2).
7. **Common failures and troubleshooting order** (by frequency): (1) dressing soaks through fast → first check whether the bottom layer was ever stripped → check whether pressure went on the wound edge instead of the proximal supply point → check whether a fracture end is pushing the wound open (add fixation); (2) distal limb purple-black and numb after the strap → first check whether the time was recorded (**the commonest fatal error**) → check whether it was over-wound (loosen until bleeding just stops); (3) nosebleed not stopping → check whether the head was tilted back → check whether the hand kept letting go to look.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = recite every step in a drill and perform the whole sequence once completely; L2 = timed trial: from "injury called out" to effective pressure ≤60 s, to completed tourniquet time-marking ≤3 min, three consecutive passes; L3 = quarterly drill records + ledger of real injury cases (no "died of blood loss" outcome, no forgotten-tourniquet case).

## 44.3 Wound Grading and Debridement

### 44.3.1 Three-Tier Grading Table

Grading is completed before irrigation — the grade determines all subsequent treatment. Three observable dimensions: depth, contamination source, and time since injury.

| Grade | Identification (observable) | Treatment | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ⅰ Clean minor laceration | Length <50 mm; depth only into subcutaneous fat; wound edges tidy; bleeding is oozing or slight welling; ≤6 h since injury; injuring object clean (knife, fresh wood, freshly broken glass) | Irrigate ≥500 mL → immediate suturing or butterfly strips → cover with dressing and observe | Over 6 h, doubtful source, or high summer heat: downgrade to Grade Ⅱ without exception |
| Ⅱ Contaminated wound | Soil/rusty objects/clothing carried in; animal bites; >6 h since injury; edges bruised, ragged, uneven | Irrigate ≥1 L → conservative debridement → **no suturing; open drainage**; on days 3–5, if the wound shows no sign of infection, perform delayed suturing | No intuition that it "still looks clean" changes the grade |
| Ⅲ Deep and penetrating wounds | Deeper than fat, reaching fascia or muscle; penetration into a joint cavity; retained foreign body; chest or abdominal penetration | Irrigate the surroundings, cover, **do not explore, do not suture**, immobilize, record and transport | Body-cavity penetration = outside the capability boundary (capability boundary table); only cover and transport |

The 6 h limit is an empirical value (the order of magnitude of time for contaminating bacteria to multiply to pathogenic numbers; at air temperature >25 ℃ it shortens to 4 h). Calibration: compile infection rates quarterly from the injury record sheets; if >10%, tighten the limit and transcribe the local value into the ledger.

### 44.3.2 Irrigation

- Water quality: warm water settled and filtered, then boiled at a rolling boil ≥3 min (water intake, Chapter 10; fire, Chapter 6; ceramic vessels, Chapter 11).
- Apparatus and pressure: a hanging bag positioned high (cloth bag, skin bladder, or ceramic pot) 1–1.5 m above the wound; a fine outlet forms an impact stream — **what flushes out foreign matter and bacteria is the mechanical force of the flow, not the water quality itself**; basin-and-dip washing is ineffective.
- Saline preparation: 3 g of salt per 1 L of clean water (salt, Chapter 23; weighed on scales, Chapters 3/22; **grabbing salt by feel is forbidden** — error by feel can reach 3-fold). Mix fresh the day it is used.
- Minimum volumes: Grade Ⅰ ≥500 mL; Grade Ⅱ ≥1 L; Grade Ⅲ ≥500 mL around the wound, never into the depths.
- Technique: the flow moves from the wound center outward; remove visible foreign matter with boiled tweezers; **scrubbing back and forth across the wound with cloth is forbidden** (it wipes surrounding dirt into the wound and damages tissue).

### 44.3.3 Foreign Body Handling

| Situation | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Superficial: visible under the skin or easily palpable, diameter <5 mm, not a sharp long object lying against a vessel | After irrigation, extract with boiled tweezers along the entry angle, then treat by wound grade |
| Deep or embedded: penetrating wooden stakes or iron spikes, large glass shards, any foreign body lying against a pulsating vessel | **Do not pull it out.** Fix in place: ring-pad with soft cloth around it, then bandage the foreign body and the body as one unit to prevent shifting during transport. Reason: the object is tamponading the vessel it cut; the moment it is pulled out there is massive hemorrhage with no transfusion available |
| Multiple tiny foreign bodies (sand, debris) | Do not remove one by one; after irrigation, leave them to work out on their own; record daily |

### 44.3.4 Excision of Devitalized Tissue (Conservative Principle)

Cut only three things: skin margins that are obviously shredded and bloodless, loose fat fragments, and loose tissue fragments carrying dirt. Criteria: no bleeding, pale or blackened, comes off at a touch of the tweezers. **No aggressive debridement "down to a freshly bleeding surface"** — without an antibiotic backstop, every extra cut is another portal of entry for infection; tissue of doubtful blood supply is always left for observation, and cut only after the necrotic boundary naturally clarifies over 3–5 days (in coordination with the Section 44.9 infection surveillance).

### 44.3.5 Choosing Between Suturing and Open Drainage

| Wound | Suture | Drain or leave open |
|---|---|---|
| Grade Ⅰ clean lacerations; face, scalp, and joint surfaces needing closure to prevent drying and aid hemostasis | Immediate suturing | — |
| Grade Ⅱ contaminated | **Delayed suturing** after days 3–5 with no sign of infection | Initially open: loose dressing cover, daily observation |
| Deep pocketed wounds, animal bites | Do not suture | Open + drain strip (boiled gauze strip inserted loosely, tail left outside) |
| Abscess incision cavity (Section 44.9.3) | Do not suture | Open drainage until granulation fills |

One-sentence principle: **suturing is for hemostasis and apposition, not for wrapping contamination out of sight; suturing contamination closed is the same as culturing it**.

### 44.3.6 Suturing Technique and Substitutes

- Instruments: needle holder (fine-shaft ironwork, Chapter 19; hemostatic forceps may substitute); curved needles are better than straight; thread is 0.3–0.5 mm linen or silk (Chapter 15), boiled 15 min or soaked in alcohol 10 min before the procedure.
- Spacing: margin (entry point to skin edge) 3–5 mm; stitch interval 8–12 mm, use the upper limit where tension is high; on low-tension sites such as the face and back of the hand, stitch interval 5–8 mm.
- Needle entry: enter perpendicular to the skin surface, exit on the opposite edge at the same depth — with equal depth on both sides the edges appose naturally; **an inverted (rolled-in) edge means removing that stitch and re-suturing** (inverted edges heal poorly and hide infection).
- Knots: instrument ties are primary; the first throw is doubled to prevent slipping, finished as a square knot; push the knot to one side, leaving thread tails ≥5 mm for easy removal.
- ◎ The hand-feel of suturing and knotting cannot be conveyed fully in words. **Practice method — stretch a whole hide from slaughter or thick leather taut on a wooden frame, cut a 30 mm incision, and accumulate 30 completed stitches as the assessment baseline**. Pass criteria (measurable): difference between largest and smallest stitch intervals ≤2 mm; no inversion; each knot holds a 500 g sandbag for 1 min without slipping. Misjudgment compensation: cut off the sutured strip of skin and hang weights from both ends as a tensile test — if the thread breaks first or the skin edges gape open >5 mm, it fails.
- Butterfly strip substitute (hide glue or fish-maw glue + cloth strips, Chapter 15): a 10 mm wide cloth strip glued at both ends and narrowed at the middle, laid across the wound to pull the edges together. Suitable for <30 mm low-tension superficial lacerations and for delayed-suture closure; criterion: edges apposed with no seeping gap. Do not use strips across joints, the pre-tibial shin, or other high-tension sites — suture those.

1. **Input materials**: saline (salt 3 g/L clean water, weighed and mixed fresh, used the same day; **never grab salt by hand** — hand error reaches 3×); irrigation bag and clean water (settled, filtered, boiled ≥3 min); boiled instruments (knife, scissors, forceps, clamp, needle holder, water at a full boil ≥15 min); suture needles (curved or fine straight, Chapter 19) and thread (0.3–0.5 mm linen or silk, Chapter 15; each batch sampled — 2 kg hung 1 min without breaking); dressing cloth; drain gauze strips; butterfly tape (Chapter 15 hide glue or fish-bladder glue + cloth strip). Substitutes and losses: clean water for saline (more sting, no loss of safety); tape for suture (<30 mm low-tension superficial lacerations only; closure strength one grade lower).
2. **Tools required**: bag-drop check (hung 1–1.5 m, water-level mark); balance (Chapters 3/22) for the salt; water clock; skin-frame practice piece (whole hide or thick leather tacked to a wooden frame, 44.3.6).
3. **Sequence and control points**: **Read the urgency criteria first — in this order**: (1) **massive bleeding (spurting/welling) → hemostasis per Card 44-01 first; hemostasis comes before grading and before debridement**; (2) **unconscious or in shock** (no response, pale, cold sweat) → lie flat, keep warm, nothing by mouth, assess and move first; no suturing and no incision that day; (3) **deep or impaled foreign body** (iron spike, wooden stake, sheet glass, anything sitting on a pulsating vessel) → **do not pull it out**; ring-pad it with soft cloth, bandage the object and the body as one unit, transport (44.3.3 hard boundary). Main sequence: **(1) grade first (I/II/III) — the grade decides everything**; (2) irrigate by grade (I ≥500 mL; II ≥1 L; III ≥500 mL around the wound, not into the depth; flow from the centre outward; **never rub the wound surface with cloth**); (3) boiled forceps remove superficial foreign bodies (<5 mm) along their entry angle; (4) conservative excision of dead tissue (no bleeding, pale or black, lifts at a touch — all three before cutting); **(5) grade I closed at once; grade II left open to drain, immediate suture forbidden (delayed closure on days 3–5 if no infection signs); grade III covered, splinted, not probed**; (6) dressing + injury record sheet. Control points: no suturing or incision on any day the instruments were not boiled; grading is finished before irrigation; the 6 h limit (>25 °C: 4 h) is not overridden by "it still looks clean".
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: 6 h grading limit (>25 °C: 4 h), irrigation volumes, margin 3–5 mm, stitch spacing 8–12 mm (5–8 mm on low-tension face and back of hand), thread 2 kg × 1 min, knot 500 g × 1 min — all empirical. Calibration: this settlement's quarterly infection rate from the record sheets; >10% tightens the limits and the local figures go into the ledger (Chapter 43). This card involves no medicine; where analgesia is needed, this chapter gives no dose — follow the Chapter 46 medicine-material cards and trial increments.
5. **Observations to record**: wound grade, irrigation volume, foreign bodies removed (number and kind), stitch count and measured spacing, immediate or delayed closure, number of drain strips — in the "wound" column of the injury record sheet.
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = edges apposed with no inversion, no ooze, dressing dry. Rework = a single inverted stitch or out-of-range spacing (remove that stitch and re-suture); infection signs at delayed closure (44.9.2) → abandon the closure and keep the wound open. Scrap = a grade II contaminated wound sutured immediately (open it within 48 h, monitor throughout, enter as a failure case). **Hard gate**: penetration of a body cavity is outside the capability boundary — cover and transport only.
7. **Common failures and troubleshooting order** (by frequency): (1) throbbing pain + fever on days 2–3 → remove the tightest 1–2 stitches to check for tension necrosis → lift the dressing and check redness and discharge → check for retained foreign body → if fluctuant, drain per 44.9.3; (2) discharge soaking the dressing repeatedly → check whether this wound should have been left open to drain but was sutured; (3) wound edge pale, black, dry → check whether spacing and margin are too tight (remove the tight stitches, reduce tension).
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = 10 stitches on the skin frame, self-assessed against the criteria; L2 = passed the 30-stitch assessment (max–min spacing ≤2 mm, no inversion, each knot 500 g × 1 min) + at least one recorded successful delayed closure; L3 = quarterly infection rate ≤10% archived + ledger transcription + one apprentice brought through the 30-stitch assessment.

## 44.4 Burns and Scalds

### 44.4.1 Area Estimation (Palm Method)

With the casualty's five fingers together, the palm surface of their hand (fingers included) ≈ 1% of their own body surface area. Use **the casualty's own hand** to count off the area. First-degree burns (redness only) are not counted in the burn area.

### 44.4.2 Depth Grading and Treatment Table

| Degree | Observable identification | Treatment | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| First degree | Red, painful, no blisters, blanches under pressure | Clean cold water flush or soak ≥15 min; no oils, no powders; avoid pressure | If first-degree area >20%, manage as a large-area burn with fluid replacement and observation |
| Superficial second degree | Tense full blisters, damp red blister base, severe pain | Cold water ≥15 min; **keep the blister roof** (a natural dressing); high-tension blisters: the medic nicks a 5 mm opening at the lowest point to drain — remove the fluid, keep the skin | Tearing off blister roofs forbidden; applying wood ash, mud, or grease forbidden (contamination + obscures observation) |
| Deep second degree and third degree | Waxy white, scorched yellow, or charred; sensation dulled to absent (nerves destroyed) | Cover with a clean dry dressing, protect from pressure; apply nothing to and peel nothing off the eschar; cut clothing around adherent parts and leave them attached | Circumferential eschar (a full ring constricting a limb or the chest) and area beyond the limit = beyond the boundary (Section 44.4.6) |

### 44.4.3 Cold-Water Procedure (Continues Chapter 6, Fire and Water)

Flush or soak in clean cool water (well water, stream water — **not ice**) for **≥15 min**, with pain cessation or marked relief as the effectiveness criterion. **For large-area burns (>10–15% of body surface), prolonged whole-body drenching is forbidden** — to prevent death from hypothermia: rotate flushing by region, keep the trunk warm as the priority, and watch lip color and shivering while flushing.

### 44.4.4 Blister Management Principles

Keep the roof > drain at a low point > remove the epidermis; of the three options, follow this order. A bare surface where the blister roof has already torn away is treated as deep second degree: dry dressing cover, protect from pressure, daily observation.

### 44.4.5 Steam Scalds Are Worse Than Hot Water (Continues Chapter 28)

Water at 100 ℃ releases only sensible heat as it cools; steam at 100 ℃ releases sensible heat first and then, **as it condenses on skin, its latent heat of vaporization of about 2 260 kJ/kg — more than 5 times the heat released by the same mass of water cooling by 100 ℃ (about 420 kJ/kg)**; pressurized steam (boilers and piping, Chapter 28) is hotter than 100 ℃. Conclusion: for equal contact time, every steam scald is estimated and treated one depth grade deeper; prepare second-degree protocols by default for scalds in the steam engine house.

### 44.4.6 Fluid Replacement Advisory and Beyond-Boundary Cases

For adults with burn area >10% (children >5%), start oral rehydration: 1 L clean water + 3 g salt + 20–30 g sugar (**a burn-rehydration variant; for the master diarrhea-dehydration formula, Section 47.3.3 governs**) (**this is the burn-rehydration variant; for the master diarrhea-dehydration formula, Section 47.3.3 governs**) (weighed and mixed), **small amounts, frequently** — 100–200 mL at a time, once every 15–30 min; the observed items are relief of thirst and maintained urine output (at least once every 4–6 h); gulping a large amount at once causes vomiting and is forbidden. **The following cases are already beyond this chapter's capability; treatment is reduced to warmth, fluid replacement, limited analgesia, accompaniment, and records: adults with area >20%; third degree >5%; face and neck burns with a hoarse voice, singed nasal hairs, or coughing soot-streaked sputum (airway burns); perineal burns; circumferential eschar of a limb or the chest.** Inform the settlement and the injured person's family honestly, and record item by item.

## 44.5 Fractures and Immobilization

### 44.5.1 Identification

Signs of fracture: deformity, abnormal mobility, crepitus noticed incidentally during moving (**never test for it deliberately**), local swelling and pain with refusal to move, visible bone ends in an open wound. Whenever identification is in doubt, immobilize as a fracture — over-fixing is harmless, missed fixation cripples.

### 44.5.2 Closed-Fracture Splinting

General principles: **the splint must extend across the joint above and the joint below the fracture** (joint-spanning fixation; a splint that cannot hold both joints is the same as no fixation); stop bleeding first (Section 44.2), then immobilize; pad the splint's limb-contacting face along its full length with ≥10 mm of padding (cloth, dry grass), thicker over bony prominences.

| Step | Key points and criteria |
|---|---|
| 1 Pad | Padding fills the whole space between splint and skin; double the thickness over bony prominences such as ankle and elbow |
| 2 Splint placement | Forearm: double splints, palm side + back side; lower leg: double splints, inner + outer sides; thigh: a full-length splint fixing together with the hip |
| 3 Tie | Each strap ≥30 mm wide, ≥2 straps; tie the knots on the splint side or in the gaps between bones, never against skin |
| 4 Circulation check | After tying, distal nail-bed press refill ≤2 s; distal purple or numb = re-tie one notch looser |
| 5 Recheck | Check circulation again after 30 min — gradual swelling tightening the straps is the most easily missed failure |

◎ Hand-feel for strap tightness: a flat finger slides in with resistance but can enter. Misjudgment compensation: always defer to the nail-bed refill ≤2 s criterion. Practice method: healthy people strap each other blindfolded and judge tightness, 5 times per quarter.

### 44.5.3 Open Fracture = Contaminated Wound + Immobilization + No Reduction

Why no on-site reduction: first, the exposed bone end is already environmentally contaminated; pushing it back sends dirt straight into the medullary cavity — in an era without antibiotics, close to a death sentence. Second, forced reduction without anesthesia can cut previously intact vessels and nerves on the sharp fracture ends. Treatment sequence: stop bleeding (Section 44.2) → irrigate around the wound → cover the bone end and wound with boiled wet cloth (prevent drying) → splint (including supporting the exposed segment, ring-padded with soft cloth) → record and transport. **Exception clause**: only when transport is extremely long and the bone end is about to push through the skin may the medic, after rinsing it clean, push it back in a single motion and treat the whole course as a Grade Ⅱ contaminated wound left open — this maneuver requires two people present to cross-check, with item-by-item recording.

### 44.5.4 Spinal Injury Transport (Multi-Person Flat Lift)

Suspicion indicators (any one present = treat as a spinal injury): a fall from ≥2 m; a heavy object striking the head, neck, or back; neck or back pain; numbness or weakness of the limbs; a fall casualty who is not fully conscious.

- **One person carrying on the back, in the arms, or on the shoulder is forbidden** — any posture that bends the spine can let bone fragments sever the spinal cord, turning a walkable injury into lifelong paralysis.
- Flat-lift method: ≥3 people kneel on the same side of the casualty (respectively in charge of head-shoulders, waist-hips, and lower legs); the person at the head calls the count, and **the whole body is lifted as one, with the spine kept in a straight line**; the head-neck handler holds the head and neck aligned with the trunk axis; turning the head is strictly forbidden. Lay the casualty onto a rigid board (door plank, thick plank), stuff cloth bundles tight on both sides of the neck, and fix with three straps at chest, hips, and knees.
- Log-roll method (for transport through narrow passages): three people in a line, head-shoulders-hips roll 90° to the side in sync; no twisting of the long axis.

### 44.5.5 Conservative Boundaries for Dislocations

| Site | Within-boundary actions | Do not |
|---|---|---|
| Jaw dislocation (mouth open and cannot close) | Early after injury, while the muscles are not yet rigid: the medic places both thumbs, wrapped in cloth, on the molar region and pushes down and back; if one attempt fails, stop, switch to a soft diet and observe | Repeated forceful reduction attempts |
| Shoulder dislocation | Cloth sling fixed against the chest; 5–7 days after the swelling subsides, with two assistants and sedation per Section 44.9.1, attempt reduction by slow external rotation; if it fails, abandon permanently and compensate with exercise | Immediate violent traction on site |
| Elbow, hip, and cervical spine dislocations | Immobilization only | Never reduce, without exception (high rate of vascular and nerve injury; no means of repair) |

[Card 44-03 Splinting Card] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: 4 planed straight-grained wood strips (Chapter 7; lengths 0.3/0.5/0.8 m, width ≥40 mm, thickness ≥10 mm); padding cloth or dry grass (≥10 mm thick); 4 straps (≥30 mm wide, Chapter 15). Substitutes and losses: split straight bark inner face (needs doubling for thickness), rolled mat sections (forearm only, one grade less support).
2. **Tools required**: no measuring instruments; water clock for the 30 min circulation-recheck timing.
3. **Operating sequence and control points**: ① stop bleeding first, then immobilize; **② the chosen splint length must span the joint above and below the fracture**; ③ full-length padding, thickened over bony prominences; ④ ≥2 straps, knots on the splint side; **⑤ immediately after tying, do the nail-bed refill test (≤2 s), recheck after 30 min**. Open-fracture additions: irrigate around, cover with a wet dressing, no reduction (exception clause requires a two-person cross-check).
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: padding ≥10 mm, strap width ≥30 mm, and refill ≤2 s are empirical values; validate and revise against the quarterly drill race time (immobilization + circulation check ≤5 min) and the missed-recheck rate.
5. **In-process observation items**: injury mechanism, site of deformity, splint specifications, circulation-check times and results, 30 min recheck results — enter in the "immobilization" column of the injury record sheet.
6. **Accept / rework / scrap criteria**: Accept = spans the joints, padding fully laid, circulation passed, no missed recheck. Rework = distal purple or numb (loosen one notch and re-tie); splint not spanning the joints (restart with longer boards). Scrap = transported without a circulation check (deemed an unfinished process step; Iron Law 7).
7. **Common failures and troubleshooting order**: distal swelling and purple color → first check strap tightness → check whether padding has slipped, leaving a bony prominence pressed → check whether the 30 min recheck was missed. Pain sharply worsening after fixation → check straps too tight → check whether the deformed segment has shifted. Open-fracture bone end retracting into the wound → escalate to Grade Ⅱ contaminated-wound treatment and record.
8. **L1/L2/L3 evidence**: L1 = complete the four steps of one immobilization in paired practice between healthy people; L2 = drill race ≤5 min including circulation check, passing 3 times consecutively + one passing three-person flat-lift transport; L3 = quarterly drill records + real-injury-case ledger + 1 apprentice brought through to pass L2.

## 44.6 Eye Injuries

The general principle for eye injuries: **injuring one of a pair of eyes is half-blindness risk; authority for treatment is centralized in the medic; every "let's just try once more to get it out" impulse stops**.

### 44.6.1 Iron Filings and Foreign Bodies in the Eye

- First action: **no rubbing** (rubbing grinds the foreign body into the cornea, forming an ulcer scar).
- Irrigation: wash from a clean water cup or flush with the hanging bag's fine stream; when flushing the eyelid conjunctiva, evert the eyelid (upper-lid eversion method: have the casualty look down, then press the lid margin up and over with tweezers or a thumb).
- Boundary: **superficial loose particles** on the conjunctival sac or the inner eyelid surface (when flushing fails to remove them) may be lifted off with a boiled, moistened cloth corner or cotton swab; **an embedded foreign body on the cornea is never approached with any removal attempt** — a needle instrument mistakenly wounding the cornea means a permanent white scar. Cover with a dry dressing (covering both eyes keeps the healthy eye from dragging the injured eye into movement) and transfer to the medic for observation.
- Forging and grinding workstations (Chapters 19–20) wear mesh guards or wood-framed glass-lens eye protection (glass, Chapter 14); this is part of the procedure itself (Iron Law 7).

### 44.6.2 Chemicals in the Eye (Continues Chapter 23)

- Immediately flush with large volumes of clean water for **≥15 min**, with the injured side lower as the head is tilted (the outflow drains away and does not enter the healthy eye); evert both upper and lower lids to flush the conjunctival fornices; completing the flush time precedes all transport preparation.
- **No "acid-alkali neutralization"** — the neutralization reaction releases heat, amounting to a second burn (Chapter 23). With quicklime in the eye, wipe all powder particles off with dry cloth before flushing.
- After flushing, cover and have the medic observe for 24 h; for corneal damage the observation items are light sensitivity, tearing, and inability to open the eye; record daily.

### 44.6.3 Photophthalmia (Glassworkers and Arc-Type Exposure; Continues Chapter 14)

4–8 h after prolonged direct viewing of molten-glass flames or strong arc light, both eyes develop severe pain, tearing, and light sensitivity. Treatment: rest away from light, cold compresses, light dressing cover without pressure; no rubbing; **most cases resolve on their own within 24–48 h — this is from observation records, and no "effective therapy" is claimed**; if not relieved after 48 h or vision clearly worsens, record and observe long-term as a corneal injury. Prevention: filtered colored glass at furnaces and viewing ports (Chapter 14); continuous direct viewing of the flame ≤10 min.

## 44.7 Carbon Monoxide and Asphyxiation First Aid

### 44.7.1 Ventilation First and Rescuer Protection

CO is colorless and odorless, and what it kills is not only the casualty — **an unprotected rescuer who rushes in is the second casualty, and after that there is no one left to rescue anyone**. Rescuer self-protection is the first process step of this section (the first item of the safety boundary):

1. First open doors and windows and the damper for forced ventilation ≥5 min, then enter: **entry requires a rope, with two people monitoring outside**.
2. A wet cloth over mouth and nose **blocks smoke and dust only; it does not adsorb CO** — it must not be counted as protection.
3. Casualty dragging method: the low posture serves only to avoid smoke in heavy-smoke environments (CO is nearly the same density as air and mixes through the whole room — **crouching low does not protect against CO**); grab the collar or armpits and drag along the ground to a ventilated spot; underground, two people lift and drag.
(A weakening, bluing flame can serve as an **auxiliary warning** — a flame dies at roughly 16–19% oxygen, before humans suffer severe hypoxia; but an open flame risks igniting combustible gas, so it **must not be the sole criterion**; with headache or dizziness, everyone evacuates.)
4. Begin assessment and treatment only once at the ventilated spot — **performing any treatment inside the contaminated environment is burying the first-aider along with the casualty**.

Multiple people with simultaneous headache, nausea, and weakness = an environmental signal, not individual illness: evacuate everyone first, then treat one by one.

### 44.7.2 Hands-Only Assistance for Respiratory Arrest

**This route has no mechanical ventilator and no oxygen cylinders** (external dependency, Class-C list). What bare hands can do is open the airway + **cardiopulmonary resuscitation**:
- Determination: unresponsive + no normal breathing (agonal, sighing breaths count as no breathing) → start compressions immediately;
- **Adult chest compressions**: heel of the palm on the midpoint of the line between the nipples, **100–120/min, depth 5–6 cm**, allow full chest recoil after each compression, minimize interruptions;
- **Compressions : breaths = 30:2** (with two rescuers, one compresses exclusively); those unwilling or untrained in rescue breathing do **continuous compressions only** (still far better than no rescue);
- Children (1–14 years): one or two palms compressing to 1/3 chest depth; infants: two fingers; compression : ventilation 15:2 (two rescuers);
- Boundary stated honestly: this route has **no defibrillation capability**; relieving a reversible cause (asphyxia/hemorrhage/hypothermia/poisoning) is the only path to recovery — relieve the cause with full effort while resuscitating; swap in a fresh rescuer when tired (every 2 min); continue until strength runs out or rescue arrives.

1. Head-tilt, chin-lift airway opening: supine; one hand presses the forehead to tilt the head back while the index and middle fingers of the other hand lift the bony part of the chin upward — the fallen tongue root is the leading cause of airway obstruction in the not fully conscious, and this maneuver alone reestablishes the airway in most cases.
2. Criterion: lean down, listen at the mouth and nose for airflow and watch chest rise; determine within ≤10 s.
3. Breathing but unconscious: place in the recovery position (side-lying, head tilted back, upper leg bent at the knee for support) to prevent suffocation by vomit; recheck every 2–3 min.
4. Mouth-to-mouth breathing is the rescuer's voluntary supplementary measure (one breath every 5 s; pinch the nose, seal fully, visible chest rise = effective); whether to perform it is the rescuer's own judgment of infectious-disease risk — this manual neither mandates nor forbids it. Record the results of sustained hands-only assistance honestly: **for those with no return of spontaneous breathing past one incense stick (about 30 min), the realistic outlook is poor; whether to continue is decided jointly by family and medic and recorded**.

### 44.7.3 Recognizing CO Poisoning

Headache, nausea, weakness, impaired judgment; severe cases: coma and a cherry-red complexion (this sign is unreliable and is not a primary criterion). After removal from the environment, mild cases rest quietly under observation for 24 h (delayed deterioration exists); severe cases follow Section 44.7.2. Recognition records are reported case by case (an epidemiological clue for Chapter 47: clustering at the same furnace or kiln points to a ventilation defect — refer to Chapter 49, occupational health).

## 44.8 Amputation: The Final Boundary

This section begins honestly: **this is the highest-failure-rate operation in the entire chapter**. No anesthesia, no transfusion, no antibiotics — the death risk of a single amputation is reckoned in ten-percent increments. Its decision weighting therefore has exactly one question: **"will not doing it kill?"** — not "can function be preserved", not "does it look frightening". The default when not operating is conservative: dressing changes, drainage, immobilization, records.

### 44.8.1 Indications for Considering Amputation (Two Items; Missing Either = Not Done)

1. **Wet gangrene with infection spreading toward the trunk**: limb blackened, foul-smelling, with exudate; red streaks traveling toward the heart (Section 44.9.2); fever that will not subside — once infection enters the blood, death follows; cutting away the focus is the only way to stop it at its source.
2. **Crush gangrene / devastating injury**: the limb is completely destroyed and without blood supply (macerated, charred), with a clear necrotic boundary; keeping it only produces toxins and infection.

### 44.8.2 Timing Principle: Wait for the Boundary, Do Not Race the Clock

Dry gangrene (dry and black, clear boundary, no odor, no fever) **is not operated on** — conservative waiting for self-separation has better outcomes than the knife in most cases; amputating in haste a limb that "might go bad" converts retainable labor into a certain severe casualty. Only spreading wet gangrene, with fever and red streaks progressing, escalates to a surgical question.

### 44.8.3 Operating Clearance Checklist (Proceed Only When All Are Met; Missing One = Turn to Conservative Care or Transport)

| # | Condition | Criterion |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Instrument sterilization | The full set of knives, saws, forceps, and thread boiled ≥15 min (Chapters 11, 18) |
| 2 | Staffing | 1 operator (medic level 2 or above) + 2 assistants + 1 recorder, ≥4 people present in total |
| 3 | Hemostasis capability | 2 tourniquet sets + forceps and thread capable of ligating vessels; no blood reserve (stated in writing) |
| 4 | Analgesia plan | Alcohol sedation (Section 44.9.1) and strong immobilization (restraints + a wooden bite wedge) prepared |
| 5 | Post-operative care | Dressing-change staffing (≥1 person daily × 14 days), oral rehydration and reinforced rations, record-keeping system |
| 6 | The casualty or guardian consents while lucid and signs the record | Record sheet filed (Chapter 43) |

### 44.8.4 Intra-Operative and Post-Operative Essentials

- Tourniquet on + time marking (same procedure as Section 44.2.3); during the operation **ligate every identifiable vessel one by one** before releasing the tourniquet to check — this is the key control point for reducing post-operative massive hemorrhage.
- The cut plane is chosen in healthy tissue 50–100 mm above the necrotic boundary; skin and deeper layers are separated where possible; bone is cut with a saw; **the wound is always left open, never sutured**, with a loose dressing; on days 3–5, delayed suturing if conditions allow, or leave it to heal by secondary intention.
- For 14 days post-op, record daily: temperature, exudate, odor, red streaks, mental state. Fever + spreading means reopening the assessment (a re-amputation higher up may be needed — explain honestly to the family).

### 44.8.5 Decision and Records

Every case (those operated on and those decided against) goes onto the injury record sheet and is archived under the accident 24 h three-item rule (Section 37.6.2): indication, item-by-item checking of the condition checklist, persons present, outcome. These records are the settlement's only basis for future improvement, and the highest-value samples for the Chapter 47 case data.

## 44.9 Limited Means for Pain and Infection Management

### 44.9.1 Limited Analgesia

| Means | Usage | Boundary and risks |
|---|---|---|
| Immobilization | Good fixation and a quiet environment | First choice; no risk |
| Cold water | Burns, sprains, localized swelling and pain (Section 44.4.3) | Not for those at frostbite risk (the not fully conscious) |
| Alcohol (≥50% distilled spirits, Chapter 23) | Administer orally, diluted with water, to the degree of "relaxed, drowsy, but responsive when called" | **Half-drunk is the limit; fully drunk kills**: excess depresses breathing; vomiting causes aspiration. Nothing by mouth for anyone unconscious; dose and time of every administration recorded |
| Distraction and companionship | Talking with the person during fixation and bandaging, guided breathing on instruction | No risk; formally listed as a step |

Opioids and general anesthetics: **no route provided** (capability boundary table). The effect of all analgesic means is recorded only as observation: "1 h after administration, self-reported pain halved / unchanged"; never write "effective".

### 44.9.2 Recognizing Infection Signs and Escalation Criteria

Observe the wound daily on five items: **redness** (expanding halo at the margins), **swelling**, **heat** (the site hotter than the healthy side), **pain** (throbbing, worsening rest pain), **exudate** (turning yellow-green, foul-smelling). Add two whole-body items: fever (thermometer per Chapter 27, or the back-of-hand comparison method) and **red streaks**.

**Red streaks = red lines running from the wound toward the heart (toward the trunk)** — the danger signal of lymphatic spread; when present together with fever, this is the escalation criterion meaning beyond this chapter's boundary:

| Observation combination | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Local redness, swelling, heat, pain; no fever, no red streaks | Open up the dressing, warm compress (clean hot cloth), immobilize, record daily |
| Deep throbbing pain, fluctuance appears | Incise and drain as a superficial abscess per Section 44.9.3 |
| Red streaks heading toward the trunk, or fever + spreading redness and swelling | **Beyond the boundary**: the only means are more thorough opening (remove sutures, widen drainage) and escalated assessment (Section 44.8); inform honestly and record the course |
| Fever + chills + listlessness | Signs of infection entering the blood: this route has no countermeasure — accompany, rehydrate, record the outcome |

### 44.9.3 Incision and Drainage of Superficial Abscess

Within-boundary requirements: the abscess is **superficial** (palpable under the skin), **fluctuance is definite** (a fluid wave), and it does not sit on a major vessel or nerve trunk (never in the front of the neck, the deep axilla, or the popliteal fossa).

- Incision conditions are the same as the suturing card: instruments boiled, clean water irrigation.
- Steps: rinse the skin → at the point of most obvious fluctuance and lowest droop, incise 10–20 mm along the skin lines → spread the abscess cavity with tweezers, **no deep dissection with fingers** → expel the pus and necrotic debris and rinse clean → loosely pack a gauze drain strip leaving a tail → open dressing changes daily, until granulation fills.
- Record the pus character daily (color, odor, quantity) — an epidemiological clue for Chapter 47.

### 44.9.4 Tetanus: No Solution, Stated Honestly

Days to weeks after a contaminated deep wound (rusty objects, soil, deep splinters), **difficulty opening the mouth (lockjaw), whole-body spasms triggered by the slightest sound or light, and opisthotonos (arched back)** appear — this is tetanus. **This route has no antitoxin and no reliable means of sedation to control the spasms; the outcome is usually death; this chapter states honestly that there is no solution.** The only line of defense is up front: thorough irrigation of contaminated wounds (Section 44.3.2), conservative debridement (Section 44.3.4), **never suturing contaminated wounds immediately** (Section 44.3.5), and open drainage for deep puncture wounds. Early "tightness of the jaw" is easily misdiagnosed as a dental problem — any difficulty opening the mouth after a contaminated deep wound is recorded as a tetanus alert without exception, and the person is isolated somewhere quiet and dark (sound and light trigger spasms). The very existence of this disease is the final reason this chapter sets "less invasion, more opening" as its general principle.

### 44.9.5 Registration Method for Traditional Dressings (Never Write "Effective")

Honey dressing and sugar dressing: honey kept sealed is applied directly to a clean wound surface, or powdered sugar is sprinkled thickly over the wound before covering with a dressing; change daily. **Register as a "traditional observation method"**: for each case record wound size, exudate, granulation color changes, and outcome; conclusions on effect are left to the accumulated records (Chapter 47); this chapter claims no therapeutic efficacy. Honey and sugar that have changed flavor or fermented are forbidden (they are themselves contamination sources). Herbal topical applications follow the same registration method: register the collection location, plant part, preparation method, and observation records; they do not enter the procedures and no efficacy is claimed.

## Failure Modes and Diagnosis

Ordered by most common and hardest to detect. Wound infection is this chapter's typical silent failure: at the time of suturing it "looks fine", and only on day 3 does it reveal itself as throbbing pain and fever.

| Symptom | Candidate cause | Verification method | Treatment | Prevention |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Throbbing wound pain + fever on days 2–3 post-op | Infection (contaminated wound sutured by mistake, insufficient irrigation, retained foreign body) | Treatment sequence: first remove the tightest 1–2 stitches to check for tension necrosis → lift the dressing to check redness, swelling, exudate → check for foreign body → if fluctuance, drain per Section 44.9.3 | Remove sutures and open, drain, record daily; fever + red streaks = escalate (Section 44.9.2) | Grade Ⅱ: immediate suturing forbidden; minimum irrigation volumes |
| Tourniquet limb distal end purple-black and without sensation | **Forgotten application time** (most common fatal error) | Check the hand/forehead and tourniquet markings | Release immediately and switch to pressure; once the necrotic boundary is clear, assess per Section 44.8 | Write the time in two places at application; the time must be reported at every transport handoff |
| Suture line white, black, and dry | Suturing too tight and too dense; ischemic necrosis of the skin edges | Direct inspection + perfusion observation | Remove tight stitches to relieve tension; excise once the necrotic boundary is clear | Minimum margins and intervals; do not suture high-tension wounds |
| Red streaks running from the wound toward the trunk | Lymphangitis; spreading infection | Visually check the direction of travel; take the temperature | Beyond-boundary escalation: open drainage fully, immobilize, record | Open drainage for contaminated wounds |
| Wound exudate turning yellow-green and foul | Deep retained foreign body or liquefying necrotic tissue | Explore with tweezers; fingers forbidden | Widen, drain, extract; record daily | Minimum irrigation volumes; observation after conservative debridement |
| Casualty still down after rescue and the rescuer also dizzy | Rescuer unprotected; second poisoning | Re-check the environment with the flame test | Both withdraw together, then reassess | The Section 44.7.1 sequence must never be inverted |
| Shivering and purple lips after flushing a large burn | Flushing-induced hypothermia | Watch shivering and lip color | Stop flushing, dry, keep warm, rewarm | Above 10–15%, rotate flushing by region |
| Blister fluid turbid and yellow | Infection inside the blister | Direct inspection | Nick at a low point to drain, remove crusts, leave open | Keep the blister roof; apply nothing extraneous |
| Sharp pain increase after splinting, distal swelling | Straps too tight or padding slipped | Nail-bed refill test | Loosen one notch and re-tie; reposition the padding | Never skip the 30 min recheck |
| Weeks after injury: lockjaw, spasms triggered by sound and light | Tetanus (often misdiagnosed as dental disease) | History: contaminated deep wound | Isolate in a dark, quiet place; accompany and record; state honestly that there is no solution | Contaminated wounds left open unsutured; thorough irrigation |

## Safety Boundary

> **Hard gate restated (implementation of the Part declaration)**: wherever the criteria section of a medical card in this chapter marks a situation "beyond the boundary", all action stops; treatment is reduced to warmth / fluid replacement / analgesia / accompaniment / records (see the Chapter 44 Part declaration).

The following items are part of the process steps (Iron Law 7); failure to execute them is deemed failure to complete the treatment:

1. **Rescuer self-protection comes first**: no reaching in while the source is live, no entering while toxic gas lingers, no approaching a machine not yet stopped — the second casualty cannot save the first; the Section 44.7.1 sequence must never be inverted.
2. A tourniquet without a marked time = deemed not hemostased; the application time must be reported at every transport handoff.
3. Wash hands with soap solution before treating any open wound (Chapter 23); on any day the instruments have not been boiled, no suturing and no incision.
4. Nothing by mouth for the drunk or the unconscious (aspiration).
5. Night transport requires at least two people carrying torches (Chapter 6), with the route scouted first.
6. For large-area burns, warmth takes priority over flushing: shivering means stop flushing.
