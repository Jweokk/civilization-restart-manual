### 13.1 Identifying Limestone

Chapter 8 (Section 8.3) made the first limestone registration; this chapter upgrades it to a mineability verdict.

**Appearance and feel**: gray-white to bluish-gray, fine-grained and dense, commonly bedded; hardness about 3 — a bone splint barely scratches it, quartz scratches it easily leaving a white mark (hardness reference set, Chapter 2). Distinguishing it from quartzite and sandstone: quartzite at hardness 7 cannot be scratched; sandstone shows visible grains and does not fizz with acid.

**Acid test — the modern substitute for dilute hydrochloric acid**: local acids ranked by strength — fermented vinegar (the Chapter 10 pickling liquid, or grain vinegar) > sorrel juice > soured fruit pulp. Procedure: drip several drops of the acid onto a fresh fracture surface:

| Reaction | Verdict | Engineering meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Violent fizzing, stopping quickly | high-calcium limestone | first choice: burns fast, slakes fast, best lime |
| Powder fizzes slowly, lumps slowly | dolomitic limestone | usable but slow slaking, less paste; store separately |
| No fizzing | not a carbonate | exclude (may be siliceous rock) |

The acid must be strong enough to make you "wince on tasting" to be sensitive; if it is not concentrated enough, grind the sample to powder and retest (more reaction surface). Test 3 or more rocks from different strata at each deposit — quality can differ widely between layers of the same hill.

**Other calcium sources**: shells and coral (sea and lake regions) — high purity, small pieces burn through easily; an excellent raw material but only worthwhile in bulk. Marble (metamorphosed limestone) — burnable, coarse crystals, slightly lower quality after burning. Oyster shells and eggshells collected piecemeal suffice only for small uses.

**Selection rules**: piece size from fist to head (too big burns through with difficulty, too small blocks the draft); **flint nodules and siliceous bands are rejected** (hard lumps inside the limestone layer — they will not burn through and become impurities); strip the weathered crumbly surface crust. Register deposits in the Chapter 2 format: location, estimated quantity, acid-test results for the three strata, haul distance. Haul distance is the biggest factor in lime economics: stone is heavy, lime is light — and the other reason to choose near over far is that the kiln itself should also be built beside the deposit.

### 13.2 Burning and Slaking Lime

**The chemical chain (three reactions, one picture)**:

> Burning: CaCO₃ (limestone) →(900–1000°C)→ CaO (quicklime) + CO₂↑
> Slaking: CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ (slaked lime), **violently exothermic** — it can boil the water
> Carbonation: Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ from the air → CaCO₃ + H₂O — the principle behind mortar hardening: **the lime paste is "turning back into stone"**

**Burning**: use the Chapter 12 shaft furnace or a dedicated lime kiln (stone and fuel loaded in alternating layers: a layer of charcoal, a layer of stone, each layer about a palm thick; continuous firing for 1–2 days, experience value, must be field-calibrated — the bigger the lumps, the longer the high-temperature hold). Judge the temperature with the slump-cone set or limestone test pieces (the 900–1000°C band).

**Three-state burn table (piece-by-piece inspection at kiln draw)**:

| State | Appearance and fracture | Weight feel | Disposition |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Properly burned** | fracture uniformly white or gray-white, porous | distinctly lighter | into the slaking line |
| **Underburned (under-fired)** | white rim on the fracture, gray dense **hard core** at the center; or the whole lump keeps its original color | on the heavy side | reburn (hard-cored lumps must not enter the feed pile) |
| **Overburned (over-fired)** | fracture porcelainized and shiny, texture harder and denser ("dead-burned") | heavy and hard | sluggish reaction to a drip of water — slakes extremely slowly, low paste yield; **store separately for downgrade use** (can be ground fine as filler; expect no reactivity) |

**Three criteria of proper burn** (confirming properly burned): distinctly lighter; fracture uniformly white and porous with no hard core; a drip of water on the fresh fracture vaporizes violently with heat. **Countermeasure for an overburned kiln load**: enter it in the kiln-load archive; next kiln lower the temperature grade or shorten the time — consecutive overburns mean the firing rhythm is systematically too long.

**Slaking — the highest-hazard operation of this chapter**. The injury mechanism first: quicklime dry powder meets skin sweat and burns exothermically on contact; in the eyes it can blind; slaking throws boiling water and lime splashes. Protection: full body covering (close-woven hemp clothing), a mesh-face veil or a wide brim + hanging curtain, oil-soaked leather gloves (15.6), thick-soled shoes; stand upwind.

**Slaking procedure (safety order, never reversed)**:

1. A large pit or wooden trough first holds ample water (water ≥3–5× the volume of quicklime, experience value, must be field-calibrated).
2. Quicklime goes in **in batches** (one basket at a time), **sliding slowly into the water** along the pit edge — never splash a small amount of water onto the lime (eruptive boiling and splashing), and never dump it all at once.
3. Stir with a wooden stick; rising steam during the violent reaction is normal; keep more than one arm's length away.
4. When there is no more steam and all lumps have dispersed into a paste, add the next batch.
5. Split the product: thick paste (lime paste) — scrape into a storage pit and cover with clean water (the water seal keeps air out; keeps for months; **age 7 days or more before use** — the older the better, experience value, must be field-calibrated; fresh paste needed in a hurry runs "hot" and plastered surfaces crack and blister); thin milk (milk of lime) — for whitewashing and mixing.

**Quicklime storage**: sealed against moisture (pottery urn with lid + mud seal, or in a dry shed on straw over planks) — quicklime that drinks the air's moisture silently fails into slaked powder. Dry slaked powder (made by the spray-and-turn method) stores and hauls well, but often carries unslaked cores (a later curse: lime pops on the wall); **wherever aging can be afforded, always age it as paste**.

**Field quick-identification table (against mis-buying and mis-use)**:

| Material | Appearance | Drip of water | Use verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Quicklime lumps | light, white or gray-white, porous fracture | violent vaporization | raw material awaiting slaking; forbidden directly on a wall |
| Dry slaked powder | white powder | slightly warm | emergency masonry; first re-check for hard grains |
| Lime paste (aged) | putty-like, kept under water | — | first choice for plastering and mixing |
| Underburned lumps | heavy, gray dense hard core in the fracture | no reaction | reburn; must not enter the feed pile |

[Card 13-01 Slaking Quicklime] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: properly burned lime lumps (three checks: noticeably lighter, uniformly white and friable on a fresh break with no hard core, water dripped on a fresh break hisses and boils); water at ≥3–5 times the lime volume. **Under-burned white cores must be picked out and returned to the kiln** — buried in the slaking pit they become the "pops" that burst out of a wall months later.
2. **Tools required**: large pit or wooden trough (sized to the batch), wooden paddle, bamboo baskets (batch charging), clay jar for storing unslaked lime (lidded, clay-sealed). Calibration source: water measured by volume with the Chapter 3 standard measure.
3. **Sequence and control points**: **fill the water first** → slide lime in by the basketful **slowly along the pit edge** → stir, keeping an arm's length away → add the next basket only when steam stops and the lumps have fully broken down → split the product (thick slurry raked into a storage pit under water). Control points: water must be in place before any lime goes in; **never throw a little water onto lime** (violent boiling and spatter) and never dump a whole charge at once — these two are the main cause of injuries in this chapter.
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: water ≥3–5 times lime volume (empirical, calibrate on site); age the putty at least 7 days before use, longer is better; storage pit must be water-sealed against air and keeps for months. **Over-burned lumps** (vitrified and glossy on the break, heavy and hard) slake very slowly with low paste yield — stack separately and downgrade, never mix into the main batch.
5. **Observations to record**: per kiln run — lime mass / water added / slaking duration / how long steam lasted / paste yield / number of hard cores removed / date put into ageing / date used.
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = all lumps broken down to slurry with no hard grains; rework = hard grains present (under-burned lumps got in), screen them out and log the kiln run; scrap = a batch that does not react at all (not limestone, or badly over-burned) — never put it on a wall; downgrade it to fill.
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) violent boiling and spatter during slaking → water was not in place first or charging was too fast; stop charging, add water, restart; (2) slow slaking and low yield → high proportion of over-burned lumps, check the kiln log; (3) hard grains in the paste → under-burned lumps; (4) wall blisters and pops months later → incomplete slaking, the source was hard cores; the plaster has to be cut out and redone.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one slaking run plus yield record; L2 = three consecutive runs with comparable yield and hard-core rate, plus one plastered surface using 7-day-aged putty that passes acceptance; L3 = a second person completes a run from this card alone, with yields within 10% of each other.

### 13.3 Clay, Sand, and Mineral Fillers

**Sand**: river or hill sand both work, but they must be: ① **graded** — coarse, medium, and fine together (grab a handful and look: all-one-size sand has big voids and low strength; half coarse half fine is best — grade by the settling-cylinder method, then blend by standard-measure volume); ② **washed** — excess silt is fatal (wash by settling until the panning water runs clear, or clay films coat the grains and strength collapses); ③ angular sand (crushed rock) interlocks better than rounded river sand and gives higher strength at the same ratio, but poorer workability — use more paste.

**Clay**: clay in mortar is capped (≤10% by volume, experience value, must be field-calibrated) — in excess it dries and cracks; clay has its own proper trades: adobe brick (13.8) and rammed foundations.

**Pozzolanic materials (the key to upgrading air-hardening to hydraulic)**: lime + water hardens only in air (air-hardening); underwater or in permanent damp it does not harden. Once "pozzolanic materials" are added, the hydraulic components they form keep hardening in water. Natural sources: volcanic ash, tuff, pumice (register in volcanic regions, Section 8.3 entry). **The standard substitute where there are no volcanoes: calcined clay powder** — crushed brick, crushed pottery, and underfired pottery shards pounded and sieved (finer than the sand); its reactivity comes from the calcination decomposition of clay minerals — the ancient "lime + crushed brick powder" mortar works on exactly this principle, and it is this book's mainline recipe.

**Fillers**: crushed stone (large aggregate for base courses, ≤fist size); grass fiber (cut grass mixed into mud, anti-crack for earthen walls).

**Separate piles, no mixing**: lime paste pit, sand pile, calcined clay powder urns, crushed stone pile — separate zones and signs (Section 8.4 pigment marks) — mortar mixed at the wrong ratio is worse than no plaster at all.

### 13.4 Basic Mortars and Masonry Structures

**Two mix classes (measured in volume with the Chapter 3 standard dry measure; all are experience-value starting points, must be field-calibrated — every batch goes into the ledger)**:

| Mortar | Ratio (volume) | Uses | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Air-hardening lime mortar | lime paste : sand = 1:2 to 1:3 | above-grade masonry, plastering | never underwater, never permanently damp |
| Hydraulic mortar | lime paste : calcined clay powder : sand = 1:1:2 to 1:2:3 | foundations, floors, water channels, kiln bases | slow strength development; no early loading |

**Workability criterion**: holds as a blob without slumping, does not slide off the trowel, strikes smooth without tearing — too wet, add sand; too stiff, add paste; **adding water freely on site is forbidden** (extra water = more shrinkage = cracks).

**Hardening and curing**: carbonation proceeds slowly from the surface inward — days to first hardness at the surface, weeks to months in the depths. Curing = staying moist through the hardening period: sprinkle water and cover with straw on plaster and masonry for days to two weeks; racing a large plastering job under sun and wind = shrinkage cracking. **Stop work during freezing** (ice expansion bursts the mortar).

**Masonry processes**:

- Stone masonry: lay with the large face down, bedded in mortar; **stagger the joints** — vertical joints offset ≥10 cm between courses; every few stones add one "through stone" spanning the full wall thickness to keep the two faces together; fill the joints full and strike-and-press them as you lay (against water-seeping joints).
- Brick masonry (11.8 fired brick): overlap ≥1/4 brick length; joints 1–2 fingers wide; strike the joints on fair-faced sides.
- Adobe masonry: laid in mud mortar, coated outside with a lime mortar protective layer (adobe must not take direct rain).

**Foundations**: dig to firm subsoil (in cold regions dig past the winter frost depth — frost heave will lift the foundation); stone foundation ≥30 cm above grade (against splash and capillary water — capillary rise is the number-one moisture path into a wall); foundation width ≥1.5× wall thickness (experience value, must be field-calibrated).

**Acceptance**: mortar test blocks (pressed in a wooden mold as 5 cm cubes, cured with the job's own batch) — at 7 days they survive breaking by hand; at 30 days a knife cannot scratch them; masonry spot checks: pry one stone off and check the bedded-mortar face ≥80% (experiential criterion); splash a ladle of water on the finished wall — no damp mark on the back.

**Common-mistakes table (mandatory checks in the construction log)**:

| Mistake | Consequence | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Adding water freely on site to restore workability | high shrinkage, sharp strength loss | downgrade the batch to fill layers; mix afresh |
| Plastering large areas with unaged fresh paste | blistering and cracking | strip and re-plaster; return the paste to the pit to age 7 days or more |
| Striking joints once and never re-pressing | joints dry-crack and seep | second compaction and strike before initial set |
| Rushing work during the freeze | mortar frozen crumbly, spalls in spring | stop work; strip the frozen mortar |
| Adobe walls left bare without plaster | rain erosion and spalling | add the protective coat and check capillary water at the foundation |

**Mortar process card template (one card per job batch, Section 8.7 format)**: date / batch number | use | ratio (measures: lime paste — calcined clay powder — sand) | water description (workability criterion) | mixing method | weather that day | test-block numbers and 7-day, 30-day results | masonry location | anomaly record. A missing entry voids the card — disputes over mortar quality are finally adjudicated by this card.

**Three-part earth (rammed lime + sand + clay, the mainstay for floors and foundation reinforcement)**:

- Ratio (volume, experience-value starting point, must be calibrated): lime paste 1 : sand 2 : clay 2 (or replace part of the clay with crushed brick powder to add hydraulicity);
- Mixing: mix the dry materials evenly, then add water to the "squeezes into a lump that holds, drops and blossoms" grade (◎ hand-feel; calibration: squeeze a handful hard — a lump that holds without weeping water is the moisture ceiling; one that falls apart on landing is the floor);
- **Ramming discipline (the soul of this process)**: place in layers, each layer loose-laid **half a palm thick**, compacting to about two-thirds; **ram each layer 3–5 passes** (experience value, must be calibrated); finish a layer on two criteria — ① **paste blooming at the surface** (ramming squeezes lime paste out of the surface into a film) ② **further ramming no longer sinks it** (a hard heel-stomp shows only slight spring). Both criteria met = lay the next layer; either missing = ram more;
- Cure as for mortar (sprinkle water and cover with straw, no baking sun); walking on it the next day is fine; heavy loads (wheels, machine bases) wait 7–30 days;
- Acceptance: probe and scrape with the tip of an iron rod (the Chapter 16 iron rod or a hardwood rod) — a white mark only, with no crumbling, passes; crumbling or powdering means rework and re-ram.

[Card 13-02 Mortar Test Cubes and Masonry Acceptance] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: lime putty aged ≥7 days; sand passing the 8.2 settling test with low clay content; burnt-clay powder (for hydraulic mixes); water. **Clay in the sand is the hidden cause of mortar cracking** — wash sand that fails the settling test before use.
2. **Tools required**: standard measure (Chapter 3, for volume ratios), cube molds (wooden frames, n ≥ 3 per mix), scored rule, plumb line, trowel. Calibration source: ratios measured by volume with the same measure; strength judged coarsely by whether cubes crack (no press available); wall plumb measured with a plumb line.
3. **Sequence and control points**: measure by ratio → mix to workability → cast cubes (n ≥ 3 per mix) → cure (straw cover, sprinkling, several days) → lay masonry with checks as you go → accept. Control points: **adding water on site at will is forbidden** (more water = more drying shrinkage = cracks); every batch goes into the log, no "adjusting the mix by feel".
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: air-hardening lime mortar, putty:sand = 1:2 to 1:3 (above-ground masonry and plaster; never underwater, never permanently damp); hydraulic mortar, putty:burnt clay:sand = 1:1:2 to 1:2:3 (foundations, floors, channels, kiln bases; strength develops slowly, no early loading). **Workability criteria**: forms a ball that does not crumble, hangs on the trowel without running off, and trowels to a finish without tearing. Masonry: stone joints staggered ≥10 cm, brick stagger ≥1/4 brick length, joint width 1–2 fingers, footing ≥30 cm above grade; wall plumb deviation <1 finger per rod height (empirical).
5. **Observations to record**: per batch — mortar type / ratio / sand source and clay judgment / workability notes / cube count and curing days / whether cubes cracked / masonry checks (stagger, joint width, plumb).
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = cubes uncracked after curing, stagger and joint width compliant, plumb within tolerance; rework = workability off (too wet add sand, too stiff add paste — **not water**); scrap = cubes crack and still crack after adjusting ratio and sand (sand too clayey or putty spent).
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) cubes crack → check water content first, then sand clay content, then whether curing was interrupted; (2) mortar never hardens → putty proportion too low, or carbonation is starved of moisture (must stay damp while hardening); (3) plaster tears when trowelling → sand too coarse or mix too lean; (4) wall leans → layout or laying was not plumbed; the further it goes the worse, so dismantle early.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one batch of cubes plus one masonry acceptance record; L2 = one air-hardening and one hydraulic batch, with cube results comparable to their ratios, and both masonry sections accepted; L3 = a second person mixes and accepts from this card alone, and both read the same wall's plumb within half a finger.

[Card 13-03 Rammed Lime-Sand-Clay (Sanhetu)] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: lime putty, sand, clay (volume 1:2:2; crushed brick dust may replace part of the clay to add hydraulic character); water. Clay must pass the 8.2 three tests; sandy clay calls for proportionally less sand.
2. **Tools required**: tamper (wood or stone, weight matched to crew), formwork for edges, standard measure (Chapter 3), iron or hardwood probe (for acceptance scraping), watering can.
3. **Sequence and control points**: dry-mix thoroughly → add water to the "clench into a ball, shatters on dropping" stage → spread in layers (**half a palm thick loose**) → 3–5 tamper passes per layer → next layer only when both criteria are met. Control points: **loose-layer thickness and tamper passes**; moisture judged by the clench test (a ball that holds without seeping is the upper limit, one that shatters on dropping is the lower), not by feel alone.
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: ratio putty:sand:clay = 1:2:2 (empirical starting point, calibrate on site); loose layer half a palm, compacting to about two-thirds; 3–5 tamper passes per layer. **Two finishing criteria**: (1) slurry rises to the surface as a film; (2) further tamping produces no more settlement (a firm heel press shows only slight spring). **Missing either one, tamp more — do not spread the next layer.**
5. **Observations to record**: per area — ratio / moisture stage / loose thickness / passes per layer / whether slurry rose / whether settlement continued / curing days.
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = both criteria met and the probe scrapes a **white mark with no crumbling**; rework = crumbling or dusting (under-tamped or wrong moisture), tamp more or lift and re-spread; scrap = a whole layer too loose to recover (moisture out of control), remove and redo.
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) surface skins and crumbles → check tamper passes first, then whether moisture was short; (2) never firms up, keeps settling → too wet or layer too thick, thin the layers; (3) cracks on drying → too much clay or curing interrupted; (4) soft spots → sub-layer not compacted, open it up and redo that area, do not just press the surface.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one floor area (about one room) plus acceptance record; L2 = three areas with different ratios or clay sources, with results comparable to their mixes; L3 = a second person tamps one area from this card alone and passes the probe test, with both judging "slurry risen" the same way.

### 13.5 How to Build the Workshop, Storeroom, and Kiln Shed

**Three planning principles** (before the first spade of earth):

1. **Process flow line**: raw-material entry → work area → semi-finished area → finished-goods store, laid out in one line, no crossings, no backtracking.
2. **Hazard separation**: fire-use buildings (kiln shed, furnace house) ≥15 paces from other buildings (experience value, must be field-calibrated) and downwind; the fuel store another ≥8 paces from the kiln.
3. **Water-fire separation**: water convenient but never flooding — storeroom floors above the surroundings; water channels do not cut through building groups.

**Three standard units**:

| Building | Size (experience value) | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| Workshop | clear inside about 5×4 paces, wall height one person plus a hand (about 2.2 m) | side windows for daylight (side light, Section 9.3 principle); rammed earth floor + lime paste surface; benches along the walls (9.3 spec); tool wall (9.6 tool-box station) |
| Storeroom | sized to stock, usually from 4×3 paces | raised wood floor (1 cubit off the ground) + low-in high-out vents; sorted shelving; desiccant (straw bags + ash bags, changed each season) |
| Kiln shed | rain cover only, open on all four sides | **never enclosed** — ventilation against CO (6.8); shed posts footed in refractory brick; segregated fuel zone |

**Nine construction steps**:

1. Siting: high ground, good drainage, near materials and water, downwind of the housing area.
2. Setting out: 3-4-5 rope for right angles (3.2), Type-A level frame for level (3.3), stakes and lines.
3. Foundation excavation: to firm subsoil, one palm wider than the wall line.
4. Stone foundation: bedded masonry, 30 cm+ above grade.
5. Walls: brick/stone/adobe laid per 13.4; reserve lintel positions at doorways (a large stone slab or hardwood beam, ≥ one palm of bearing at each end).
6. Roof frame: ridge beam + rafters, joinery + lashing (7.5); whole-truss erection with the tripod (7.7).
7. Roof: thatch (two fingers thick, compacted, steep for runoff) or 11.8 tile (overlap ≥1/3); eaves overhang 1–2 palms beyond the wall.
8. Ground and apron: indoor floor rammed + lime paste surface; outdoor grade sloped outward all around.
9. Acceptance: walls plumb (plumb line: deviation <1 finger per rod height, experience value); roof pour test does not leak; floor shows no rising damp.

[Card 13-04 Nine-Step Building Procedure] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: stone, brick, or adobe; lime mortar or sanhetu as the element requires; timber (roof frame, lintels, rafters); thatch or tile; water.
2. **Tools required**: 3-4-5 cord (layout), A-frame level (Chapter 3), stakes and line, tamper, trowel, plumb line, tripod (for raising the roof frame). Calibration source: square and level all trace back to the Chapter 3 cord and level — never "looks straight".
3. **Sequence and control points (nine steps, none skippable)**: (1) siting — high ground, good drainage, near materials and water, downwind of living quarters; (2) layout — 3-4-5 cord for square, level frame, stake the lines; (3) excavation — down to firm soil, one palm wider than the wall line; (4) stone footing — bedded in mortar, at least 30 cm above grade; (5) walls — per 13.4; leave lintel seats at openings, with stone or hardwood lintels bearing ≥one palm at each end; (6) roof frame — ridge beam and rafters, mortised and lashed, raised as a unit with a tripod; (7) roofing — thatch two fingers thick, weighted, steep for drainage; tile overlap ≥1/3; eaves overhang 1–2 palms; (8) floors and apron — compacted with a mortar surface inside, graded away from the building outside; (9) acceptance. Control points: **footing height in step 4 and lintel bearing in step 5** — skip either and you will be back to fix it.
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: wall plumb deviation <1 finger per rod height (plumb line, empirical); thatch two fingers thick, tile overlap ≥1/3; eaves 1–2 palms; fire-using buildings ≥15 paces from others, fuel store a further ≥8 paces (double in windy areas).
5. **Observations to record**: step by step — siting rationale / diagonal difference at layout / measured footing height / wall plumb readings / lintel bearing length / roof water-pour test / floor dampness.
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = plumb within tolerance, roof holds the pour test, no rising damp in the floor; rework = wall out of plumb (correct locally before dismantling becomes necessary; beyond 1 finger per rod, take it down), roof leaks (increase overlap or thickness); scrap = footing never reached firm soil and the building settles as a whole (underpin or rebuild).
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) wall leans → check the layout diagonals first, then whether each course was plumbed; (2) wall base damp → footing too low or no capillary break (2–3 finger pebble layer or tar coating); (3) roof leaks → overlap short or pitch too shallow; (4) cracking above an opening → lintel bearing short or no lintel at all.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one small building (storeroom class) built through the nine steps with records; L2 = three buildings of different use (workshop, storeroom, kiln shed) all accepted; L3 = a second person runs a build from this card alone and passes acceptance.

### 13.6 Water Channels, Drainage, and Basic Water Supply

**Gradient**: the open-channel gravity-flow slope rule of thumb — about 1 finger of drop per 10 paces of length (a comparative rule, propagated with the Section 3.3 method: Type-A level frame + rods checked segment by segment). Too steep scours the channel bed, too flat silts up; both destroy the channel.

**Three channel grades**: earth channel (rammed, clear water, short distance); stone-lined, plastered channel (hydraulic lime mortar, the 1:1:2 recipe); clay-pipe channel (11.8 clay pipe, bell-and-spigot joints wrapped with oakum and mortared, laid in shallow trenches against frost and crushing). A **sand-settling basin** at the channel head (dig a pit to let the silt settle first), a dividing gate at the tail (insertable stone slabs).

**Building drainage**: a ring ditch around every building — 1 pace from the wall, half a cubit deep, sloped outward toward low ground; stone paving where the eaves drip; the apron carries wall-base water away from the foundation. **A soaked foundation is cause number one of uneven settlement** (13.7) — drainage ditches are not optional decoration.

**Three sources of basic supply**:

- Spring capture: build a collection basin at the spring mouth (plastered against seepage); if above the point of use, run bamboo pipe (nodes knocked through) or clay pipe by gravity; each pipe segment pitched like the channel.
- Well: siting reads two things — places where puddles linger after rain or water-loving plants grow lush; surface water where animals move about normally. Dig to the water-bearing layer, lining with wooden framing as you go (against collapse); well mouth half a cubit above ground + a cover (against filth). First water judgment: clear, no odor, no strange foam after boiling — **all drinking water must be boiled** (the Chapter 6 rule).
- Rainwater capture: bamboo gutters under the eaves, into pottery urns or plastered cisterns, covered against insects.

**Rough filtration (removes turbidity, not microbes)**: filter bucket in layers — small stones at the bottom, coarse sand in the middle, fine sand and crushed charcoal (broken from fully fired charcoal) on top; the output still must be boiled. Wash the filter and change the sand every ten days (against clogging and stink).

### 13.7 How to Keep Buildings Dry, Un-cracked, and Stable

**Three anti-damp moves**:

1. Cut capillary water: stone foundation + damp-proof course — a pebble layer (2–3 fingers thick) on top of the foundation before walling; or a tar coating on the foundation top (8.5) before building.
2. Block the rain: eave overhangs, lime plaster on the walls (checked and touched up before every rainy season), plinth (an extra thick coat at the outer wall base).
3. Ventilate the moisture out: raised floors, through-vents, ash moisture absorbers in the stores, changed each season.

**Three anti-crack checks**:

- Settlement cracks (large diagonal cracks): foundation soft-hard uneven — replace soft soil with compacted crushed fill, widen the foundation; for existing cracks, observe first (wipe a thin coat of paste over the crack and record the date; continued growth means it is still settling — reinforce; repair only after it stabilizes).
- Shrinkage cracks (fine network): inadequate mortar curing, or adobe dried too fast in the sun — enforce the 13.4 curing and shade-dry the adobe.
- Thermal cracks (regular vertical cracks): long walls expand and contract — leave a finger-width joint every 10–15 paces of long wall, filled with compressible straw-mud, covered by the surface layer.

**Anti-instability rules**:

- Height-to-thickness caps: adobe wall height ≤6× wall thickness; brick/stone ≤10× (experience values, must be field-calibrated). Beyond the cap add pilasters (one every 5 paces, projecting half the wall thickness).
- Cross-wall ties: long buildings get a cross wall or brace every 8–10 paces, forming a skeleton.
- Roof thrust: both thatch and tile roofs push the walls apart — lash the truss bottom chord (horizontal tie beam) between the two eaves walls to cancel the thrust; if not used, add diagonal braces on both sides.
- Opening limits: door and window openings ≤2× wall thickness wide, ≥3 chi from the corners; a lintel is mandatory over every opening.

**Insects and fire**: in termite country all timber sits on stone footings (wood never touches soil), sill members smoked + tar-coated feet (7.8 damp-proof oil); where a flue passes through the roof, pack thick mud-lime ≥3 fingers around it (6.8 fire rules); stoves ≥2 chi from any timber.

**Ventilation, smoke, and fire response (a section newly established in this second edition — the hardware face of building hygiene, two sides of one sheet with the sanitation engineering of Chapter 45)**:

- **Dwelling and workshop ventilation**: every room at least **two diagonal vents** (low-in high-out, same logic as 12.1), with adjustable opening (wooden slide board); criterion: a wet cloth hung up dries within a day, brazier smoke does not gather overhead. **Winter heating with a sealed room + insufficient ventilation = carbon monoxide buildup** — any indoor brazier must have a flue or a direct-draft opening, and **all live braziers are moved out at bedtime** (the building version of the 6.8 Iron Rule);
- **Furnace house and kiln shed smoke**: the kiln shed is open on all four sides (13.5); a walled furnace house gets a high smoke outlet + a low makeup-air inlet, outlet area ≥ furnace mouth area (experience value); flues cleaned of soot on schedule (soot carbon = fire fuel);
- **Three-tier fire response** (per building zone):
  1. **Water**: one covering-water vat per zone (half-buried against winter freezing), long-handled dipper;
  2. **Sand**: one pit of dry sand + wooden shovel at each furnace area and fuel store (no water on oil fires or metal fires);
  3. **Hook**: long-pole iron hook (drag burning material apart, pull down dangerous racks);
- **Fire-separation discipline** (the 15 paces / 8 paces of 13.5 are minimums; double them in windy regions); **fire alarm signal** (a bell struck in continuous rings) and night evacuation routes drilled once per season;
- **Fuel store red lines**: hay and firewood stacks capped at person height (tall stacks self-ignite), 3 paces between stacks, ventilated against damp (internal fermentation heating of a damp stack is where smoldering starts).

[Card 13-05 Three-Tier Fire Equipment and Separation Distances] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: a covered water jar per zone (half-buried for winter frost), long-handled dipper, dry sand (one pit at the furnace area and one at the fuel store), wooden shovel, long-handled iron hook (evolved from the Chapter 16 probe).
2. **Tools required**: the equipment above; a bell or noise-maker for the alarm; marked evacuation routes. Calibration source: equipment is allocated per building zone, not per headcount.
3. **Sequence and control points (fit-out and drill)**: fit all three tiers per zone — **water** (covered jar plus long-handled dipper), **sand** (dry sand pit plus shovel; oil and metal fires must not be fought with water), **hook** (long-handled iron hook to drag burning material apart and pull down unsafe racks) → verify separation distances → agree the alarm signal (bell rung in continuous strokes) and the night evacuation route → **drill once a quarter**. Control points: all three tiers must be present in the same zone; missing one means none.
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: minimum separation — fire-using buildings ≥15 paces from other buildings, fuel store a further ≥8 paces, **double in windy areas**; fuel-store red lines — hay and firewood stacks no taller than a person (tall stacks self-heat), stacks 3 paces apart, ventilated and dry (damp stacks ferment and self-heat into smouldering); ventilation — at least two diagonal vents per room (low in, high out) with adjustable shutters; furnace-room flue area ≥ the furnace mouth area (empirical); where a flue passes through a roof, wrap it in mud plaster ≥3 fingers thick.
5. **Observations to record**: per zone — three-tier inventory and counts / inspection date / water level / whether sand is dry / whether the hook moves freely / drill date and time taken / measured separation distances.
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = all three tiers serviceable, distances met, quarter's drill done; rework = any tier missing or failed (dry jar, damp sand, seized hook) — correct within a set deadline; scrap = distances cannot be met and cannot be altered (relocate the building or downgrade it to non-fire use).
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) jar empty during a fire → the inspection routine was never enforced; move to a monthly water-level check; (2) oil fire spreads when water is thrown on it → sand pit too far or nobody knew to use sand; drills must cover which fire takes which agent; (3) stack self-ignites → stack over height or damp inside, break it down and dry it; (4) charring where the flue crosses the roof → mud wrap under 3 fingers.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one zone fitted out plus inventory; L2 = all zones fitted and one drill completed with timing recorded; L3 = a second person fits out and runs a drill in one zone from this card alone, and both measure the same separation distances.

### 13.8 From Temporary Shelter to Stable Production Site

**Three building tiers**: camp shelter (poles + thatch, 1–7 days, rain cover and sleep) → seasonal work building (adobe + thatch roof, 1–3 years, seasonal cover) → permanent workshop (stone foundation + brick walls + tile roof + hardened floor, 10 years+, protects assets).

**Upgrade trigger criteria (when a permanent building is worth the investment) — any two of the three = break ground**:

1. The process has run stably for ≥1 year (reached that chapter's L2 acceptance).
2. Equipment and stock are valuable (kiln, seed grain, tool chests) — one soaking would cost more than a tenth of the build cost.
3. The process is clearly weather-bound (rainy-season downtime already visible in the ledger).

**Master-plan zoning** (ordered by prevailing wind and hazard):

| Zone | Placement |
|---|---|
| Housing | upwind |
| Workshops | flank of the housing area |
| Storage | dry high ground, near the workshops |
| Kilns | downwind, ≥30 paces from housing (experience value), ≥15 paces between fire-use buildings |
| Livestock pens | downwind periphery ≥20 paces (10.7) |
| Water | bring water in, drain water out, water never passes through the stores |

**Hazardous-materials hut**: quicklime, strong lye, and hay fuel stored in separate huts, red-stripe marks (8.4), ventilated, moisture-proof, locked.

**Investment order (production assets first, comfort later)**: dry storeroom → kiln shed → workshop → livestock pens → housing upgrades. Logic: one soaking of seed grain and tools costs more than a store room.

**Building ledger**: for every structure record build date, material quantities, labor, and repair history; annual roof inspection (before the rainy season), annual foundation inspection (settlement observation points: scored lines at the wall bases for alignment).

### 13.9 Four Topics of Building Physics (completed in C6)

**Stack effect calculation**: draft ∝ stack height × (flue temperature − outside temperature) / outside absolute temperature — used comparatively: double the height, double the draft; comparing ventilation between two rooms compares only the "height difference × temperature difference" product. Outlet-to-inlet area ratio ≥1.5:1 (bigger outlet, smaller inlet, enough pressure differential).
**Wall thermal inertia**: doubling the wall thickness of the same material roughly halves the indoor temperature swing (experiential comparison: adobe 40 cm ≈ the ±3 °C daily-swing grade); choose wall thickness by asking "flatten the day-night swing, or warm up fast" — thick walls for dwellings, thin walls for day-use workshops.
**Window-to-floor ratio**: daylight criterion 1:8–1:10 (window area / floor area; workshops take 1:6); place windows high on the south, low on the north (northern hemisphere), side windows above working sightlines against glare.
**Fire compartments**: one brick/earth fire wall per 15 paces of building length (rising ≥2 fingers above the roof); between stores and furnace buildings use an open yard as the compartment; each zone has ≥2 independent escape openings (drawing on the 13.7 three-tier fire response).

## Safety Boundaries

- The lime cycle end to end is this chapter's hazard number one: slaking must be "lime into plenty of water," the reverse is strictly forbidden; on slaking and lime-sieving days keep the wind down and children away; work starts only with the full protective equipment (15.6); lime burns: flush immediately with large quantities of clean water for 15 minutes or more (the 6.8 burn protocol), sweep the dry powder off before flushing, never cover with earth or ash.
- Deep excavation (deeper than one person) must be benched/sloped or framed with timber; no soil piles and no standing within 1 pace of the trench edge.
- Scaffolding and lifting execute the 1.5× static load validation of 7.4; roof work is roped and anchored; no going up on wet thatch or in the rain.
- Kiln sheds are never enclosed; flues through the roof are packed with mud coating; kiln-fuel separation executes 13.5.
- Well mouths and cellar mouths (10.6) are always covered; enter any long-closed underground space only after ventilating + the torch oxygen check.

## Acceptance Criteria

- **L1 proof-of-principle**: one limestone acid-test verdict; one kiln load of quicklime burned with the drip test confirming activity; one safe slaking producing lime paste.
- **L2 repeatable manufacture**: two kilns in a row with burn-through rate ≥90% (share of lumps without a hard core, experience value, must be field-calibrated); both mortar classes passing test-block acceptance (unbroken at 7 days, knife-proof at 30 days); one stone wall and one brick wall built, bedded-mortar spot check ≥80%; one building through the nine construction steps passing all three checks (plumb, pour test, dry floor); drainage ditches effective in the rainy season with no standing water.
- **L3 sustainable production**: lime paste under year-round water-seal storage with a running monthly production account; mortar ratio ledger reproducible (a second person mixes same-grade mortar from the ledger); the three unit buildings (workshop / storeroom / kiln shed) completed and in use by their processes for a full year with no structural damage; settlement observation and annual inspection running; master-plan zoning complete, hazardous materials stored separately.
