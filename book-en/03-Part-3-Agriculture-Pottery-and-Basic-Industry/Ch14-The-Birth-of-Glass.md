# Chapter 14: The Birth of Glass

> Part 3 · Agriculture, Pottery, and Basic Industry. Glass is the first "artificial material" route: melting sand, alkali, and stone in proportion into a state rarely seen in nature — a supercooled liquid that does not crystallize on cooling. It will go on to play four roles: vessels (the carrier of the chemical industry), windows and instruments (for observing materials), the blanks for lenses and vacuum devices, and the **parent material for metal–glass seals** (the physical prerequisite of electronic devices). This chapter's goal is set at "vessel grade": reliably melting glass that is uniform, low in bubbles, and properly annealed; and at the end, an honest map of how far the road "from vessel glass to optical glass" still runs.

## Chapter Objectives

- Establish the three-component concept of glass (sand–alkali–lime) and the mix-ratio-to-melting-behavior comparison.
- Establish three-stage iron removal in sand dressing (winnowing / magnetic separation / acid-wash upgrade slot) and the mass-production process for extracting alkali from wood ash.
- Establish the crucible melting regime: melting—fining—homogenization—forming—**stepped annealing**.
- Establish the defect troubleshooting table (bubbles, inclusions, striae, cracks) and master the cullet remelting loop.
- Establish the **sample quench test** (destructive sampling for stress acceptance).
- Explain the thermal-expansion coefficient and the seal-matching principle (Section 14.9), laying the physical groundwork for the electronic devices of Chapter 33.
- Produce beads, rods, crude sheet, shallow dishes, and simple vessels, with each one's usage boundary made explicit.

## Capability Boundaries

**Can do:** stable production of vessel-grade glass (L2); occasional T-4 grade samples (the lens-blank threshold); the principles of sealing and trial transitional seals on small pieces.

**Explicitly cannot do:** optical glass (the four hard-specification gaps are in Section 14.7; graded L1); stable mass production of hollow drawn tube (a breakthrough item); matched materials for hermetic seals to metal (the lead glass + platinum wire route is given in Chapter 33; this chapter only establishes the principles and hand skills).

**External dependencies:** firewood (converted at ten-to-one for alkali) and low-iron sand sources — **what caps glass output is the raw material, not the furnace**.

### 14.1 Glass Raw Materials and Basic Composition

**Engineering definition**: glass is a silicate melt that does not crystallize on cooling. Quartz sand alone melts only above 1700 ℃ — beyond local furnaces; adding alkali pulls the melting point down into the 1100–1300 ℃ range; adding lime then makes the glass water-resistant. Each of the three components has its own job:

| Component | Function | Local source | Mix ratio (parts by weight, empirical starting point) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Quartz sand (SiO₂) | Skeleton | White quartz sand | 60–75 |
| Alkali (potash / soda) | Flux: lowers the melting point by several hundred degrees | Purified wood ash, natural alkali lakes (if any) | 15–25 |
| Lime (CaO equivalent) | Stabilizer: raises chemical durability | Shell powder, limestone powder | 5–10 |

**Mix ratio vs. melting behavior (comparative, calibrated with the slump-cone set)**:

| Alkali level | Melting behavior | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Alkali 15 parts | Melts slowly, needs the yellow-white temperature band, sand grains tend to remain | Good durability |
| Alkali 20 parts | Melts out smoothly, fining is quick | The vessel-grade baseline |
| Alkali 25 parts | Melts at lower temperature, glass is thin | Chemical durability drops, crucible corrosion worsens |

**The serious consequence of leaving out lime**: pure sand + alkali glass dissolves in water — a vessel holding water will slowly turn sticky and devitrify. The stabilizer is not optional.

**Impurities and color**: iron is the most common impurity. Ferrous iron tints glass blue-green; under oxidizing conditions it shifts toward yellow-green — **the first-generation target is not colorlessness but "uniform pale green"**. Decolorizing: if pyrolusite is registered (Section 8.4), blend in 1%–2% by volume to offset the green with purple (too much turns it purple; fix the boundary with gradient tests).

**Raw material survey list** (into the resource map): white quartz sand (the whiter the better — white = low iron; riverbank sand, dune sand, quartzite weathering sand); hardwood ash (potassium-rich) and grass-straw ash (contains calcium and silicon; poor as an alkali); natural alkali-lake frost (if any, the best option); shells / limestone; pyrolusite; **Iceland spar (transparent calcite crystals, for birefringence checks; registered in Section 8.3)**.

### 14.2 From Mineral Raw Materials to Fusible Batch

#### 14.2.1 Sand Dressing: Three-Stage Iron Removal

**Stage 1: panning out the mud**. Stir the sand in a water trough and pour off the turbid top water; repeat until the poured water runs clear — mud is one of the main carriers of iron.

**Stage 2: winnowing out the heavy-mineral concentrate (the step that improves color the most)**. Spread the sand in a shallow wooden pan, hold it level, and winnow with a shaking motion — the light quartz grains roll to one side while the heavy iron minerals (black and dark-red grains) sink and stay at the pan's center; sort repeatedly.

**Stage 3: magnetic separation (if a natural lodestone is available)**. Wrap a natural lodestone (magnetite with natural magnetism — a resource-survey item) in cloth and drag it back and forth across the surface of **dry sand** — the magnetite grains (black) are picked out. **Limitations, stated honestly**: hematite and limonite are barely attracted — magnetic separation removes only the magnetic iron minerals, so winnowing remains the mainstay; a lodestone's magnetism decays with use, and when its pull clearly weakens, "recharge" it by stroking it repeatedly in one direction against iron filings (an empirical method, must be field-verified), or find another lodestone.

**Upgrade slot (activated after Chapter 23)**: washing the sand in dilute acid — dilute acid dissolves the iron film on the grain surfaces and removes more iron than winnowing and magnetic separation combined, but it requires acid-resistant vessels and waste-liquid disposal (Chapter 49). At this chapter's stage the conditions do not exist, so it is registered as an upgrade route.

**Sieving and drying**: sieve out the 0.1–0.5 mm fraction; sun-dry or roast it dry in a pan — **wet sand entering the crucible is one source of bubbles**.

1. **Input materials**: raw sand (riverbank sand, dune sand, quartzite weathering sand — **the whiter, the lower the iron**); water for washing; finished grain size **0.1–0.5 mm**.
2. **Tools required**: water trough (washing), shallow wooden winnowing pan (heavy-mineral winnowing), natural lodestone (wrapped in cloth; a resource-survey item), fine sieve (to take the 0.1–0.5 mm fraction), drying yard or roasting pan, balance (weighing by parts, Chapter 3). 3. **Sequence and control points**: (1) **wash out the mud** — stir the sand in the trough, pour off the turbid top water, repeat until the poured water runs clear (mud is a main iron carrier); (2) **winnow the heavy minerals** — spread the sand in the shallow pan, hold it level and shake: light quartz rolls to one side, heavy iron minerals (black, dark-red grains) settle at the pan's center; sort repeatedly; (3) **magnetic separation** — drag the cloth-wrapped lodestone back and forth over **dry sand** to pick out black magnetite grains; (4) sieve to 0.1–0.5 mm; (5) sun-dry or roast dry in a pan. Control points: **magnetic separation only on dry sand**; **wet sand never enters the crucible** (one source of bubbles); winnowing is the mainstay — magnetic separation removes only magnetic iron minerals (hematite and limonite are barely attracted).
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: grain size **0.1–0.5 mm** (specification); the winnowing endpoint = **a handful shows no black grains**; when the lodestone's pull fades, "recharge" it by stroking it repeatedly in one direction against iron filings (**empirical method, must be field-verified**); dilute-acid washing is an upgrade slot — no conditions for it at this chapter's stage, registered only.
5. **Observations to record**: per batch — source and color of the raw sand / quantity of black heavy mineral at the pan's center (falling batch by batch = stable source) / amount picked up by the lodestone / whether clumps crumble when rubbed after roast-drying / sieve grade and grain size.
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = **black grains visually zero** and roast-dried clumps crumble when rubbed; rework = black grains still visible after winnowing (add winnowing rounds, repeat magnetic separation); scrap = the sand source is too high in iron (still black after winnowing) — **change the source**.
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) black grains remain after winnowing → too few rounds, or the pan was not held level; (2) the lodestone picks up nothing → sand not dry, or the stone has lost its magnetism (recharge or replace); (3) color still dark after magnetic separation → the iron is non-magnetic (hematite, limonite): add winnowing rounds or change source; (4) dense fine bubbles after charging → sand not fully dry, see the Section 14.4 troubleshooting table.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one batch washed until the poured water runs clear with no black grains in a handful, recorded; L2 = **T-grade records stable across 5 consecutive sand batches**; L3 = a second person dresses the same source from this card alone, matching color grade and bubble count.

#### 14.2.2 Potash Preparation (Mass-Production Line)

1. Leaching: ash : water = 1:3–5, soak 4 h twice, combine the clear supernatants (the Chapter 8 process).
2. Evaporation and concentration: boil over direct fire in a wide, shallow ceramic pan, topping up liquid as it boils, until it cooks down to a thick paste.
3. Burning purification: transfer the paste to a ceramic dish and roast it red-hot in the kiln until the char particles are gone — this yields grayish-white alkali blocks (mostly potassium carbonate).
4. Yield: **about ten baskets of ash per basket of alkali blocks** (empirical value) — the alkali for glass is "bought with burned firewood"; if a natural alkali lake exists, it takes priority.

Alkali blocks are strongly deliquescent; store them in a ceramic urn sealed with clay and reseal immediately after each use.

#### 14.2.3 Batching and Small Trials

Grind each of the three components fine and sieve them separately; weigh by parts by weight; **dry-mix, then add water, knead into batch cakes, and dry them** before charging — dry material charged straight into the crucible will stratify (light alkali floats, sand sinks). **Every time the raw-material batch changes, run a small trial first**: one crucible's worth of marble-sized melt beads, graded visually (transparency, bubble count, color); no full crucible is melted until the small trial passes.

**The resource cost account (must be settled before firing)**: of the full cost of one small-trial crucible, the firewood behind the alkali is roughly half (empirical value). Glass production scheduling follows the firewood surplus, not orders — in the slack farming seasons when firewood is plentiful, concentrate firing and stockpile semi-finished goods; firing hastily in the rainy season burns the wood that should have carried you through the winter (the Chapter 38 energy account).

### 14.3 Melting, Forming, and Annealing

**Crucible**: a refractory-base-mix shallow crucible — mouth diameter 10–15 cm, depth 8–12 cm, wall thickness about 1.5 cm (empirical values); coil-built from clay strips, shade-dried, then **pre-fired** (one empty firing to red heat) to drive off structural water — an un-pre-fired crucible spalls on contact with the melt and the whole charge is scrapped. Brush the crucible's exterior with fine refractory slurry to reduce corrosion.

**Furnace**: Chapter 12's separated-firebox shaft furnace + piston bellows, charcoal-fueled; aim the slump-cone set from softening grade up through the yellow-white band (about 1100–1300 ℃, empirical). Place the crucible at the hot zone at the furnace's center (Section 12.7's temperature-distribution map).

**The four melting stages (driven by observational criteria, not guessed temperatures)**:

| Stage | Observational criterion | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Sinter collapse | Batch cakes brighten and slump into a mass | Keep raising the temperature |
| Melt-out | Surface reflects like a mirror | Enter vitrification |
| Fining | Large bubbles rise and burst, the "boiling" gradually stops | **Extend the time** — clearing bubbles depends on coalescence and flotation; it cannot be rushed |
| Homogenization | Melt surface calm and uniform | Stir slowly two or three turns with a dry refractory ceramic rod (a wet rod introduces bubbles) |

[Card 14-02 Melting One Crucible] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: this crucible's batch cakes (batched at sand : alkali : lime = 60–75 : 15–25 : 5–10 by weight — each ground and sieved separately, dry-mixed, wetted and pressed into cakes, dried until they crumble when rubbed); cullet (three to four tenths, collected in a dedicated bucket, free of mud and metal); a pre-fired shallow crucible (mouth 10–15 cm, depth 8–12 cm, wall about 1.5 cm, one empty firing to red heat to drive off structural water, exterior brushed with fine refractory slurry); charcoal (15–25 kg per crucible); dry sand (furnace-front bunding); a standard-sample box (2×2 finger) and number tags.

2. **Tools required**: Chapter 12's separated-firebox shaft furnace + piston bellows (two boxes in parallel, air output calibrated per Card 12-01); the slump-cone set (to read the temperature band); the temperature-distribution map (to fix the crucible's hot position); long-pole wooden crucible tongs wrapped with wet straw rope; a dry refractory ceramic rod (stirring rod, must be dry); a narrow-slit wooden frame (to look into the furnace mouth, short looks repeated); dry sand and shovel; balance and weights (batching); the crucible-run parameter sheet; the standard-sample box. Calibration source: temperature is not read off an instrument — cone advance plus melt-surface criteria are judged together; the batch is weighed by balance in parts by weight, never estimated by eye.

3. **Sequence and control points** (batch → small sample → charge the crucible → the four heating stages → take the melt → close out): ① **Batch and press cakes** — grind and sieve the three components separately, weigh by parts, dry-mix, wet and press into cakes, dry. Control point: dry batch fed straight into the crucible stratifies (light alkali floats, sand sinks) — it must be pressed into cakes and dried first. ② **Small sample first** — with every new raw-material batch, melt one marble-sized bead and read transparency, bubble count and color by eye. Control point: **no full crucible without the small trial**. ③ **Charge the crucible** — batch cakes and cullet at three to four tenths, leaving room for the boil at the mouth. Control point: log the cullet ratio per crucible; do not estimate it. ④ **Position in the furnace** — the crucible at the hot zone at the furnace centre (Section 12.7 map), the flame wall working, no flame playing directly on the crucible wall. Control point: a direct flame on the wall is the first source of a burn-through. ⑤ **Heat to sinter collapse** — the cakes brighten and slump into a mass; keep raising. ⑥ **Melt-out** — surface reflects like a mirror: vitrification (the 1100–1300 ℃ band, judged by cone plus melt surface). ⑦ **Fining** — large bubbles rise and burst, the "boiling" gradually stops. Control point: **extend the time, never force the fire** — clearing bubbles depends on coalescence and flotation and cannot be rushed. ⑧ **Homogenization** — once the melt surface is calm and uniform, stir slowly two or three turns with the dry ceramic rod. Control point: **a wet rod introduces bubbles** — the rod must be dried first. ⑨ **Take the melt** — grip the crucible with the long tongs, stand to the side, move slowly, dry-sand bunding in place in front of the furnace. ⑩ **Close out** — keep a 2×2 finger standard sample from every crucible, numbered into the specimen library; scrap goes to the cullet bucket under the remelt rule; the four stages' fuel charges and the fining time go into the crucible-run sheet.

4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: batch sand : alkali : lime = 60–75 : 15–25 : 5–10 (parts by weight, empirical starting point); melting band 1100–1300 ℃ (cone + melt-surface criteria, empirical); cullet ratio three to four tenths (empirical); crucible mouth 10–15 cm, depth 8–12 cm, wall about 1.5 cm (empirical); one crucible takes 3 people half a day (2 on the blast in shifts + 1 furnace keeper); consumption per crucible: sand 2–3 kg, alkali 0.5–1 kg, charcoal 15–25 kg, crucible depreciation 0.1–0.2 of a pot. The stage fuel charges and fining time carry no fixed minutes — empirical, **to be calibrated to local conditions**: log them per crucible until the site's "fuel charges vs stage" rhythm table exists, then follow that table.

5. **Observations to record**: date and crucible number; raw-material batch number and ratio; cullet ratio; fuel charges at each of the four stages (sinter collapse / melt-out / fining / homogenization); fining time; stirring turns and whether the rod was dry; forming method and piece count; defect list (bubbles, unmelted sand grains, stones, striae); annealing method and quench-test result; standard-sample T grade; crucible wall erosion and whether it has reached the scrap line (erosion past 1/3 of the original wall at any point).

6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = melt surface calm and uniform, no new string of bubbles after stirring, a sample that can be graded, crucible uncracked and not burnt through; rework = insufficient fining (back in the furnace for more time, no forcing) / striae from poor homogenization (extend homogenization, one more turn); scrap = the crucible has cracked or burnt through (stop at once, cool inside the sand bunding, recover the batch as cullet); the whole crucible's batch is written off with the cause logged.

7. **Common failures and diagnosis order** (by frequency): ① dense fine bubbles (fining time short, then damp batch) → extend fining, dry all raw materials through, re-fry the cakes; ② isolated large bubbles (air stirred in / wet rod) → dry rod, slow stirring; ③ unmelted sand grains (temperature too low, then sand too coarse or batch too large) → raise heat and air, sand below 0.5 mm, thinner layer; ④ stones, off-color hard points (crucible erosion spalling, then mold crumbs) → scrap the pot past its erosion limit, keep mold ash clean; ⑤ striae and clouding (uneven mixing, batch added in portions, then poor homogenization) → batch the whole charge at once, extend homogenization; ⑥ melt sticking in the pot, hard to pour (hold too long, alkali attacking the pot, slurry layer worn) → shorten the hold, re-brush slurry, change pot; ⑦ crucible cracking or melt running into the furnace (not pre-fired, direct flame, tongs unchecked) → pre-firing regime, reset the flame wall, check the tongs every pot.

8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one crucible yields glass beads (transparent or translucent, hard, conchoidal fracture), with the four-stage criteria and fuel charges on file; L2 = five consecutive crucibles at ≥70% yield, recurrence of the same defects falling, the cullet remelt system running, the quench test passing; L3 = sand, alkali (firewood account) and lime each with a standing reserve, a continuous crucible-run archive from which a second person reproduces the same grade, crucibles made and repaired in-house, and the monthly T-grade quality curve on the books and used to order improvements.

**Forming methods (three routes in the pre-blowpipe era)**:
1. **Molding (the mainstay)**: preheat clay molds or damp sand molds; put batch cakes into a shallow-trough mold and melt in-furnace together; out of the furnace come crude sheet, ingots, and beads (ash mold: poke holes into compacted damp ash with a stick and fill the holes with batch, which melts into beads).
2. **Dip-and-roll**: pick up a melt gob on the end of a dry ceramic rod; roll it on a stone slab into beads, or press it between two slabs into sheet — tap free after cooling.
3. **Drawing**: pick up melt on two rods and pull them apart to form a rod — **rod drawing is the foundation of tube drawing (a thermometer prerequisite) and of the sealing hand skills; practice it to mastery first** (◎ two-rod pulling rhythm: withdraw together at uniform speed; uneven speed means uneven thickness. Practice method: draw 20 rods from scrap and measure mid-section diameter differences with a snap gauge).

Blow forming needs a hollow blowpipe and continuous airflow, so it is an upgrade item; the core-forming method (dip a solid clay core into melt to form a small bottle, then dig the core out after cooling) is registered as an alternative.

**Crucible removal and pouring**: grip the crucible with long-pole wooden tongs (the ends wrapped with wet straw rope for heat insulation); move slowly and stand to the side — **a crucible breaking and splashing molten glass is this chapter's number-one mechanical hazard**. Spread dry sand as bunding on the floor in front of the furnace.

#### 14.3.1 Annealing: The Stepped Cooling Regime (a Mandatory Step)

Glass's outer layer cools first and its inner layer later; the mismatched contraction creates internal stress — **glass that was not annealed looks perfectly normal on the day it is made, then days later, or at the first cut, it shatters: a textbook silent failure** (Iron Law Six).

**Annealing mechanism and the steps**: soda-lime glass is most sensitive to contraction in roughly the 450–560 ℃ band (the transition region, empirical) — this band must be crossed slowly; above it stress relaxes on its own, and below it (roughly <400 ℃) contraction is small enough to go faster. From this, the stepped regime (using the ash pit as the example, with pit-condition anchor criteria):

| Step | Pit-condition anchor (◎ auxiliary criterion) | Hold / rate | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hold step | Embers under the ash in the pit glow faint red (about the 500–550 ℃ class) | Bury the workpieces and hold 1–2 h (take the upper limit for thick pieces) | Relieve forming stress |
| Slow-cool step | The faint red fades until "a hand near the pit mouth feels heat" (about 550→400 ℃) | **Cool down naturally overnight; raking out the ash to speed it up is forbidden** | Cross the transition region slowly |
| Fast-cool step | Hot → warm | Next day open the pit and air-dry naturally to ambient | — |

Acceptance criteria (choose one of three, by spot check):
1. **Tap-tone method** (◎): a single crisp "ring" = low stress; a dull, sandy tone = high stress; re-annealing can save some pieces;
2. **Sample quench test** (destructive sampling; draw 1 piece per batch): soak the piece in 60 ℃ warm water for 5 min, then plunge it straight into cold water — no cracking = the batch's annealing passes; cracking = the whole batch returns to the pit for re-annealing (re-anneal at most 2 times; if it cracks again, scrap it);
3. Shelf observation: 7 days in daylight with no self-cracking and no edge chipping.

1. **Input materials**: formed workpieces awaiting annealing (thickness ratio ≤2:1); the ash pit (lid sound, interior dry — **a wet pit is a source of shattering**); embers and covering ash in the pit; one basin of 60 ℃ warm water and one of cold water for the quench test.
2. **Tools required**: ash pit and covering shovel (**for covering only, never for raking ash to speed things up**), small tapping hammer (tap-tone method), warm and cold water basins, batch tags and record sheet (the annealing column of the Section 14.4 crucible-run table). 3. **Sequence and control points**: (1) **hold step** — bury the workpieces with embers under the ash showing faint red (about the 500–550 ℃ class), hold **1–2 h** (upper limit for thick pieces); (2) **slow-cool step** — the faint red fades to "a hand near the pit mouth feels heat" (about 550→400 ℃); **cool naturally overnight; raking out the ash to speed it up is forbidden**; (3) **fast-cool step** — next day open the pit and air-dry to ambient; (4) spot-check criteria, choose one of three: tap tone / sample quench test / 7-day shelf observation. Control points: **the transition region (about 450–560 ℃) must be crossed slowly**; raking ash is the one forbidden act in this operation; mold-cast pieces get a doubled hold (Section 14.11).
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: transition region about **450–560 ℃** (soda-lime, empirical); hold at the 500–550 ℃ class **1–2 h** plus overnight slow cooling; below about 400 ℃ contraction is small enough to go faster; quench test **60 ℃ warm water 5 min → straight into cold water** (destructive sampling criterion); **re-annealing at most 2 times**; thickness ratio ≤2:1 (design rule); mold-cast pieces hold **2–4 h**, 2 pieces drawn per batch.
5. **Observations to record**: per batch — pit-condition anchors (faint red / heat felt by hand) / hold duration / times of opening and emptying the pit / tap tone (crisp or dull-sandy) / quench-test piece number and result / whether any piece self-cracked or chipped within 7 days.
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = the quench test **does not crack**, no self-cracking or edge chipping in 7 days, and a single crisp "ring" on tapping; rework = dull sandy tone (re-annealing saves some pieces) or quench cracking — **the whole batch returns to the pit**; scrap = cracking again after 2 re-anneals.
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) the quench test cracks → hold too short, or the slow-cool step was accelerated by raking ash; (2) self-cracking days later, chipping on cutting → insufficient annealing (silent failure); check next for abrupt thickness changes; (3) shattering inside the ash pit → wet pit, or pieces put in too hot; (4) a dull, dead tap tone → high stress, go straight to re-annealing.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one batch through all three steps with one quench test run (success or failure, filed); L2 = **quench-test spot checks pass and 7 days bring no self-cracking**; L3 = a second person anneals from this card alone, reading the pit anchors the same way with a comparable pass rate.

### 14.4 Glass Bubbles, Inclusions, and Cracks

**Defect troubleshooting table (ordered by probability)**:

| Defect | Check first | Check next | Remedy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dense fine bubbles | Insufficient fining time | Wet batch | Extend fining; dry all raw materials thoroughly; re-roast the batch cakes |
| Isolated large bubbles | Stirring entrained air | Wet stirring rod | Stir slowly; switch to a dry rod |
| Unmelted sand grains | Temperature too low | Sand too coarse; charge too large | Raise temperature, add blast; limit sand to below 0.5 mm; thin the charge layer |
| Stones (hard spots of an off color) | Crucible corrosion and flaking | Mold debris | Scrap the crucible when corrosion exceeds limits; keep mold ash clean |
| Striae and haze | Uneven mixing; staged charging | Insufficient homogenization | Make the full batch into cakes mixed in one go; extend homogenization |
| Grayish-white devitrified surface | Too much lime | Too little alkali | Adjust the ratio back — fix the boundary with single-variable gradient tests |
| Color too dark | Sand high in iron | Insufficient manganese decolorizing | Change sand, re-winnow + magnetic separation; manganese powder in gradient steps |
| Self-cracking days later / edge chipping on cutting | Insufficient annealing (silent failure) | Abrupt thickness changes | Assign the cause with the sample quench test; re-anneal; keep the thickness ratio ≤2:1 |
| Sticks to the crucible, hard to remove | Hold too long; high alkali corroding the crucible | Slurry coating worn | Shorten the hold; re-brush slurry; change the crucible |

**Cullet remelting (an important institution)**: glass can be remelted completely, and remelting cullet costs less alkali and less temperature than fresh batch — put edges and rejects into a dedicated bucket (guard against cuts) and blend 30–40% into the next crucible (empirical value). Yield loss is no longer a pure loss.

**Records**: keep a 2×2-finger reference sample piece from every crucible, numbered into the specimen library; the **crucible-run parameter record table** (date/crucible no. | raw-material batch no. | mix ratio | cullet fraction | fuel-charge counts for the four stages | fining duration | stir count | forming method and piece count | defect list | annealing method and quench-test results | sample T grade) — a missing field voids the record; all progress in glass craft starts from comparing rows of this table.

### 14.5 Glass Vessels, Tubing, and Simple Observation Devices

| Product | How made | Uses and boundaries |
|---|---|---|
| Beads | Melted in ash-mold holes / dip-and-roll | Ornaments, trade goods; loose dimensional tolerance |
| Solid rods | Two-rod pulling | Stirring rods, scraper blanks; the basis of tube drawing and sealing technique |
| Crude sheet / window panes | Melted flat in a shallow-trough mold in the furnace | Windows, work surfaces; uneven thickness |
| Shallow dishes, plates | Press molding | Holding material, weighing samples; precursors of the evaporating dish |
| Tumbler cups | Deep-trough molding | Drinking vessels, measuring-cup blanks (graduations ground separately) |
| Small vials | Core forming (alternative) | Sample storage |

**Usage boundary statement**: vessel glass is **not thermal-shock resistant** — heating it over a direct flame is forbidden. Heat it with a bath (indirect warming in a hot-water ceramic pan / a hot-sand tray). Holding ash-alkali solutions and weak acids briefly is acceptable (when the lime stabilizer passes spec); prolonged boiling in strong alkali corrodes it — once weight loss shows, scrap it.

**Simple observation devices**: window panes, cover slips, the **water-filled glass ball** — a coarse glass ball filled with water is the earliest form of the magnifier (poor imaging, but enough to verify "glass + curvature = magnification"), paving the way directly for Chapter 24.

**Care**: rinse new vessels in warm water; sudden hot-cold shocks are forbidden; a hazy devitrified film on the inner wall = an early sign of alkali attack — downgrade it to dry-goods use; polish out light scratches with whetstone powder + water — deep scratches are crack sources; retire the piece.

**Tubing status**: solid rods can be drawn; **hollow tube (the prerequisite for thermometers, condensers, and vacuum-tube envelopes) is the key bottleneck** — it needs a stable melt gob plus two-person pulling with air blowing to form tube; it is listed as a breakthrough item and recorded in the archive.

### 14.6 How to Improve Transparency and Uniformity Step by Step

**Improvement roadmap (the order is the priority; move only one variable per step)**:
1. Sand purity (carry winnowing + magnetic separation all the way — the biggest contributor to color);
2. Alkali purity (purify by burning; residual char is a source of bubbles and haze);
3. Crucible quality (less corrosion = fewer stones and striae);
4. Melting regime (fining time, homogenizing stir, temperature steps);
5. Decolorizing (pyrolusite at 0.5% / 1% / 2% — three steps to find the local optimum);
6. Annealing regime (a gradient of hold-step durations, checked against quench-test pass rates).

**Transparency grading**:

| Grade | Criterion | Use |
|---|---|---|
| T-1 light-transmitting | Bright against backlight, casts a visible shadow | Windows |
| T-2 sharp shadow | Shadow edges clear | Vessels, viewports |
| T-3 text-readable | Charcoal-pencil writing readable through the piece | Premium vessels |
| T-4 magnification-grade | Fine lines seen through it without distortion; no striae or bubbles visible to the naked eye | **The lens-blank threshold** |

Grade each furnace run's sample pieces into the archive — month-over-month comparison gives the quality curve. Once transparency is up, go back and look at the mix ratio: sometimes the problem is not the process but the material (one sand change beats ten fire adjustments).

### 14.7 Why Ordinary Glass Is Not Optical Glass

| Metric | Optical requirement | This chapter's status | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Uniformity | Refractive index identical everywhere | No striae visible to the eye is the bare minimum gate | Homogenization means are primitive; no stirred melt pool |
| Purity | Bubbles and stones trending to zero | Small bubbles tolerated | Fining time and raw-material purity insufficient |
| Annealing | Residual stress minimal | Stepped slow-cooling grade, no controlled temperature curve | Needs a precision annealing furnace (Chapter 25 temperature control) |
| Composition consistency | Refractive index stable batch to batch | Ratios by parts by weight; batch-to-batch variation | Needs quantitative batching and stable, pure raw materials |

**Grading conclusion**: this chapter's product is **vessel grade**. Chapter 24 selects lens blanks from this chapter's T-4 sample pieces — enough to make "a useful magnifier"; the glass needed for "qualified telescope and microscope objectives" must wait until the four gaps in the table above are closed one by one. Writing the boundary clearly is more honest — and more useful — than promising "we can build a microscope right away."

### 14.9 Seal Fundamentals: Thermal-Expansion Matching (the Physical Prerequisite for Chapter 33)

**Why this gets its own section**: Chapter 33's vacuum devices must seal metal lead wires into glass. **This is not "stick it in and fire it" — ordinary glass against ordinary metal will crack or slow-leak on cooling**, because the thermal expansion coefficients (α, relative elongation per degree Celsius) do not match: as a sealed joint cools from red heat to room temperature, the two materials contract by different amounts and stress builds at the interface — a 30% difference can already crack it; a difference of nearly twofold will certainly crack it or leave a slow-leak path.

**Coefficient-of-expansion magnitude table (×10⁻⁶/K, empirical orders of magnitude, for material matching)**:

| Material | α | Sealing assessment |
|---|---|---|
| Borosilicate glass | ≈3.3 | Close to tungsten (4.4) and molybdenum (5) — a long-term route |
| Soda-lime glass (this chapter) | ≈9 | **Matches platinum (≈9)**; 30% off iron (≈12) — risky |
| Lead glass | ≈9 | Matches platinum; low softening point favors lampwork |
| Iron / low-carbon steel | ≈12 | 30% off this chapter's glass: transitional seals only |
| Copper | ≈17 | Nearly twofold off this chapter's glass: a direct seal will certainly crack |
| Platinum | ≈9 | **Matches this chapter's glass** — sourcing is the problem (Chapter 33 Class-C dependency) |

**Three things this chapter can do**:
1. **Practice the lampwork hand skills**: butt-fusing solid rods, fusing rod to sheet, plugging small holes — before matched materials arrive, get the hands trained first (◎ flame position and preheating rhythm; practice method: 50 scrap-rod fusions, then snap each joint apart to inspect how deep the fusion went);
2. **Transitional seal trials (demonstration grade)**: multi-step stepped joints of glass—smaller glass—smallest glass, or trial seals of glass with extremely fine iron wire (diameter ≤0.5 mm; fine wire's flexibility tolerates the mismatch) — immediately after sealing, run the sample quench test and the 7-day observation, and **register the success rate honestly**;
3. **Establishing the slow-leak check**: invert the sealed piece into a water trough, or drip water onto the seal, with wood-ash powder placed inside beforehand — a fine string of bubbles, or ash powder being sucked in, marks the leak. Chapter 33 reuses this method directly.

**Boundary statement**: the hermetic-seal materials route (lead glass + fine platinum wire, or a carbon-electrode substitute) is given in Chapter 33. This chapter delivers only: the expansion-coefficient table, the lampwork hand skills, transitional-seal experience, and the slow-leak check method — without these four, the word "seal" in Chapter 33 is an empty one.

### 14.10 Blowing Breakthrough, in Detail: The Blowpipe, Gathering, and the First Blown Vessel

**Breakthrough positioning**: blowing is the only low-waste route that turns "a gob of glass" into "a uniform thin-walled cavity" — with molding, the deeper the cavity the thicker the wall; the same weight of glass blown into a hollow bubble holds 5–10 times the volume of a solid bead (empirical comparison). This section is written out in detail for the third-tier objective; before the wrought-iron hollow tube arrives, the cold-state and rhythm practice of steps 1 and 3 can begin — no need to wait for the tube.

**Self-made blowpipe (specifications and acceptance)**:

| Item | Specification (empirical) | Criteria |
|---|---|---|
| Tube material | Wrought-iron hollow tube (a metal-route product; source registered in another chapter) | Held to the light the bore looks round and through; blowing meets no resistance |
| Inner diameter | 8–10 mm | Too coarse wastes breath and is hard to control; too fine freezes and clogs easily |
| Length | 110–130 cm | Tube end to furnace mouth about one arm's length; leave 40 cm spare at the mouth end so it stays cool to the lips |
| Gathering end | Filed flat, deburred | End face perpendicular to the tube axis — skew is an inborn source of off-center gathering |
| Pre-use check | 10 clear empty blows; with the tip 2 cm into water, no bubbles | Bubbles = the tube is cracked; discard |

**The six operating steps (each with its criteria)**:

1. **Heat the pipe and gather ◎**: bake the pipe tip at the furnace mouth until it is bright yellow (about 20–30 s, empirical — a cold pipe cannot hold the batch; it only picks up a crumbly shell), then plunge it 1–2 cm below the melt surface and twirl the pipe at constant speed for 3–5 turns to wind up a date-pit-sized gob (first-piece target 20–40 g; weigh it and record). **Rotation prevents sagging drips**: under gravity, soft glass left unrotated for 2–3 s draws to a point and droops — twirl at constant speed (about 1 turn per second) from gathering to delivery; this is turning a roasting spit, not stirring soup.
   ◎ Practice method (three stages to pass): (1) cold state — stick a thumb-sized ball of wet clay to the pipe tip and twirl it cantilevered; 3 sets × 20 turns × 3 days; pass if the clay ball neither droops nor wanders off center. (2) scrap glass — 20 gathers at the furnace mouth, weighed each time; pass if one person's variation is ≤±20%. (3) misjudgment compensation — after gathering a full gob, stop rotating for 1 s and look at the lower edge; if it has drawn to a point, add turns at once; going by "feels about right" is not allowed.
2. **Rolling-board shaping**: sprinkle a thin layer of charcoal ash over a ground-flat bluestone slab; roll the gob on the slab 5–10 turns — the purpose is not to make it round but to **align the gob's axis with the pipe axis**. Criterion: raise the pipe to the light and rotate one turn; the gob's shadow is concentric, with no skewed image; skewed = back to the furnace to reheat and roll again; blowing directly is not allowed.
3. **First inflation**: hold the gob at the furnace mouth between orange-red and bright yellow, then at the mouthpiece **gently puff one breath**, producing first a fingernail-sized base bubble — not blowing a light bulb in one breath. The air comes from cheek compression, not the lungs: cheek-puffed air is short and controllable (◎ practice method: blow 30 times through a thin tube into a cup of water; pass if a fine string of bubbles rises without splashing).
4. **The heat–roll–blow cycle**: before each inflation round, reheat at the furnace for 30–60 s (dark red: do not blow; bright yellow: blow — the color criterion); alternate inflation and rolling board 2–4 rounds until formed; limit each round's inflation to a visibly doubled diameter — greed in a single round yields a thin bottom.
5. **Opening, trimming the mouth, and detaching**: touch the end of a wet stick to the mouth rim and draw a circle; the hot glass cracks along the line and the mouth opens; trim the rim round with small tongs (a forged needle-nose pair) while rotating. Detaching: score the neck with a wet line, tap lightly to shake the piece free, and send it straight to the annealing pit. Criteria: the mouth rim is round and true; held to the light, the mouth's plane is perpendicular to the body; a skewed mouth = off-center attachment to the pipe; on the next piece, attach at a corrected position.
6. **Annealing handoff**: blown pieces have thin walls and cool fast — take the hold-step upper limit of 2 h, and draw 2 pieces per batch for the quench test; schedule the first blowing season at a 50% yield rate (empirical; the pass-line criteria are in Card 14-06).

**Failure troubleshooting (ordered by frequency of occurrence)**:

| Symptom | Check first | Check next | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pipe mouth frozen clogged (no air goes in) | Pipe tip too cool; a cold glass shell has formed at the mouth | Inner diameter too fine | Reheat at the furnace to melt the mouth open, then blow; frozen clogged 3 blowing runs in a row = cut 1 cm off the pipe tip and re-verify |
| Bubbles carried into the glass | Cold shell at the pipe mouth rolled in with the batch | Gathering too deep, stirring up the crucible bottom | Limit gathering to 1–2 cm below the melt surface; carried in anyway = scrap the piece into the remelt bucket |
| Off-center (uneven wall thickness) | The rolling-board step was skipped | Gathering end face not perpendicular | Reheating and re-rolling can save it; file the end face flat, re-verify, then resume |

[Card 14-06 Blowpipe Gathering and First Inflation] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: a qualified crucible (calm uniform melt surface, all four Section 14.3 stages checked off); blowpipe (wrought-iron hollow tube, **inner diameter 8–10 mm, length 110–130 cm**, gathering end filed flat and deburred, end face perpendicular to the axis); charcoal ash (for the rolling board); gather target **20–40 g** (first piece).
2. **Tools required**: blowpipe, ground-flat bluestone slab + charcoal-ash box (rolling board), balance (weighing the gather), wet stick (opening the mouth), forged needle-nose tongs (trimming), calipers (wall thickness on a snapped shard), annealing pit. Calibration source: color criteria replace temperature (bright yellow / orange-red / dark red); twirl speed is set per person, anchored on "no sagging drip after 2 s stopped".
3. **Sequence and control points (six steps)**: (1) **heat the pipe and gather** — bake the tip to **bright yellow** (about 20–30 s), plunge **1–2 cm** below the melt surface and twirl **3–5 turns** at constant speed to wind up a date-pit-sized gob; (2) **rolling-board shaping** — roll **5–10 turns** on charcoal ash to align the gob's axis with the pipe axis; raised to the light and rotated one turn, the gob's shadow must be concentric; (3) **first inflation** — reheat to between orange-red and bright yellow, **one cheek-puffed breath**, producing a fingernail-sized base bubble first; (4) **heat–roll–blow cycle** — reheat **30–60 s** per round, **2–4 rounds** alternating with the board; (5) **opening, trimming, detaching** — draw the mouth line with a wet stick, score the neck with a wet line, tap the piece free, straight into the pit; (6) **annealing handoff** — hold at the upper limit of 2 h, 2 pieces drawn per batch. Control points: **no gathering with a cold pipe**; **never stop rotating during the gather (about 1 turn/s; 2–3 s stopped draws a point and it droops)**; **blow only after the board confirms concentricity**; **dark red: do not blow; bright yellow: blow**.
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: inner diameter 8–10 mm, length 110–130 cm (specification); gather 20–40 g; twirl ≈1 turn/s, anchored on no drip after 2 s stopped; rolling board 5–10 turns; reheat 30–60 s per round, 2–4 rounds in all, **each round limited to a visibly doubled diameter**; the first blowing season is scheduled at **50% yield**; practice pass line — one person's 20 weighed gathers vary by **≤±20%**.
5. **Observations to record**: blow-run table (date / crucible no. / gather weight / rounds / wall-thickness spot checks / defects); three-tier spoken color calls (bright yellow = may blow / orange-red = may roll / dark red = trim only), sagging drips, bubbles carried in, base-bubble size.
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = concentric inflation, walls visually uniform, mouth rim round and true (mouth plane perpendicular to the body); rework = off-center (back to the furnace to re-roll); scrap = bubble strings carried in, thin bottom punctured (snap it up into the remelt bucket).
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) pipe mouth frozen clogged (no air goes in) → tip too cool, a cold glass shell at the mouth: reheat to melt the mouth open and blow again; **3 frozen clogs in a row = cut 1 cm off the tip and re-verify**; (2) bubbles carried into the glass → cold shell rolled in with the batch; check next for gathering too deep and stirring the crucible bottom; (3) off-center (uneven wall) → the rolling-board step was skipped; check next for a non-perpendicular gathering face; (4) thin bottom → one round inflated too greedily, one fewer round on the next piece; (5) a light bulb in one breath → blowing from the lungs instead of the cheeks.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = a first blown vessel plus one row in the blow-run table; L2 = **10 consecutive blowing runs at ≥50% yield** (the breakthrough pass line, the L2 baseline); L3 = a second person gathers and inflates from this card alone, gather weight varying ≤±20% with comparable wall thickness.

### 14.11 Mold-Cast Glass: The Gypsum-Mold and Ceramic-Mold Dual Routes

**Why this gets its own section**: the "molding" of Section 14.3 is a one-off rough blank in a clay or ash mold; this section upgrades mold casting into a repeatable process — same mold, same pattern, continuous production — delivering shallow dishes, standard beads, vial blanks, and measuring-cup blanks (the objects graduated in Section 14.13).

**The two routes**:

| Route | Material and method | Strengths | Weaknesses and life | Suitable pieces |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gypsum mold | Calcine gypsum into plaster of Paris (tiered calcining in the same kiln as lime, Chapter 13; scale-up registered in Chapter 23); mix with water, pour, and carve/trim to shape | Good detail reproduction, smooth surface, easy to carve and trim | Face spalls under repeated red heat; one mold ≤10 castings (empirical; spalled face = scrap) | Shallow dishes, relief beads, small pieces with fine mouth rims |
| Ceramic mold | Fireclay + grog powder about 4:1 by volume (empirical); sculpt and carve to shape, shade-dry, then pre-fire | Withstands red heat; long life (≥100 castings, empirical) | Poor detail reproduction, rough surface | Measuring-cup blanks, vial blanks, production-run beads |

**Release agent (common to both molds)**: fine-sieved charcoal ash or grog powder, brushed in a thin coat over the mold cavity with a soft brush — only a thin layer; too thick presses an ashy face into the glass. **Oils and starch paste are forbidden**: at red heat they carbonize, scab, and press into the glass surface as black spots. Criterion: the cast face comes out matte with no sticking points; sticking points = a missed spot of dusting or release too late — re-dust and cast again.

**Release timing**: glass cooled all the way through shrinks and grips the mold (ceramic molds worst of all) — **release at the dark-red state** (grip it with a damp cloth underneath); once cold, only light tapping is allowed, no hard prying. Criterion: inverted and tapped lightly it drops free = the timing is exactly right; stuck fast = back to the furnace, warm it to dark red, then release.

**Annealing doubled**: mold-cast pieces are thick and often push the thickness ratio to the 2:1 ceiling — double the hold step to 2–4 h; the overnight slow cooling is unchanged; draw 2 pieces per batch for the quench test (one more than for vessel pieces); if the 7-day observation fails, the whole batch returns to the pit (re-annealing ≤2 times, per Section 14.3.1).

**Scope boundaries**: shallow dishes (holding material, weighing samples, evaporating dishes), standard beads (a hundred beads from one mold — dimensional consistency is their very value), vial blanks (mouths bored and trimmed with a hollow drill), straight-walled measuring-cup blanks. **Deep-cavity pieces are not on this list** — mold-cast pieces are hard to extract; deep cavities belong to blowing (Section 14.10).

### 14.12 Lead Glass Pre-Research: Gradient Formulations and the Red Line

**Interface statement**: lead glass's raw-material prerequisite is red lead (Pb₃O₄), from Chapter 23's lead production line (this chapter produces no lead). **Until red lead is in hand, this section is a shelved blueprint, not a current task** — the pre-research deliverables are only the registered formulation gradients and the red-line institution.

**Three advantages (matching three long-term roles)**:
1. **Soft**: softening point about 100–200 ℃ lower than soda-lime (empirical magnitude), a wide lampworking window and easy mouth trimming — Chapter 33's sealing operations depend on this cell;
2. **Bright**: high refractive index; whiter and brighter than soda-lime under the same process — a candidate optical-blank material (the four gaps of Section 14.7 do not vanish for this, but "bright" leaves testing some margin);
3. **α ≈ 9 matches platinum** (the Section 14.9 table): the long-term parent material for metal lead-wire seals.

**Formulation gradients (parts by weight; registered, pending verification)**:

| Grade | Sand | Potash | Red lead | Positioning |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pb-1 | 65 | 20 | 15 | Transitional grade: bright but its character is still close to soda-lime |
| Pb-2 | 60 | 18 | 22 | Baseline grade: the main material for lampwork and sealing trials |
| Pb-3 | 55 | 15 | 30 | Upper-probe grade: brightest and softest, with the highest crucible-corrosion and devitrification risk |

Small-trial criteria (one marble-sample crucible per grade, compared against the soda-lime standard samples): hold to the light and compare whiteness and brightness; compare file-scratch depth (visibly deeper = softer); heft equal volumes (visibly heavier); run the quench test as usual. All three grades go into the sample library; register the locally best grade into the ledger.

**The red line (non-negotiable)**: **lead-glass vessels are categorically forbidden from holding food or drinking water.** Lead slowly leaches into weak acids (fruit juice, wine, vinegar); poisoning is counted in months, with no early warning — chronic harm is treated the same as a workplace injury (the same logic as Iron Law Seven). Enforcement measures: engrave a "◇" plus a red-paint dot on every piece; store in a dedicated cabinet, never in the kitchenware store; collect shards in a dedicated bucket; melt only in dedicated crucibles (lead glass corrodes crucibles fiercely, and sharing them with vessel glass is forbidden); dispose of crucible-wash water separately in the waste-liquid pit, logged; and at the furnace mouth, follow this chapter's protection plus wet sweeping against lead dust.

**Trial boundaries**: only decorative beads that serve as **optical and sealing raw material** and never touch the mouth; no mass production, no production scheduling. When red lead arrives, run three small trial crucibles and stop; restart per Chapter 33 specifications when the need returns.

### 14.13 Measuring-Instrument Glass: Droppers and Graduated Cylinders

**Positioning**: what gets glass into the metrology system is not its transparency but its **inertness and engravability** — for measuring instruments that must not touch acids and alkalis, only glass can serve locally. This section delivers droppers and graduated cylinders/cups, premised on the tube-drawing breakthrough having produced first articles (a short tube will do; its straight-wall uniformity requirement is lower than a thermometer tube's). This section is a third-tier objective.

**The dropper (tube drawing's first product)**: take a 10–15 cm hollow tube blank; heat one end until soft and pull it off the fire quickly into a taper (fine-tip inner diameter 1–2 mm, empirical); saw-score and snap off, and pass the tip's rim through the furnace-mouth flame 2–3 times to fire-polish. Acceptance: clear water drips freely, without clinging to the wall or breaking the stream; **drop-weight calibration** — drip 20 drops into a pre-weighed small cup; a total of 9–11 g (about 0.5 g per drop, empirical) passes; out of range = grind the tip on a fine whetstone with water and re-measure, or scrap it. **Number every dropper and register its measured 20-drop weight**; users convert by the registered value, not by the "one drop, one gram" default.

**The "local milliliter" engineering convention**: 1 mL = 1 g of clear water weighed on this site's balance (use within a water temperature of 15–25 ℃; recalibrate outside that range; the balance is cross-checked against weights, Chapter 3). Strictly speaking this is a circular definition (the density concept is still missing); this book accepts it as an **engineering convention** — graduation, verification, and the ledger all use the same balance and the same water-temperature range; **consistency takes priority over absolute accuracy**. Chapter 25's water-weight graduation of the drip-leak standard shares this section's origin: the same gram, the same balance, the same ledger.

**Graduating cylinders/cups**: use the mold-cast straight-walled tumbler (Section 14.11) as the blank; **pass the quench test first, graduate second** (a graduated piece sent back to the furnace for re-annealing = all graduations void). Work grid by grid: pour in 5 g of water and scribe one line, pour in another 5 g and scribe the next — a 50 mL cylinder gets 10 grids in all. Level assurance: set the blank on a flat plane, sight level at the bottom of the meniscus, and scribe the line fully around the wall; self-check by rotating the cup — the line must kiss the water surface everywhere. Acceptance criterion: **the error of any grid ≤5%** (empirical threshold; spot-check 3 grids per cylinder by re-weighing); out of tolerance = add a corrected line and lap the old line flat with abrasive.

**Engraved marks (abrasive scribing + color filling)**: scribe the wall with a steel stylus wetted with water carrying corundum powder, lines 0.2–0.5 mm deep (empirical); after washing and drying, dry-rub fine charcoal powder or red-ochre powder into the lines, then wipe the surface's loose powder clean — the lines keep their color while the face stays unmarked. Durability criterion: 20 wet-cloth scrubbings without losing the line; if it fades = the line is too shallow (deepen and re-scribe) or the powder too coarse (sieve finer).

[Card 14-07 Graduating and Verifying the Measuring Cylinder] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: mold-cast straight-walled tumbler blank (uniform walls, level mouth, **quench test already passed**); clear water (15–25 ℃); steel stylus + corundum powder; fine charcoal or red-ochre powder (color filling).
2. **Tools required**: balance (cross-checked against weights, Chapter 3), flat table (setting the blank level), steel stylus, soft cloth (wiping loose powder), abrasive (lapping old lines flat). Calibration source: **the grid value is fixed by weighing water on this site's balance** — 1 mL = 1 g of clear water here, used within 15–25 ℃ and recalibrated outside that range.
3. **Sequence and control points**: (1) **annealing acceptance first, graduation second** (a graduated piece sent back for re-annealing = every line void); (2) work grid by grid — pour 5 g of water and scribe a line, pour another 5 g and scribe the next (10 grids for a 50 mL cylinder); (3) level assurance — blank on a flat plane, sight level at the meniscus bottom, scribe the line fully around the wall; (4) **rotating-cup self-check** — the line must kiss the water surface everywhere; (5) engraving — scribe with the stylus wetted with water and corundum powder, lines **0.2–0.5 mm** deep; (6) wash, dry, dry-rub color powder into the lines, wipe the face clean; (7) spot-check 3 grids per cylinder by re-weighing. Control points: weigh the water and scribe grid by grid; the order of acceptance before graduation is never reversed.
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: grid value **5 g of water per grid**; error of any grid **≤5%** (empirical threshold, 3 grids spot-checked per cylinder); line depth 0.2–0.5 mm; **20 wet-cloth scrubbings without losing the line**; the "local milliliter" is an engineering convention (consistency ahead of absolute accuracy).
5. **Observations to record**: actual weight per grid, level-sight meniscus reading, line depth, result of the 20 scrubbings, the 3 spot-checked grids — into the graduation verification table; the finished piece's number into the instrument ledger.
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = all grids within 5% and color surviving 20 scrubbings; rework = individual grids out of tolerance — add a corrected line and lap the old one flat; scrap = blank cracked, water lines skewed (uneven wall thickness).
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) whole-cylinder systematic offset → check whether the balance weights have drifted, or the water temperature left its range; (2) random offsets grid to grid → the blank is not round (uneven wall); (3) a single grid off → hand tremor while scribing, add a line; (4) the line fades → too shallow (deepen and re-scribe) or the powder too coarse (sieve finer).
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one cylinder with all 10 grids scribed and 3 grids re-weighed within tolerance; L2 = graduation verification tables and the numbered instrument ledger kept continuously, finished pieces under the dedicated instrument cabinet; L3 = a second person graduates one cylinder from this card alone, spot-checked grids within 5% and readings at the same grade.

## Safety Boundaries

- **Molten glass's infrared injures the eyes**: never stare at the furnace mouth or the melt surface for long; view through the slit wooden-frame shield, short looks many times (cataract is the glassworker's occupational disease — chronic harm, Iron Law Seven).
- **Crucible rupture spilling melt in the furnace**: dry-sand bunding in front of the furnace, stand to the side, check the tongs before every crucible; **water stays absolutely away from the melting furnace** (hot glass meeting water erupts in violent spatter).
- Burn treatment follows the Section 6.8 procedure: cold-water rinsing ≥15 min; for a wound from broken glass, remove the shards first, then rinse and bandage (Chapter 44 first-aid interface).
- Alkali dust and hot alkali liquor: prevent inhalation, prevent eye contact, mark containers with a red band.
- Furnace-room CO rules and hazard isolation as usual; keep the annealing ash pit covered and marked against accidental stepping.
- The quench test is a destructive test: the test piece is thrown only toward a fixed bucket direction, and no one stands in the splash zone.
- **Lead-glass work**: guard against inhaling lead dust (wet sweeping, dedicated crucibles, separate disposal of crucible-wash water); **lead-glass vessels are forbidden from holding food or drinking water** (the Section 14.12 red line; a violation means the whole batch is destroyed).
- Blowing work at the mouthpiece: keep the mouthpiece end cool (swap wet-cloth wrappings in rotation); when reheating at the furnace, hold the pipe angled tip-down so residual glass inside the tube cannot flow back and burn the lips.
