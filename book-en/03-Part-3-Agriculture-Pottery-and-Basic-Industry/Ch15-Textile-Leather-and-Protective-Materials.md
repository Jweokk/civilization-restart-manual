### 15.1 Fiber Harvesting and Processing

**Bast fibers (flax family — the mainstay)**: After harvest, bundle and **water-ret** — pool-soak 5—10 days (short in summer, long in winter; empirical value, must be field-calibrated); criterion: the outer skin separates from the woody core with ease, and fibers pull away as a web. After taking out, pound lightly with a wooden mallet, comb and rinse the fiber bundles in clean water, sun-dry into strands. Retting water teems with decay bacteria: wash hands after handling and keep wounds out of contact (reuse of the Section 7.3 warning).

**Wool**: In the shedding season, collect wool naturally shed onto branches as the primary route; shearing needs shears — pull-cutting with stone knives is inefficient and counts as a legacy shortcut B (starting point B: metal shears), or upgrade after Part 4's metal shears. Washing: knead-wash in warm ash water (Section 8.3) until the grease is out and the water runs clear, rinse clean in fresh water, sun-dry; sort out burrs and dung tags.

**Regionally optional routes** (activated per the resource-map registry): cotton (hand-roll out the seeds; yield depends on acreage); silk (boiling cocoons and reeling needs a pottery pot, Chapter 11; yields long filament — the top pick for high-strength thread and long-term fabrics); nettle and tree-bark fiber follow the existing Section 7.3 processes.

**Four fiber evaluation indices (comparative method, no instruments needed)**: length (a single fiber ≥ 2 finger-joints is spinning grade); strength (a single bundle survives 20 repeated pull-bend cycles); fineness (at equal weight, the longer the fiber the finer and better — balance + counting wraps on a standard rod); cleanliness (no skin scraps, no black spots or mildew). Sort into four grades: Grade 1 → spinning and weaving; Grade 2 → rope (Section 7.4); Grade 3 short flock → felt and stuffing; Grade 4 → fuel.

**Storage**: hang dry, turn and sun regularly; wool needs moth-proofing (smoking, herb bedding, frequent sunning); flax needs mold-proofing (shelve only when hand-feel moisture reads "dry" grade).

### 15.2 Spinning, Weaving, and Sewing Tools

**Spindle whorl + shaft (complete drop-spindle set)**:

- Spindle whorl: a pottery whorl (fired from clay per Chapter 11; disc diameter 2—3 finger-widths, thickness half a finger-width; center hole drilled with the sand-grinding of Section 9.2) or a ground stone whorl; **sort by weight class** — light whorl for fine yarn, heavy whorl for coarse yarn (empirical classes, must be field-calibrated).
- Spindle shaft: a straight hardwood shaft 40—50 cm, notched at the top to hold the yarn leader, lower end seated in the whorl hole.
- Spinning method: feed fiber onto the shaft tip, let the weighted shaft hang and spin to add twist; feed as it twists, wind as you feed. Twist direction is uniform team-wide (S-twist); plying runs the reverse direction (the reverse-twist plying principle of Section 7.4).
- **Speed–fineness relationship (v2 completion)**: for the same feed, **the faster the shaft spins and the higher the twist, the tighter, thinner, and harder the yarn; the slower the spin and the lower the twist, the coarser and softer the yarn** — so "light whorl with fine yarn" is not just a weight matter: a light whorl is easily dragged slow by the yarn and adds little twist per revolution, naturally suiting slow fine spinning; a heavy whorl has greater inertia and steadier speed, suiting fast coarse spinning. **Hand-feel criterion (◎)**: a twisted yarn segment rolled between the fingers that snaps back "crisply" has twist enough; limp looping means too little twist (give it two more turns); hardening and kinking into loops means over-twist (discard that segment and respin). Practice method: from one fiber batch spin one shaft at each of three speed classes, pull-test pairs for strength and weigh to assign numbers — the speed class at the "strength peak" is the team's standard twist speed, written into the yarn-number archive.
- Yarn quality criteria: a single spindle's yarn is visually even in thickness; breaks ≤ 3 per spindle (empirical value, must be field-calibrated); pull two yarn segments against each other — the break must not land on the join.
- Yarn quantification (primitive yarn count): wind a hank of fixed turns around the standard rod and weigh it (balance, Chapter 3) — batches are compared by this number, forming the "yarn number".
**Backstrap loom (ground loom) — the minimum configuration of a frameless loom**:

| Part | Fabrication | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Warp beams ×2 | Straight hardwood rods | One end tied to a tree/stake, the other to the waist, holding warp tension |
| Shed rod | Long straight rod | Lifts the odd warp ends, forming the natural shed |
| Heddle rod | Rod + thread loops | Lifts the even warp ends to change the shed |
| Beating sword | Hardwood/bone ground to a thin blade | Beats the weft tight |
| Shuttle | Carved wood wound with weft | Carries the weft |
| Back strap | Wide leather strap/thick cloth | Leaning back tensions the warp |

**Eight steps of plain weaving**: warping (wind the warp ends around the warp beam, passing them alternately odd/even over the two rods) → tension (brace with the back) → open shed one (the natural shed of the shed rod) → insert weft → beat → change shed (lift the heddle rod) → insert weft → beat; repeat. Key points: constant weft tension (the same tightness on every pick — consistent force on the beating sword); densest selvedge (a missed weft at the edge means a fraying edge); cloth width = shed-rod length, typically 30—60 cm (empirical value); weft density 10—15 picks per finger-width (empirical value, must be field-calibrated).

**Wool felt (wet-felting method, wool only)**: lay wool fibers in two crossed layers (layers at 90° to each other) → pour hot water (ash water is better) → roll with a wooden roller + repeated roll-pressing until the fibers entangle → criterion: tearing the edge does not separate layers. Felt becomes sheeting without any weaving; used for hats, boot liners, pads, and bellows seal rings (Chapter 12).

**Sewing**: bone needle (Section 5.4) + flax thread or sinew thread; a leather finger stall as thimble; two seam types suffice — plain seam (garment panels) and lap seam (reinforcement); whip-stitch the cloth edges to prevent unraveling. First products in value order: rope bags, coarse cloth packs, shirts, blankets.

**Spinning and weaving failure troubleshooting table**:

| Symptom | Check first | Check next | Remedy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Yarn breaks frequently | Twist too high (yarn hard and brittle) | Fiber too short or too dry | Reduce twist (slow the shaft); match fiber lengths; dampen fiber slightly before spinning |
| Yarn uneven in thickness | Unsteady fiber feed | Wrong whorl class | Fix the feed per draw (a fingertip pinch as baseline); switch to a heavy whorl for coarse yarn |
| Cloth face skews | Uneven warp tension | Loose selvedge | Equal tension per end at warping; double the selvedge ends |
| Weft density wavers sparse and dense | Inconsistent beating force | Missed shed change | Count a rhythm on the beating sword; check against a gauge every finger-width |
| Cloth narrows as it weaves | Selvedge ends not taut | Missed wefts at the edge | Add tension to selvedge ends; lock the edge |

For every bolt woven keep a labor-hours account: time warping, weaving, and finishing separately — this is the data for later piecework by division of labor and capacity planning (an extension of the Section 9.5 system).

1. **Input materials**: fiber (hemp, wool, graded per 15.1); whorl (clay: 2–3 fingers across, half a finger thick, center hole drilled by the 9.2 sand method; or ground stone), **in weight grades**; spindle shaft (straight hardwood 40–50 cm, grooved at the top to catch the yarn, seated in the whorl at the bottom).

3. **Sequence and control points**: join fiber to the shaft tip → let the hanging spindle turn to insert twist → draft and wind as you go. Two control points: **one twist direction for the whole settlement (S twist)**, reversed when plying (the 7.4 reverse method); **speed must match feed rate** — at the same feed, a faster spindle gives more twist and a tighter, finer, harder yarn.
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: shaft 40–50 cm; **no more than 3 breakages per rod** (empirical, calibrate locally); yarn count by the plain method — wind a fixed number of turns on a standard rod and weigh it on the balance; batches are compared on that number.
5. **Observations to record**: per rod — fiber type / whorl grade / breakage count / yarn weight / evenness by eye.
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = even thickness, ≤3 breakages, and a two-piece pull test that breaks in the yarn rather than at a join; rework = uneven twist (feed not matched to speed), re-spin that length; scrap = too many joins (fiber too short — back to 15.1 for another source).
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) thickness wanders → feed rate out of step with spindle speed; (2) frequent breakages → short fiber or too little twist; (3) yarn hard as cord → too much twist, slow down or feed faster; (4) yarn falls apart when pulled → twist direction opposite to the previous length (not standardized).
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one acceptable rod of yarn plus its count; L2 = five consecutive batches with stable breakage and count; L3 = a second person spins to the same count from this card alone, weights within 5%.

[Card 15-02 Eight Steps of Plain Weave on the Backstrap Loom] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: yarn (from 15-01, warp and weft prepared separately); loom parts (warp beam, shed rod, heddle rod, beater). The **minimum frame-less loom**; cloth width is set by the shed rod's length.
2. **Tools required**: backstrap for tension, beater, shed rod, heddle rod. Calibration source: weft density counted against a rule (picks per finger-width), never by eye.
3. **Sequence and control points (eight steps)**: (1) **warping** — wind the warp on the beam, dividing odd and even threads across the two rods; (2) **tension** — backstrap; (3) **first shed** — the natural shed formed by the shed rod; (4) **pass the weft**; (5) **beat**; (6) **change shed** — heddle rod; (7) **pass the weft**; (8) **beat**; then repeat 3–8. Control points: **constant weft tension** (every pick the same tightness — keep the beater stroke even); **densest selvedge** (a missed weft at the edge unravels the cloth).
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: cloth width = shed-rod length, usually **30–60 cm** (empirical); **weft density 10–15 picks per finger-width** (empirical, calibrate locally); yarn breakage ≤3 per rod.
5. **Observations to record**: per bolt — **timed in three phases**: warping, weaving, finishing (this is the data for later piecework and capacity planning); sample weft density; selvedge condition.
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = density on target, selvedge sound, no long warp breaks; rework = density wandering (beater rhythm) — cannot be fixed in place, log it for rhythm training on the next bolt; scrap = unraveling selvedge (no edge treatment) or an unrepairable run of broken warp.
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) density wanders → uneven beater force or a missed shed change; count beater strokes and check the rule every finger-width; (2) unraveling selvedge → edge not densified; (3) cloth wrinkles → uneven warp tension (odd/even not split across the two rods at warping); (4) cloth narrows as you weave → shed rod slipped.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = a first 30 cm-wide plain-weave cloth; L2 = five consecutive bolts meeting density and breakage targets; L3 = a second person weaves to the same density from this card alone.

### 15.3 Cleaning and Processing Animal Hides

**Nine-step process (raw hide → leather)**:

1. **Flaying**: the opening line runs from throat to tail; the stone-flake knife lies flat under the skin and **push-cuts** rather than pull-cuts — cutting through the hide is the largest loss; flay with the fat left on, keeping the hide's shape intact.
2. **Preservation (when processing cannot start at once)**: spread flat and shade-dry (soak in water to re-soften before use), smoke, or knead with salt (salt sources, Chapter 10). Mildew-spotted hides are downgraded and recorded.
3. **Water soak to re-soften**: 1—2 days in clean water; running water is best; change the water; a stink of over-fermentation means the hide is already damaged — discard.
4. **Fleshing and de-greasing**: stretch the hide on a wooden frame (roped at the four edges) and prop it at a slant; a bone scraper (Section 5.4)/stone scraper removes every trace of flesh and fat — **residual fat goes rancid, yellows, and throws oil spots**; scrape until the membrane under the skin is evenly whitish.
5. **Dehairing (when unhaired hide is needed)**: soak in milk of lime 3—5 days (the lime-slurry line of Chapter 13; empirical value, must be field-calibrated); pull out once the hair loosens; push the hair off with a dull blade. **Over-soaking ruins the hide** (soft and sticky means too far). Hides keeping the hair side (felt substitute, fur) skip this step.
6. **De-alkalizing**: soak and wash in clean water half a day + rub-wash with sour water (dilute acid-pickle liquor, Chapter 10) until the hide surface is no longer slippery — an incompletely de-alkalized hide stores up trouble: uneven tanning afterward.
7. **Tanning — vegetable tanning (main route)**: crush the tannin sources (oak bark, acorn cups, spruce bark — regional choice, registered under harvest quotas) and steep them in hot water (hand-feel grade: "too hot to hold long") for several hours to make tanning liquor. **Concentration-ladder method**: the first pit's liquor is weak-tea colored → move to a strong-tea-colored pit the next day → the final pit dark brown, raising concentration step by step (visual grading, uniform team-wide). Submerge the hide fully and turn it daily; total time 5—15 days (empirical value, must be field-calibrated; double it for thick hides).
8. **Full-tannage criterion (core acceptance)**: (1) cut a strip from an edge or corner and inspect the cross-section — brown penetrates the full thickness, no white core at the center (a white core = not through; it re-hardens and rots once wet); (2) **boiling-water test**: boil an edge/corner scrap in boiling water for 3 minutes — no curling, no hardening into glue — raw hide curls instantly; this test sorts true from false in one shot.
9. **Oiling and setting**: squeeze out the water to half-dry; knead warm grease (fish-liver oil/animal fat/emulsified brain) into the flesh side until absorbed; stretch and knead soft, pulling and kneading repeatedly through shade drying; finish with half a day of smoking (insect proofing, added water resistance).

**Brain-tanning alternative route** (regions without tannin bark): each animal's own brain is roughly enough to tan its hide; dissolve it in warm water, rub it in, and work with warm humid smoking and repeated kneading — the product is soft as suede but **poor in water resistance and needs re-tanning maintenance** (performance loss labeled honestly: downgraded for damp environments, not used for field duty in rainy zones).

**Product grading**: fully tanned + passing the boiling-water test + supple = L2-grade leather; an intact grain with no knife holes is top grade; hides with knife holes are cut into small pieces (palm patch material for gloves). Use routing: thick leather → drive belts, soles, apron fronts; medium leather → vessels, bellows leather, protective gear; soft leather → garment linings, the outer layer of wound wraps.

[Card 15-03 Vegetable Tanning in Nine Steps] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: fresh hide (**flayed with the fat left on, pelt shape intact**); tannin source (oak bark, oak cups, spruce bark — chosen by region and registered with a harvesting quota); lime milk (Chapter 13); clean water; grease for finishing.
2. **Tools required**: stone flake knife, stretching frame (roped at four corners, propped at an angle), bone or stone scraper (5.4), blunt knife for dehairing, tanning pit. Calibration source: tannage is judged by **cross-section and the boiling test** — two hard criteria, not by calendar.
3. **Sequence and control points (nine steps)**:
   (1) **flay** — open from throat to tail, blade flat under the skin, **push-cut, never slice** (cuts are the biggest loss); (2) **preserve** if not working at once — flat shade-drying, smoking, or salt rub; moldy hides are downgraded and logged; (3) **soak to soften** — 1–2 days in clean water, running water best; if it stinks and ferments, discard; (4) **scrape flesh and fat** — frame and prop it, scrape clean (**residual fat goes rancid, yellows, and bleeds oil**); (5) **dehair** if a naked hide is wanted — lime milk 3–5 days, pull when the hair slips, push off with a blunt knife; (6) **delime** — half a day in clean water plus a dilute acid rub until the surface stops feeling slippery (**alkali left in makes tanning uneven**); (7) **tan** — crush the tannin source and extract in hot water, stepping the liquor **from weak tea to strong tea to deep brown**, 5–15 days total; (8) **tannage criteria** — cut a corner strip: brown penetrates the full thickness, **no white core**, and **3 minutes in boiling water does not curl**; (9) **finish** — work grease in and smoke.
   Control points: step 4 fat removal and step 8's two criteria.
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: lime milk 3–5 days (empirical, calibrate); tanning liquor stepped over 5–15 days (visual grading: weak tea → strong tea → deep brown); **tannage = no white core plus 3 min boiling without curling** (hard rule, both must pass).
5. **Observations to record**: per hide — weight / dates for each step / liquor strength and number of changes / cross-section result / boiling test result.
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = both tannage criteria met; rework = white core, return to strong liquor; scrap = rancid oil patches or mold (step 4 was not clean, or preservation was skipped).
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) white core in the center → liquor too weak or time too short; (2) yellow oil patches → fat not scraped clean; (3) hardens again when wet → not tanned through (white core); (4) uneven mottling → deliming incomplete or liquor not turned.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one hide through all nine steps passing the tannage criteria; L2 = three consecutive hides fully tanned; L3 = a second person completes one from this card alone and passes the boiling test.

### 15.4 Differences Between Plant and Animal Fibers

**Performance comparison table (this table decides all material selection)**:

| Property | Flax/cotton (plant) | Wool (animal) | Engineering implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dry strength | High | Medium | Plant fiber for load-bearing and rope nets |
| Wet strength | **Stronger** | Drops | Choose flax for wet-use fishing nets and cables |
| Elastic rebound | Low (wrinkles easily, cool against the skin) | High (lofty rebound) | Choose wool for insulation layers — it locks in still air |
| Moisture behavior | Absorbs water fast, dries briskly | Absorbs slowly, still warm when wet | Outer layer in rainy zones: wool or waterproofing treatment |
| Heat resistance | Fair (chars, does not melt) | Poor (felts and shrinks, scorches) | Near-fire workwear: plant fiber + leather |
| Biological hazard | Mildews easily | Moth-eaten easily | Respective countermeasures: frequent sunning / smoke to drive off insects |
| Weather aging | Slow sun embrittlement | Fast sun embrittlement | Prefer flax for outdoor rope nets |

**Blends and composites**: wool–flax blend (abrasion-resistant + warm workwear cloth); cloth + leather composite (protective gear: cloth lining + leather face); felt + hide (bellows sealing: felt ring + leather press plate).

**Material-selection decision table**: ropes, cables, nets → flax; sails, awnings, sacks → coarse linen; winter clothes, blankets, hat and boot liners → wool and felt; summer clothes, bandages → fine linen; near-fire work clothing → flax + leather pieces; sealing gaskets → felt; long-term wet-zone components → flax (wet strength) + preservative smoking.

### 15.5 How to Make Wear-Resistant, Heat-Resistant, and Waterproof Products

**Wear resistance (abrasion)**: density is service life — high beating density, double-layer weaving, and weaves with many interlacing points resist wear; in high-wear zones (shoulders, palms, knees, bag corners) apply leather facing or a thicker patch, **patches sewn with lap seams** — when wear reaches the patch, replace the patch, not the whole garment (the maintenance-cost idea, same lineage as the Section 9.4 repair system).

**Heat resistance (honest grading — no ordinary fiber is truly fireproof)**:

| Situation | Countermeasure |
|---|---|
| Flying sparks (walking by the kiln) | Thick wool weave/felt face — wool fibers self-extinguish well once clear of open flame |
| Brief contact heat (the level of brushing a hot handle by mistake) | Leather + felt lining, double layer |
| Sustained high heat at near-fire workstations | Every textile degrades — interception layers are mandatory: pottery, stone, and clay pads + distance; the workwear standard = leather apron + felt lining, blocking sparks and brief contact only |
| True fireproof needs (lining furnace mouths, wrapping hot pipes) | **Asbestos red line: a Class-1 carcinogen, not admitted into this stage's manufacturing chain** — only register deposit sites; any future use requires wet methods + enclosure + respiratory protection, with dry cutting, dry grinding, and beating forbidden and waste sealed for disposal. The current route = multi-layer felt + pottery-shard composite, managed as a consumable |

**Three waterproofing routes**:

1. **Grease method** (general): soak and rub with warmed animal fat/fish oil, dry in shade to oxidize — repels splashing rain for several outings; needs re-coating, and the smell travels with you.
2. **Resin method** (reinforcement): hot-applied pine resin (Section 8.5); brittle and prone to cracking alone — pine resin + tar + (if available) beeswax blended soft, for seam filling and local reinforcement.
3. **Drying-oil method** (regionally optional, the best): in flax/perilla-oil regions — soak the fabric in oil and spread it to air for several days; once the oil oxidizes into a non-tacky film you have "oilcloth", the most durable; first choice for sails and tarpaulins.

**Waterproof grade boundary (must be declared)**: splash-proof (rain beads roll off) ≠ pressure-resistant (the back stays dry under long soaking). Test: stretch the cloth and sprinkle water — beading = splash-proof; water pooling for 1 hour with a wet back = not pressure-resistant. Boat sails, water bags, and roof cloth get additional structure per the pressure requirement (multi-layer + tight seams + sealed edges); do not count on a single coating.

**Leather waterproofing**: kneaded-in grease + a smoking finish (step 9 of Section 15.3); the stitch holes are the main seepage path — wax the thread (beeswax/grease) + oil-seal after sewing.

[Card 15-04 Oilcloth and Waxed Cloth] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: cloth (coarse hemp or cotton); **drying oil** (linseed, perilla — a regional option, and the critical choice: a non-drying oil never dries); or wax. The goal is **shower resistance**, not waterproofing, and the line between them must be stated.
2. **Tools required**: dipping trough, drying rack. Calibration source: **the pour test** — stretch the cloth and pour water; beading = pass. Not by feel.
3. **Sequence and control points**: soak the cloth in oil (or work in wax) → hang for several days → when the oil has oxidized to a non-tacky film it is oilcloth. Control points: **drying oil only**; the coat must be thin and even (thick coats stay tacky and crack).
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: **grade boundary, stated openly — shower resistance (beads run off) is not water pressure resistance (dry on the back after prolonged immersion)**; test: pour water, beading = shower resistant; prolonged immersion with a dry back = pressure resistant, which this process does not achieve. Leather waterproofing is grease worked in plus a smoke finish (step 9 of 15.3); **stitch holes are the main leak path** — wax the thread and oil-seal after sewing.
5. **Observations to record**: per batch — oil type / quantity / drying days / pour test / any tackiness or cracking.
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = beading and non-tacky; rework = oil will not dry (a non-drying oil was used) — change oil and redo; scrap = coating cracked and leaking (too thick, or dried too fast).
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) never dries → non-drying oil; (2) tacky → coat too thick; (3) cracks → dried too fast, or the cloth was damp when oiled; (4) leaks at the seams → thread not waxed.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one oilcloth passing the pour test; L2 = three batches meeting beading and non-tack; L3 = a second person produces an equal result from this card alone.

### 15.6 Work Aprons, Gloves, and Protective Equipment

**Per-trade equipment table (configured to the workstation demands of Chapters 12–14)**:

| Trade | Hazards | Equipment configuration |
|---|---|---|
| Kiln/glass workers | Sparks, infrared, hot parts | Thick felt apron + leather front panel, long-sleeved woolen, wooden-frame observation face shield with narrow slit (Chapter 14), thick boots with multi-layer leather soles |
| Lime workers | Caustic dust, slaking splashes | Tightly woven flax clothing covering the whole body, mesh-curtain face shield + wet cloth over mouth and nose, oil-soaked leather gloves, immediate rinse and change of clothes afterward |
| Woodworkers/stoneworkers | Flying chips, impact, flying fragments | Two-finger leather gloves, leg guards, glass-pane eye protection (Chapter 14's crude glass + leather frame — meant to stop chips, not to be optically fine) |
| Lye work | Chemical burning | Same as the lime kit + a dedicated apron (check for etch holes every shift; replace at the first hole) |
| Hauling/animal power | Abrasion, crushing, hoof kicks | Felt waist pad, leather leggings, knotted-rope gloves (woven flax, wax-dipped) |

**Glove-making essentials**: cut and shape the pieces while the leather is wet; simplify the fingers — **two-finger style (thumb + four fingers bunched) is preferred**: quick to make and durable, at the cost of fine manipulation (fine work is done without gloves; add guards to tool handles instead, Chapter 9); turn the seams outward so they do not abrade the hand.

**Face shields and eye protection**: a leather frame set with crude glass panes (Chapter 14) — positioned as "splash blocking", with no optical grade required; the tightly woven mesh-curtain face shield — breathable while blocking coarse particles. **The honest boundary of respiratory protection**: wet cloth handles coarse dust, but not smoke or toxic gas; **no cloth product of any kind stops carbon monoxide** — the only solution is the ventilation and evacuation discipline of Section 6.8; activated-carbon adsorption requires an activation process (after Chapter 23), so at this stage the countermeasures for "smoke-filled environments" are only distance and exhaust.

**Equipment maintenance system**: inspect after every shift (etch holes, scorch holes, cracks, seams); keep one spare set at key posts (the spare-parts system, Section 9.4); enter wear and tear in the ledger; two acceptance tests for gear — the **spark test** (apron: charcoal sparks falling on it for 3 seconds must not burn a hole through) and the **lime test** (lime worker's gloves: wet lime slurry smeared on the surface, the inside must stay dry for 30 minutes).

1. **Input materials**: heavy leather (gloves), thick wool felt (apron and front panel), densely woven hemp (lime worker's full cover), coarse glass pane and leather frame (viewing shield), mesh curtain and oiled leather.
2. **Tools required**: cutting patterns, bone needle and hemp thread (15.2 sewing), lap seam for reinforcement. Calibration source: equipment is allocated by **trade hazard**, not per head; the standard is "no injury in the worst case."
3. **Sequence and control points (fit by trade)**: (1) **kiln and glass workers** — thick felt apron with a leather front panel, long-sleeved wool, **narrow-slit wooden-frame viewing shield** (Chapter 14), multi-layer leather soles; (2) **lime workers** — densely woven hemp covering the whole body, mesh face curtain plus a damp cloth over nose and mouth, **oiled leather gloves**, wash and change immediately after work; (3) **wood and stone workers** — **two-finger leather gloves**, leg guards, coarse glass goggles with a leather frame (blocking chips, not for optical clarity). Control point: glove form follows the trade — two-finger (dexterity) and oiled (corrosion) are not interchangeable.
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: two-finger leather (wood/stone), oiled leather (lime); narrow-slit wooden-frame shield (glass, blocks glare and spatter); coarse glass with leather frame for eyes. **Electrical insulation boundary (a book-wide statement): ordinary cloth, hemp, leather, felt, and natural resin coatings are not reliable high-voltage insulation.**
5. **Observations to record**: per person — equipment type / issue date / inspection date / damage / replacement record.
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = complete for the trade and sound; rework = damage reinforced (lap seam); scrap = oiled leather hardened and no longer protective (replace it; do not re-oil to extend life).
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) gloves do not insulate heat → leather was used instead of felt; (2) cannot see through the shield → glass too coarse (but do not chase optical quality — chip blocking is the job); (3) lime worker's hands burn → ordinary leather instead of oiled; (4) shield fogs → missing mesh curtain or the damp-cloth layer.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one trade fully equipped and logged; L2 = all trades equipped with replacement records; L3 = a second person equips one trade from this card alone and passes inspection.

### 15.7 From Household Textiles to Industrial Textiles

**Industrial textile lineage (this chapter's engineering outlets)**:

1. **Drive belts**: leather loop belts — cut thick leather into strips and close them with diagonal lap seams (thread + tar glue, Section 8.5) into a loop; or double-layer woven belt soaked in wax oil. Performance boundary: **low speed, light load** — usable for wood-shaft power transmission (Chapter 21); elongation is the main failure (slip means re-tension; inspect once per working hour); load rating taken as "breaking load ÷ 5" (calibrated by the Section 7.4 method, the seam joint tested separately).
2. **Filter cloth**: medium-density plain weave — lime-slurry washes (Section 13.3 sand washing and mortar sieving), lye clarification (Section 14.2), dye and chemical liquors (long-term, Chapter 23). Two density grades for coarse/fine filtration; failure criterion: a sudden drop in filtration rate (clogged — backwash: pat from the reverse side with clean water); replace at the first hole.
3. **Sieves (screens)**: woven cloth stretched on wooden frames. **Mesh standard**: graded by holes per finger-width — coarse 4 holes, medium 8 holes, fine 16 holes (empirical value, must be field-calibrated; reproduce from the team's standard sieve as the reference piece). Uses: sifting flour (Chapter 10), sand grading (grain sizes after panning, Chapter 11), ore grading (previewed in Chapter 16). The standard sieves are numbered into the archive — mesh is the "particle-size language", uniform across the team.
4. **Sails and tarpaulins**: oil-treated coarse linen (Section 15.5); raft sails (raft + sail rig, Section 7.7), rain cover for open-air stockyards, wagon covers.
5. **Seals and gaskets**: felt rings (bellows piston sealing, Section 12.5; valve-plate gaskets), leather gaskets (pipe joints, clack valves), oil-soaked flax oakum (threaded and pipe seals are long-term; for now used on pottery pipe joints, Section 13.6).
6. **Skin bags and diaphragms**: bellows leather faces (the leather bellows of Chapter 12), river-crossing flotation bags, wine and water skin bags (storage and transport, Chapter 10).
7. **Rope nets for hoisting**: cargo nets, hoisting nets (rope extensions of Section 7.4), fishing nets — knots uniform (a double-sheet-bend variant), mesh counted in finger-widths.

**Electrical insulation boundary (a book-wide declaration)**: ordinary cloth, flax, leather, felt, natural resin coatings — **none of them may serve as reliable high-voltage electrical insulation**. The mechanism in one sentence: once these materials absorb moisture their insulation strength falls by several orders of magnitude — dry cloth can still separate static and the contacts of small low-voltage experiments, but **damp cloth is nearly a conductor**. Insulation in Part 6's electricity chapters (Chapters 30–32) needs a dedicated scheme: dry sealing, glass/ceramic parts, oil- and wax-impregnated composite structures. At this stage the electrical role of textiles is limited to serving as temporary spacers in the static-electricity experiments before Chapter 30 — and always labeled "unreliable". This is written down to keep someone in the future from wrapping cables in oilcloth — that would burn down all of Part 6's effort.

**Mechanical calibration system**: every batch of woven belts, leather rings, and seam joints gets static-load grading per Section 7.4 — breaking value ÷ 5 as working load, ÷ 10 where people are hoisted; calibrated values sewn into the article (pigment marking, Section 8.4). Industrial textiles, like rope, are "components with dossiers", not "a piece of cloth".

[Card 15-06 Drive Belts: Making and Static Load Rating] (eight fields)

### 15.8 Plant Dyeing Line: Starting from Three Colors

**The three-color varieties (activated per local resource registry; a missing color is not forced)**:

| Color | Dye source | Sourcing and initial processing | Registry points |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blue | Indigo (indigo-plant leaves — use whichever grows locally) | Leaves soaked and fermented in ash water 5–7 days (short in summer, long in winter; empirical value), scoop out leaves and stir the liquor, let it settle, take the blue mud from the bottom | Planting acreage and annual indigo-mud yield entered in the accounts |
| Red | Madder root | Roots two years old or more, washed, shade-dried, crushed and bagged | Harvest quota and replanting plan (same system as tannin bark) |
| Yellow | Pagoda-tree flower buds | Harvested at bud stage and shade-dried (color lightens one grade after flowering) | Dried flowers must reach "dry"-grade hand-feel before storage |

**Basic dye-bath ratio (empirical starting point, gradient calibration)**: dry dye weight = cloth weight × 1 (that is, 1:1). Field calibration method: cut 6 pieces of 10×10 cm white cloth from one batch, dye two pieces at each of the 0.5:1, 1:1, and 2:1 dye levels, air-dry, compare colors and run the three fastness tests, and take the lowest level at which further darkening is no longer obvious as the team's set level — saving material and putting data on file.

**Setting up the indigo vat (cold dyeing — the only color that never touches fire)**: in a pottery vat (Chapter 11), proportion by volume: indigo mud 1 part + clarified ash water 10 parts, add a fermentation starter (a handful of wheat bran or a bowl of rice-wine mash), and drip a few drops of animal fat on the liquid surface as a protective film. Set it in a warm spot by the stove and stir once each morning. **Vat-alive criterion**: the liquor is yellow-green, with blue-violet foam floating on the surface (the "indigo bloom"). **Vat-dead criterion**: the liquor turns black, smells of rotten eggs, and cloth entering the vat takes no color — discard the liquor and rebuild (the indigo mud is recovered, not wasted). Dyeing: wet the cloth through in clean water, wring to half-dry, immerse 5–10 min and lift out — at this point it is yellow-green; let it oxidize in air 10 min and it turns blue; light blue one dip, medium blue three dips, deep blue five or more dips — **the dip count is the color number**, entered in the cloth archive. A vat can be kept alive for months, topping up ash water and indigo mud daily against consumption.

**Madder red and pagoda-tree yellow (hot dyeing)**: clean water stands one finger above the cloth; the dry material goes into a linen bag hung in the bath. Bring the madder to the "hot to the hand but a fingertip can stay 3 seconds" grade (about 60–70 ℃, ◎ hand-feel; misjudgment compensation: touch three points along the pot rim and average), hold 45–60 min, **must not boil** — boiled madder gives brown, not red; pagoda-tree flowers at the same temperature 30 min. The cloth must be mordanted first, then dyed; turn it often during dyeing (insufficient turning means blotchy dyeing).

**Three-mordant comparison system (a controlled test every team runs once)**: 9 pieces of white cloth from one batch are mordanted in three groups — the alum group (alum at 10% of cloth weight, dissolved in warm water, soaked 1 h), the iron-rust-water group (the yellow-brown liquor from iron filings soaked 7–10 days in dilute acid-pickle liquor, soaked 30 min), the ash-water group (concentrated ash water, soaked 1 h) — then all dyed together in one pot. The results are registered as a "color chart": alum-mordanted colors are the brightest (pagoda flowers give bright yellow, madder true red); iron-rust water turns colors dull (yellow to army green, red to purple-brown, usually with higher fastness); ash water gives a dingy yellow. From then on the whole team picks colors by the chart, not by memory.

**Three-grade color-fastness testing (all three tests must pass before release)**:

| Test | Conditions | Grading |
|---|---|---|
| Wash fastness | 5×5 cm cloth sample, scrub-washed in warm ash water 5 min × 3 rounds, compared side by side with the unwashed original | Fading under one grade = top; one to two grades = medium; more than two grades = bottom |
| Light fastness | Half the sample covered, sunned facing south a full 7 days, uncovered and compared | Covered/uncovered difference within one grade = top; two grades = medium; worse = bottom |
| Rub fastness | Rub back and forth 20 times with dry white cloth pressed firm | White cloth barely stained = pass; obvious staining = fail |

Grading usage: workwear and sack cloth take "medium" or above; flags, archive-marking cloth, and name-registered cloth take "top"; dyed cloth that passes only the wash test is restricted to indoor items and labeled "no outdoor sunning". Each cloth batch keeps a 5×5 cm color sample pinned to the archive (dye batch number + mordant + one ledger line of the three test results).

**Dedicated dye-pot system (red line)**: dye pots, mordant pots, and iron-rust-water jars are all dedicated and numbered (scratch-marked before firing or pigment-marked), stored in a separate zone on separate shelves from food pots, with uses registered in the ledger; **any pot or jar that has ever boiled dye or tanning material never returns to the food line** — pigment, tannin, and iron-salt residues cannot be washed back to food grade. Washing waste goes into seepage pits far from wells and vegetable gardens; pouring it into rivers and ponds is forbidden.

> **[Process Card 15-07] Plant Dyeing Three-Color Line** (summary form)
> ① Inputs: fully scoured white cloth (wet through, wrung half-dry); dry dye starting 1:1 (indigo mud proportioned per vat); mordant chosen from three (alum at 10% of cloth weight / iron-rust water / concentrated ash water). ② Tools: numbered dedicated dyeing pottery pot, dye vat, mordant jars, linen dye bags, stirring rod — check for cracks before use; leak-test the dye vat with clean water for half a day first. ③ Order: mordant → wring half-dry → enter the bath → hold temperature and turn often → lift out and wash until the water runs clear → shade-dry (no sun-baking). ④ Parameters: bath ratio starting 1:1 (gradient sets the level); hot dye 60–70 ℃, madder 45–60 min, pagoda-tree flowers 30 min; indigo vat 5–10 min per dip, dip counts recorded as color numbers. ⑤ Records: dye batch number, dye lot, mordant, dips/duration, and color name into the cloth archive. ⑥ Criteria: all three fastness tests ≥ medium = qualified; color too light = redye deeper (rework path); blotchy = troubleshoot per ⑦. ⑦ Troubleshooting: blotchy color → cloth not wet through → liquor not stirred through → pieces folded over each other mid-dye; no color → vat ash water too strong → temperature too low → dye exhausted (check against old-batch color samples). ⑧ Evidence: each batch keeps a 5×5 cm color sample pinned to the archive with the three test results attached.

[Card 15-07 Plant Dyeing: Three Colors to Start] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: white cloth or yarn; dye sources activated by local register (**never force a color you do not have**) — blue: indigo (polygonum, baphicacanthus, or isatis leaf); red: madder root (two years or older); yellow: sophora flower buds.
2. **Tools required**: dye pot, lye water (indigo), cloth bags for crushed roots; **temperature by hand** — "hot enough that you can hold 3 seconds" is about 60–70 °C.
3. **Sequence and control points**: **blue** — leaves plus lye water steeped to rot for **5–7 days** (shorter in summer), stir and dip, **5–10 min per dip**, repeated; **red** — madder root washed, shade-dried, crushed into a bag, dye hot at **60–70 °C, never boiling**; **yellow** — sophora buds picked at bud stage and shade-dried; buds picked after flowering are a grade paler. Control points: **madder must not boil** (boiling turns red brown and dull); hot-dye temperature judged by three-point touch.
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: dye bath ratio — dry dye weight = cloth weight × 1 (1:1) to start, set by a 0.5/1/2 gradient; hot dye 60–70 °C; **indigo vat 1:10 (indigo paste : lye water by volume)**, 5–10 min per dip; **fastness in three tests** — wash, sunlight, rub; workwear ≥ medium, standard cloth = high (hard rule).
5. **Observations to record**: per batch — dye type and amount / bath ratio / temperature / number of dips / three fastness results / cloth weight change.
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = fastness at the grade for the use (medium for workwear, high for standard cloth) and even hue; rework = too pale (more dye or more dips); scrap = heavy fading (missing mordant, or cloth not wetted through).
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) uneven color → cloth not wetted through; soak before dyeing; (2) red goes dull → it was boiled; madder takes 60–70 °C only; (3) blue pale and fading → too few dips or lye not alkaline enough; (4) rubs off → insufficient rinsing of loose dye.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one piece in each of three colors with all three fastness tests; L2 = three batches per color at stable fastness; L3 = a second person dyes to the same fastness from this card alone.

### 15.9 Felting and Wadding

**Wet-felting detailed procedure (targeting the third tier: another person can reproduce it)**:

1. **Prepare the wool**: take Grade-3 short-flock wool (Section 15.1 grading) and pick out every burr; felting needs 250–400 g/m² of dry wool (empirical value; calibration method in the square-sample test of step 4).
2. **Lay the crossed wool layers**: lay 4–6 layers to the finished-product pattern, each layer along one direction, adjacent layers turned 90° (crosswise); lay out each side 2–3 finger-widths larger than the template — felt shrinks.
3. **Soap-liquor temperature**: warm ash water 50–60 ℃ (hand-feel grade: hot to the hand but a fingertip can stay 3 seconds). Too cold and the scales do not open and felting is slow; too hot and bare-hand work is impossible. Mist it on evenly, **one spraying per laid layer**, until the wool layers are fully wet with no free water standing.
4. **Rolling rhythm ◎**: first pat lightly through a cloth 5 min (keeps the wool layers from shifting) → knead gently in circles with the full palm 10 min (no grabbing with the fingers) → roll back and forth with a wooden roller, 20 passes per set, flipping and turning 90° at each set, 6–8 sets in all; at the end of each set knead the four corners 1 min (the corners get the least rolling). **Practice method**: make 6 pieces of 20×20 cm square samples from one wool batch — 4/6/8 layers × light/heavy rolling, one each; tear the edges and weigh them the same way, and take the layer class and set count where "the edges do not separate and the weight is lightest" into the felt archive; a new worker passes 3 square samples before touching production pieces. Misjudgment compensation: when hand-feel cannot be trusted, replace the judgment with weighing + the edge-tear test.
5. **Finishing**: rinse the alkali out with warm water until the water runs clear, tear the edges even, level the sheet on a board, shade-dry one day, flip and dry again; when dry, trim the edges to the template and return the offcuts to the pile for new felt.

**Felt density acceptance (both criteria required)**: (1) **the edge does not separate when torn** — tear the edge by hand: the torn cross-section's fibers are entangled into a body and the layers do not part (layer separation means rework and re-rolling); (2) **weighing sets the thickness** — weigh each piece, record "g/m²", and compare against the felt class: light by more than 15% = too thin — liner and padding items may be downgraded, sealing items are scrapped. Extra check: press hard with a thumb; it must rebound with no dead dent. Bellows seal rings (Chapter 12) add one more: seated in its groove, it must not be pullable out by hand.

**Shrinkage calibration**: laid-out dimensions ÷ finished dimensions = shrinkage, generally 20–30% (empirical value, floating with wool type and rolling intensity) — each team measures it once and records it; thereafter work back from shrinkage to the laid-out size instead of guessing.

**Wadding filling process (winter clothes/bedding)**: loose wool stuffed directly rolls and clumps; it must be made into sheets: cut coarse linen into inner and outer layers sized to the garment panel/bed cover, tear the wool flock even and spread it (winter clothes 300–500 g per garment; bedding 2–3 kg per bed, doubled in cold regions), and fix it with quilting — **quilting lines one fist apart (about 10 cm), crisscrossing into a net**, with the wool weighed out per cell and spread evenly. Bedding warmth acceptance (primitive method): the sleeper wears a single layer and sleeps three nights running; waking with a back that does not feel cold = pass; if cold, add 500 g of wool and retest. A burst wadding panel leaking wool: patch and quilt it — do not scrap the whole piece.

> **[Process Card 15-08] Wet-Felt Felting and Wadding** (summary form)
> ① Inputs: Grade-3 short-flock wool (burrs picked out) 250–400 g/m²; warm ash water 50–60 ℃; templates. ② Tools: wooden roller, flat board or reed mat, water sprayer, scale (Chapter 3), cutting knife — check the roller for straightness; a bent roller presses unevenly. ③ Order: lay crossed layers → mist each layer wet → pat through cloth → knead → rolling sets (flip and turn) → wash out the alkali → shade-dry → trim edges to the template. ④ Parameters: 4–6 layers; 6–8 rolling sets, 20 passes per set; shrinkage 20–30% (self-measured and recorded). ⑤ Records: wool batch, layer count, rolling sets, dry weight and shrinkage. ⑥ Criteria: edges do not tear apart + weight reaches its class = qualified; layer separation = rework and re-roll; weight light by more than 15% → downgrade per use or scrap. ⑦ Troubleshooting: layer separation → layers under-wetted → kneading too short; white spots with loose wool → locally not soaked through; loose corners → corners under-rolled. ⑧ Evidence: each batch keeps a 10×10 cm felt sample pinned to the archive (weight and thickness recorded).

[Card 15-08 Wet Felting and Wadding] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: third-grade short wool (graded per 15.1, picked free of grass and burrs); **warm lye water at 50–60 °C** (hand grade: hot, but a fingertip can stay 3 seconds). Too cold and the scales will not open; too hot to handle.
2. **Tools required**: interleaf cloth, wooden roller, pattern, balance for weight. Calibration source: weight and shrinkage by weighing and square-sample calibration.
3. **Sequence and control points**: prepare wool → **lay in cross layers** (4–6 layers, each layer in one direction with the next turned 90°, 2 fingers larger than the pattern all round) → pour warm lye water → **pat through a cloth for 5 min first** (stops the layer shifting) → **rub with the full palm in circles for 10 min** (no finger-grabbing) → **roll 20 passes forward and back** → rinse in warm water until clear → tear edges straight and level on a board → dry a day in shade, turn, dry again → trim to pattern. Control points: cross-layer direction, water temperature, and the **pat–rub–roll order, which cannot be reversed**.
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: dry wool **250–400 g/m²**; **shrinkage 20–30%**; water 50–60 °C; wadding quilt spacing **one fist (about 10 cm) in both directions**; quilts 2–3 kg each; master pattern checked monthly, **replaced when the perimeter differs by half a finger** (hard rule).
5. **Observations to record**: per batch — wool weight / layer count and direction / water temperature / rolling passes / finished size and weight / shrinkage.
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = **torn edge does not shed plus weight on target** (over 15% light means downgrade); rework = felt not bonding (water too cool or not rolled enough), back through the process; scrap = uneven thickness (uneven laying), remake as small items.
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) will not felt → water too cool or not enough soap; (2) uneven thickness → uneven laying; (3) shrinks too much → over-rolled; (4) edges shed → no 2-finger allowance or edges not torn straight.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one felt passing the edge and weight check; L2 = three batches with stable weight and shrinkage; L3 = a second person makes felt to the same weight from this card alone.

### 15.10 Sewing Tool Upgrades: the Needle System, Thimbles, and the Template System

**Bone needle specification system (three classes, matched to material — a deepening of the Section 5.4 bone tools)**:

| Class | Materials | Specification (empirical, team standard) | Criterion |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coarse needle | Leather, felt, wool blankets | 3 finger-widths long, shaft as thick as a matchstick, eye passing two-ply sinew thread | The tip scores leather leaving a line without slipping; bend the shaft and it springs straight on release |
| Medium needle | Coarse linen, wadding face and back | 2.5 finger-widths long, eye passing single-ply flax thread | Same as above |
| Fine needle | Fine linen, silk, bandage hems | 2 finger-widths long, shaft finely ground round, eye passing single-ply fine thread | After the needle passes through cloth and the thread is pulled, the needle holes close on themselves |

**Eye-drilling method (the main failure cause is splitting from the face side)**: first spin a dimple with a bone awl on both faces at the hole position (prevents drift), then drill through from both faces **drilling toward each other** with a fine bone awl or hardwood drill; wet-grind the hole wider with fine sand and water, and finally polish the bore by **sawing a wetted flax thread charged with fine sand back and forth** until the bore wall is smooth. Criterion: after threading, pull back and forth 20 times — no burrs rise at the hole edge and the thread does not break. Broken needles are recovered, cut short, and reworked into a lower class — never thrown away.

**Two thimble builds**: the **leather thimble** — cut a 2×3 finger-width piece of thick leather and stitch three layers into a ring, fit it on the second joint of the middle finger, and push the needle tail against the leather face; quick to make, a consumable, kept ready under the spare-parts system. The **bone thimble** — a bone plate ground into a ring band, its outside densely ground with dimples; the needle tail seats in a dimple without slipping; durable but slow to make. Acceptance: push-stitch 50 stitches in a row — no bruising on the back of the finger, the ring neither turns nor slips off. **Procedure red line: leather and multi-layer materials require a thimble; bare-finger pushing is forbidden** — the labor-hours lost to a needle through the finger (an injured hand off work) exceed all the labor of making a thimble.

**Cutting board and template system**: the cutting board — a flat hardwood board 2 finger-widths thick or more, planed flat and sanded smooth, its face waxed thin; all cutting is done on the board (it protects the board and the blade alike, and keeps the cut pieces true). **Templates have two levels**: paper templates (trial plates) — thin bark or waste pulp board, used for trying and adjusting shapes, recovered after use; leather templates (**master templates**) — cut from thick vegetable-tanned leather, edges waxed against moisture, the sole basis for batch cutting. The master-template system: one item, one number, stored hanging in compartmented wooden boxes; withdrawal logged, returned to the box after use; **master templates may never be re-cut** — a shape change requires a new template with a new number and the old number destroyed, preventing "drifting further off-shape with each change". **Monthly master-template check**: compare the perimeter with the check plate (a newly made reference plate); off by more than half a finger-width, the template is demoted to a trial plate and a new master template is cut. Template wear is a silent failure: invisible piece by piece, yet ten pieces of one batch will not sew together — hence periodic comparison, not sharp eyes.

**Tool-based thread waxing (wax block + thread groove)**: a wax block (beeswax, or the Section 8.5 resin-wax formula) is set at a slant into a wooden base, with a V-shaped thread groove cut into the base top facing the block's lower edge; press the thread end into the groove, hold the base with one hand and draw the thread with the other — as the thread passes through the groove the block's upper edge presses an even coat of wax onto it. Criteria: the waxed thread shows no white core to the eye (the wax has penetrated); drawn back and forth 10 times through leather over 30 cm, it neither fuzzes nor splits plies. Why tool it up: pinching a wax block by hand gives now too much, now too little — too much wax and knots will not hold, too little and the thread will not resist wear; the groove keeps the wax dose constant. Paired with the thimble in the same box, the leather-sewing workstation is outfitted in one pass.

| Leather hardens and shrinks after tanning | Not tanned through (white core) | Extend the time in the final pit; re-verify with the boiling-water test |
| Soapy slipperiness, de-alkalizing incomplete | Lime residue | Extend de-alkalizing; re-wash with sour water |
| Weft skews on the cloth face | Uneven warp tension | Equal tension per end at warping |
| Selvedge frays apart | Loose selvedge ends / missed wefts | Double the selvedge ends + lock the edge |
| Oilcloth turns tacky in storage | Folded before the drying oil had fully cured | Spread to air until non-tacky, then roll |
| Glove etch holes missed in inspection | No light check | Inspect each finger against the light; replace at the first etch hole |
| Blotchy uneven dyeing (cloudy patches) | Cloth not wet through before dyeing → liquor not stirred through | Redye the whole vat: wet through in clean water, wring half-dry, re-enter; stir the liquor before dyeing and turn often during |
| Felt delaminates (tears into two skins) | Layers under-wetted → kneading too short → too few rolling sets | Back to the line: re-spray warm soap liquor, add one kneading set and re-roll; re-verify by the edge-tear test |
| Template wear distorts a whole batch | Worn master-template edges not retired → no periodic comparison | Cut a new master under a new number, demote the old plate to trial; spot-check every piece of this batch against the plate |

## C. Unresolved Dependencies

- Metal shears: wool shearing and leather cutting are efficiency-limited, with stone-knife pull-cutting standing in — upgrade after Part 4.
- Fine weaving and wide-width looms: upright frame looms and multi-heddle figure weaving are upgrade routes; current cloth width is limited by the backstrap loom.
- Activated carbon and respiratory protection: wet cloth is only a stopgap; a true gas mask awaits the Chapter 23 chemistry.
- Asbestos and other mineral refractory fibers: pending resource-survey registration; not admitted into the manufacturing chain until verified.
- High-voltage electrical insulation materials: cloth and leather are explicitly unusable (the Section 15.7 declaration); awaiting Part 6's dedicated route (glass, ceramics, oil-wax composites).
- Dyeing stability: natural-dye fastness is uneven; standardization is discussed only after test data are registered.

## Safety Boundaries

- Retting water and tanning liquor are both putrid/strongly alkaline environments: wash hands after handling and keep wounds out of contact; cover and mark the lime soak pits.
- The milk-of-lime dehairing step executes every lime safety procedure of Chapter 13.
- Run the boiling-water test from an arm's length from the boiling pot, handling with stick tongs, to prevent scalding (Section 6.8).
- No open flame in the oilcloth drying area (oxidizing, heat-releasing oilcloth piles can self-ignite) — dry it spread out in single layers, never piled in sunlight.
- Cut leather with the blade edge facing away from the body, and fix the cutting table firmly; lash the hide-stretching frames to the Section 7.4 standard.
- Bellows leather faces and valve skins are safety components: seal failure = furnace gas blowing back; inspect every shift.

## Acceptance Criteria

- **L1 proof-of-principle**: spin one spindle of qualified yarn; weave the first 30 cm-wide plain-weave cloth; take one hide through the nine-step process and pass the boiling-water test.
- **L2 repeatable manufacture**: yarn break rate and cloth weft density at target (Section 15.2 criteria) for 5 consecutive batches; 3 consecutive hides fully tanned by vegetable tanning (no white core in cross-section + boiling-water test); waterproof items pass the splash test; protective gear passes the spark test and the lime test; woven belts complete static-load calibration with sewn-in markings.
- **L3 sustainable production**: two or more flax/wool raw-material sources with monthly production accounts (linked to Chapter 10's agricultural accounts); tannin-bark harvest quotas and regrowth cutting plans on the books; at least two independently certified graduates each in textiles and leatherwork (3 consecutive qualified pieces, the Section 9.5 criterion); industrial textiles (drive belts, filter cloth, standard sieves, sealing felt rings) supplied to the Chapter 12–14 workstations under the spare-parts system with wear-and-loss accounts; the protective-gear spare-set system in force.
