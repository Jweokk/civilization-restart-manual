# Chapter 10: Food Storage and Agriculture

> Part 3 · Agriculture, Pottery, and Basic Industry (opening chapter of this part). Part 2 established the minimal capability circle of "forage — make tools — control fire," but the whole system still runs on "eat what you gather the same day." This chapter turns food from a "thing hunted" into a "thing produced and stored." This chapter is not an agronomy textbook: agriculture matters here because it frees labor for metallurgy and precision manufacture — **no food surplus, no full-time craftspeople** (the agricultural basis for the population balancing of Chapter 37 is made good in this chapter).

## Chapter Objectives

- Establish an edibility test protocol (graded-exposure method); complete a tiered survey of local edible resources and seasonal-window records.
- Establish the three seed-selection criteria, **seed dormancy breaking**, a seed-saving system, **germination testing**, and an isolation-distance table against crossing.
- Complete evaluation of soil, water sources, and planting conditions; establish the **field method for judging the compost carbon-to-nitrogen ratio** (smell and heat) and a small-plot trial system.
- Derive the full set of farm tools from the composite-tool processes of Chapter 9.
- Establish five preservation routes — drying, smoking, fermentation, salting, and the cellar (including the ice cellar) — and **spoilage criteria**; establish **plant-derived insecticide decoction**.
- Establish domestication candidate criteria, pack transport, and a solid-wood wheel cart system.
- Produce criteria for "when agriculture beats foraging," a reserve safety line, and a granary ledger.

## Capability Boundaries

**Can do:** edible list (independently verified by two people), four to five preservation routes, a 45→90-day grain reserve safety line, draft-animal pack transport, and a rotation + compost fertility system.

**Explicitly cannot do:** pottery-grade sealed storage (Chapter 11); metal plows and deep tillage (Part 4); quantitative temperature and humidity monitoring (Chapter 25); chemical fertilizer (Chapter 23) — fertility has only two legs, rotation + compost; **the breeding cycle itself is time-dependent** — local domestication needs generations of selection and no tool can speed it up.

**External dependencies:** none (everything at this stage derives from stone- and wood-tool processes).

### 10.1 Identifying Edible Plants and Their Risks

In the early restart the biggest food risk is not "finding nothing to eat" but poisoning. Knowledge retention does not equal local botanical knowledge: what the reader remembers is family-level patterns, not verdicts on each local plant. **Protocols must replace guessing.**

**Resource survey**: work outward from the camp as the center, using the transect method of Chapter 2. For every suspected edible plant record: location, quantity, edible parts, ripening season, harvest difficulty. **The same plant differs in safety by season** (in some roots and tubers toxicity rises at fruiting), so record a fresh version each season.

[Card 10-01 Edibility Test (Graded-Exposure Method)] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: the plant sample to be tested (fresh, same plant and same part; the same plant differs in safety by season, so record one version per season); clean water for rinsing the mouth; a test record sheet. Only one sample is introduced at a time.

2. **Tools required**: stone flake or small knife (to crush the sample); drip clock and day anchor (Chapter 3, for timing); record board. Calibration source: time by drip-clock divisions, the 8 h waits checked against the day anchor at dawn and dusk; doses by body units — thumbnail-sized / thumb-tip-sized / a small palmful (Chapter 3 body-unit error statistics).

3. **Sequence and control points (seven levels, no skipping)**: (1) smell — crush the fresh sample and sniff (immediate); (2) skin — dab juice on the thin skin inside the elbow (15 min); (3) lip — touch juice to the corner of the lip (5 min); (4) tongue — a thumbnail-sized piece on the tongue tip, chew 15 min without swallowing; (5) swallow — if no reaction, swallow a piece the size of a thumbnail, wait 8 h; (6) increase — if no reaction, eat a small palmful, wait another 8 h; (7) routine — eat small amounts for 3 consecutive days. Control points: one item at a time; the tester introduces no other new food that day; any positive at any level ends the test at once with the mouth rinsed; record everything; anything that passes raw is retested cooked at levels 5–7 (raw safety and cooked safety do not imply each other).

4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: observation windows 15 min / 5 min / 15 min / 8 h / 8 h / 3 consecutive days; dose ladder thumbnail → thumb-tip → small palmful; **a conclusion enters the routine-food list only when two people test independently and agree** (empirical criterion, calibrated by the team on this card); agarics (gilled mushrooms) are excluded wholesale — the graded method does not apply, the toxic dose cannot be predicted.

5. **Observations to record**: per level — date / plant name and part / level number / start and end time / positive signals (bitter-almond odor, strong acridity, sharp sour-rot, redness swelling itching burning, stinging numbness swelling, soapy-astringent feel, nausea dizziness abdominal pain rash, delayed discomfort) / whether stopped and rinsed / tester and checker.

6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = all seven levels clear and a second person's independent retest agreeing, entered in the routine-food list; rework = a mild doubtful signal at some level (slight numbness at the lip) → retest at a lower level or retest cooked at levels 5–7, with the existing "forbidden" verdict not overturned; scrap = a clear positive at any level — the whole plant is registered "forbidden" unless another person independently repeats the full protocol.

7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) another new food introduced on the test day → symptoms cannot be attributed, void the run and start again; (2) skipping levels (smell straight to swallow) → doses stack, go back one level; (3) no rinsing after a positive → the lingering symptom is misread as worsening, rinse at once and note the time; (4) the two testers disagree → a third person repeats the full protocol and the level criteria decide; (5) trying to grade agarics → the class is excluded, no individual testing.

8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one complete run, at least 1 item reaching level 7 or a stop level; L2 = a food list of ≥20 species verified independently by two people; L3 = two or more members each complete the full protocol from this card alone (including the check), with the list and the resource map on file.

### 10.2 Seeds, Propagation Material, and Simple Breeding

**Three seed-selection criteria** (prefer the "three highs"):
1. **Edible-part yield**: edible dry-matter yield per unit area per season, including harvest efficiency (thick shells and small seed that is hard to thresh score lower);
2. **Storage quality**: low moisture, hard shell, dormancy that prevents premature sprouting, resistance to mold and insects — only seed that survives a full season of storage counts as a staple candidate;
3. **Harvest reliability**: uniform ripening; no seed drop or pod shattering after ripening; can set seed under local pests and diseases; matches the local water-and-heat season.

Anything scoring below "medium" on all three is managed only as a foraging resource and never put in the field.

**Vegetative propagation first**: tubers, roots, bulbs, shrubs that take from cuttings — vegetative reproduction holds traits from the first generation and pays off a season sooner than sexual reproduction. Any crop with a vegetative route is propagated vegetatively as the main line; sexual seed-saving serves only renewal and reinvigoration.

**Seed dormancy breaking (three treatments; try in order when the germination rate will not rise)**:

| Type | Applies to | Operation | Criterion and risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm-water soaking | hard seeds of cereals and legumes | soak half a day to one day in warm water (hand-feel "hot but a finger can go in," about 50–60 °C), then lift out and spread | water too hot cooks the embryo dead (open and inspect; discoloration = discard); sow the same day after soaking |
| Sand-scarring / nicking | hard-shelled seed (hard legume seed) | put fine sand and seed in a bag and knead until the seed coat shows a matte surface; or nick a small opening with a knife (do not injure the hilum) | stop at the matte surface; grinding through the embryo = discard |
| Cold moist stratification | fruit-tree and stone-fruit seed | layer seed with moist sand (1:3 by volume) in a breathable wooden frame, keep in a cool place for one to several months (through the cold season), then sow | keep the sand at "holds when squeezed, crumbles at a touch" moisture; if it molds, turn out and air |
| Lye soak | some wild grass seeds (also kills surface fungi) | soak in dilute lye (Section 8.3 weak grade) for 2–4 h, then **rinse clean with clear water** before sowing | too-strong lye injures the embryo (trial-sow a small sample first); sowing without rinsing is forbidden |

**Seed-saving system**:
- **Ear selection**: select superior plants in the field and harvest them separately (strong stalk, large ear, full grain, no disease or insect spots) — **not picked out of the heap after harvest**. Seed quantity = next season's sowing rate × 2 (safety factor);
- keep the seed plot separate from the food plot; after drying seed to the dryness standard of 10.5, package it separately, labeled with variety, year, and plot;
- **Viability test (germination rate)**: before sowing, bury 50 seeds in a box of damp sand (wooden trough + damp sand + straw cover), keep warm, count sprouts daily and record — **germination ≥80% (experience value, must be field-calibrated): use as field seed; 60%–80%: double the sowing rate; <60%: discard the seed** (or first apply dormancy breaking and retest — **distinguish "dormancy not broken" from "seed dead"**: if the retest still produces nothing, discard; open and inspect the embryo — white and firm is alive, brown and soft is dead).

**Isolation-distance table against crossing** (experience values, must be field-calibrated):

| Pollination type | Representative crops | Isolation distance | Auxiliary measures |
|---|---|---|---|
| Self-pollinating | legumes | ≥10 paces | natural isolation suffices |
| Cross, insect-borne | melons and squashes | ≥100 paces | or stagger flowering by 30 days |
| Cross, wind-borne | cereals (maize type) | ≥200 paces | or staggered dates + tall barrier strips |

Where distance cannot be had, stagger sowing dates: early/late sowing puts the two varieties' flowering more than a month apart; record heading and flowering dates daily to verify. Seed-saving records go into the ledger; if the germination rate falls back the following year, investigate whether last year's isolation failed.

[Card 10-04 Seed Saving and Germination Testing] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: seed from superior plants **ear-selected in the field** and harvested separately (strong stalk, large ear, full grain, no disease or insect spots — not picked out of the heap after harvest); seed quantity = next season's sowing rate × 2; damp sand for the germination box; wooden trough and straw cover; separate labels (variety, year, plot).

2. **Tools required**: damp-sand box (wooden trough + damp sand + straw cover); rule or standard measure; balance (Chapter 3, for batch weight); small knife (to open and inspect the embryo); record sheet. Calibration source: germination rate = sprouted grains ÷ 50, directly countable; sand moisture by the "holds when squeezed, crumbles at a touch" hand-feel grade (same as cold stratification).

3. **Sequence and control points**: ear-select and harvest separately in the field → keep the seed plot separate from the food plot → dry to the 10.5 dryness standard (grain <13%) → package separately, labeled variety, year, plot → before sowing bury 50 seeds in the damp-sand box, keep warm, count sprouts daily and record → if germination is <60%, first apply the four dormancy-breaking treatments and retest, separating "dormancy not broken" from "seed dead". Control points: selection happens in the field, not at the heap; seed stock is never mixed with insecticidal powder (10.5 forbids it for seed).

4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: **≥80% use as field seed / 60%–80% double the sowing rate / <60% discard** (experience value, calibrate to local conditions); 50-seed sample; seed quantity ×2 safety factor; dormancy breaking — warm water (about 50–60 °C, "hot but a finger can go in") half a day to one day, sand-scarring or nicking until the coat shows a matte surface, cold moist stratification (seed:sand = 1:3 by volume, cool place, one to several months), dilute lye (8.3 weak grade) 2–4 h then rinsed clean.

5. **Observations to record**: daily sprout count (until nothing more sprouts); embryo inspection of unsprouted grains (white and firm = alive, brown and soft = dead); moldy grain count; batch weight; the seed plot's isolation distance against the previous year (legumes ≥10 paces / melons ≥100 paces / maize type ≥200 paces).

6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = germination ≥80% (or 60%–80% sown at double rate); rework = dormancy not broken — retest after the four treatments; scrap = still <60% on retest, or a brown soft embryo (seed dead) → discard the seed and trace back storage damp and a failed seed-plot isolation.

7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) low germination (most common) → open an embryo first: white and firm means dormancy not broken, brown and soft means dead, then treat or discard; (2) the sand box molds → sand too wet or the straw cover unbreathable, turn out, air, and change the sand; (3) uneven sprouting → uneven temperature where the box sits, move it somewhere evenly warm; (4) the line degrades next year → last year's crossing isolation failed: check the distance or switch to staggered sowing; (5) rehumidified in store → re-dry to the dryness standard and package separately.

8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one 50-seed germination test with a record; L2 = two or more crops through the select–save–test loop (≥80%); L3 = seed records (variety / year / plot / isolation distance) in the ledger, with next year's germination traceable.

### 10.3 Soil, Water Sources, and Planting Conditions

**Three soil tests**:
1. Moist ribbon test: sand will not form a ribbon; loam forms a 2–3 cm ribbon that bends without breaking; clay forms a long ribbon that bends without breaking;
2. Settling cylinder: read the thickness ratio of the clay/silt/sand layers; loam target roughly sand 4 : silt 4 : clay 2 (experience value);
3. Structure observation: dig one spade deep and inspect the profile — good soil has root and worm channels, does not form a hard crust after rain, and breaks into blocks rather than a solid slab when dry. For compacted clay work in sand and organic matter; for sandy soil work in mostly organic matter.

**Rough acid-base judgment**: flourishing acid-loving groups (sorrel, ferns, lingonberry) suggest acidity; good growth of legumes and most cultivated grasses suggests neutral to slightly alkaline. On acid soil scatter wood ash (a small handful per pace squared) in a 2×2-pace small-plot comparison; look at the growth after one season, then scale up.

**Four water-source checks**: position (above field level so it can flow by gravity); seasonal volume (**measured in the dry season**, not on wet-season impressions); quality (taste for saltiness, check turbidity, check upstream for salt springs and animal carcasses); conveyance (open-channel distance and seepage). Saline water and polluted upstream water must not irrigate food crops.

**Salinization warning (mandatory in irrigated areas)**: a **white frost** appearing on the field surface after watering (salt carried up by the water and left by evaporation) = salinization has begun. Response order: ① stop irrigating and observe; ② flood heavily to leach salt + open drainage ditches to carry it away (**leaching without drainage only pushes the salt downward — it will rebound**); ③ mulch with straw to cut evaporation. On salted ground first plant salt-tolerant goosefoot-family plants and observe (experiential observation method, put on record).

**Terrain**: on slopes build level terraces along the contour (leveled section by section with the Type-A level frame); abandon cultivation on steep slopes to protect the soil; in low wet ground dig a pit and fill it with water as a test (drains overnight = too sandy; still standing after two days = heavy clay waterlogging, needs ditches).

**Fertility maintenance**:

**Rotation plan (start with three plots)**:

| Plot | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | legumes (builds soil) | cereals (drains soil) | fallow + green manure/compost |
| B | cereals | fallow + green manure | legumes |
| C | fallow + green manure | legumes | cereals |

No plot carries the same plant family more than two seasons running; the nitrogen fixed in legume root nodules is this stage's only "free nitrogen," and the rotation is arranged around it.

**Compost (the field method for judging the carbon-to-nitrogen ratio)**:

Raw materials come in two classes: **greens** (high nitrogen: fresh manure, legume green manure, tender grass, vegetable leaves) and **browns** (high carbon: straw, hay, sawdust, dry leaves). Target carbon-to-nitrogen ratio about 25–30:1 — **starting rule of thumb by volume: green 1 : brown 1–2** (must be field-calibrated: the pile's heat and smell are its carbon-nitrogen gauge).

[Card 10-02 Compost] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: greens (high nitrogen: fresh manure, legume green manure, tender grass, vegetable leaves); browns (high carbon: straw, hay, sawdust, dry leaves); topsoil (to inoculate microbes); water; wood ash (a little). Target carbon-to-nitrogen ratio about 25–30:1, volume starting point green 1 : brown 1–2.

2. **Tools required**: a stick (core-temperature probe); water carrier (water to "wet but not running"); straw-mud (rainy-season cover); record sheet. Calibration source: there is no instrument for the C:N ratio — **the pile's heat and smell are the gauge**, read weekly by the three-step field judgment in item 4 to calibrate the local green:brown ratio.

3. **Sequence and control points**: build in layers about a palm thick, green and brown alternating, thinner layers where manure is rich, a handful of topsoil between layers → water to "wet but not running" → turn every 2–4 weeks, outer layer to the inside → cover with straw-mud against flooding in the rainy season. Control points: minimum pile about one arm cubed (smaller loses heat too fast and never heats); a white thread-like fungal network when turning = ripening under way (a good sign).

4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: green:brown 1:1–2 by volume to start (experience value, calibrate locally); ripening 2–4 months (experience value, must be field-calibrated); turning every 2–4 weeks; minimum pile one arm cubed. The calibration method is the three-step field judgment — (1) heat: push a stick into the core, feel the end the next day, warm to hot-to-hold = fermentation normal, cool = short of nitrogen (add greens) or water (add water); (2) smell: pungent ammonia = nitrogen excess (add browns and turn), musty and flat with no ammonia = carbon excess (add greens), normal is a mild earthy smell of ripening; (3) turn and look for the white fungal network.

5. **Observations to record**: core temperature weekly (hand-feel grade + stick method); smell changes; turning dates and the fungal network; water added and rainy-season ponding; build date (to compute ripening time).

6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = black-brown, loose, odorless, raw materials unrecognizable (all four); rework = raw manure or half-ripe — keep piling, **raw manure never goes straight onto the field** (it burns roots and carries parasite eggs); scrap = flooded and stinking — spread out to dry, then rebuild the pile.

7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) no heat (most common) → check nitrogen, then water, then pile size, and add greens / add water / combine piles; (2) pungent ammonia → too many greens, add browns and turn; (3) musty, flat, never ripens → carbon excess, add greens; (4) maggot outbreak → the meat and feces share is too high, halve it and cover with more soil; (5) flooded and stinking in the rainy season → straw-mud cover and drainage ditches, spread out to dry and rebuild.

8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = a heating record for one pile; L2 = one batch reaching ripeness plus its mix record; L3 = a local "green:brown ratio — heating — ripening time" comparison table in the ledger.

### 10.5 Food Drying, Smoking, Fermentation, Salting, and Storage

**Unifying principle — water activity**: what microbes can use is not total moisture but "free water." Drying pushes water content below the level at which microbes can act: grain <13%, dried meat <10%, dried fruit 15%–20% (experience values, must be field-calibrated). Salt and sugar lock water up as bound water, lowering activity the same way. Fermentation is the third route: let useful microbes occupy the ecological niche first, and the acid they produce excludes spoilage organisms. **The three routes can be stacked** (salt then sun-dry; ferment then smoke), and stacking multiplies keeping time several-fold.

**Dryness standard (all four met at once)**: teeth bite with a crisp snap; it breaks on bending; the fracture shows no dark damp patches; pressed to the lips it feels not cool. Spot-check and re-weigh on the 7th day in storage — anything that gained weight from rehumidification is spread out and re-dried.

**Drying facilities**: drying racks 1 cubit off the ground, straw mats on top, a straw shelter overhead; cut meat and fish into strips (≤1 finger thick), shade them to half-dry before sunning (avoids a surface crust that locks water in).

**Smoking**: set a smoking frame over a small fire pit, meat ≥1 arm's length from the fire, the "hand can rest beside it indefinitely" grade of low fire, 2–4 continuous days (experience value). Smoking = slow low-temperature drying + preservative chemicals in the smoke + surface protein coagulation — three layers of preservation. Smokehouse ventilation follows the Section 6.8 Iron Rules; the watch may not sleep on duty.

**Salting**: enable when there is a salt source (registered in Chapter 8) — layer meat/fish/vegetables with salt (10–20% of raw weight, experience value, must be calibrated) and press with a weight stone; brine must cover the material; skim off any white film. Salt is a strategic material: **salting production is scheduled jointly with the chemical-industry salt of Chapter 23** — preserved foods must not crowd out life-support salt.

**Three fermentation starting lines**:
1. **Sour pickled vegetables**: knead leaves by hand with salt (3%–5% of vegetable weight) until liquid is drawn, pack into a jar / wooden trough / stone vat, press with a weight stone so the brine covers everything (**anaerobic conditions are the make-or-break**), souring begins in 5–10 days (experience value);
2. **Sourdough**: keep a starter culture, feed it daily with fresh flour and warm water;
3. **Soured milk** (once there is a dairy animal): fresh milk at room temperature sets sour in half a day; smells sweet-sour, edible.

**Spoilage-criteria table for fermentation and storage (food-safety red lines)**:

| Sign | Verdict | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Localized white film on the brine surface (film yeast) | mild | skim it off, top up with stronger brine, eat soon |
| Overall stink, sliminess, blackening | spoiled | **discard all** |
| Sour pickles gone soft and mushy after long storage, with a hydrogen-sulfide rotten-egg smell | spoiled | discard all |
| Dried meat gone soft, ammonia smell, maggot holes | spoiled | discard all (**no washing, no boiling, no cutting away**) |
| Grain heap heating, clumping, local darkening | mold starting | spread and sun immediately and sort — **any visible mold spots: discard the whole batch** |
| Nuts and oil seeds rancid-smelling (fat oxidation) | degraded | discard; rancid fats form harmful compounds, heat cannot save them |

**Plant-derived insecticides (dual use, field and granary; part of integrated pest control)**:
- **Material selection** (per the local survey, bitter-acrid plants first): chinaberry leaves/bark, mugwort, tobacco leaves, garlic, chili, pyrethrum flowers (if registered);
- **Decoction**: chop fresh leaves (half the quantity if dry), add 10× water, simmer in a pottery pot or stone boiling (Chapter 6) on a low fire down to about half, strain off the dregs; add a little soap solution (Chapter 23 soap or lye) as a spreading-sticker agent (so the liquid coats the leaf surface instead of rolling off);
- **Use**: spray at dusk or on overcast days (sunlight breaks it down and kills its effect); in the granary use the dry powder — mugwort powder mixed into the grain (1–2% of grain weight, experience value) or scattered in layers;
- **Honest positioning**: primarily a broad-spectrum **repellent**, not an insecticide — log effect as insect density and grain-loss rate against an unsprayed control sack; no kill-rate promises; **never mix into seed stock** (may hurt germination; trial on a small sample first);
- harvest the raw materials each season, dry, store, and register (resource map supplement page).

**Storage containers and pest defense**: grain enters storage in baskets / mud-lined baskets raised half a cubit off the ground against damp and rats; turn and sun or smoke monthly against insects; the ledger records intake quantity, moisture spot checks, and losses to mold, insects, and rats each logged separately — **a seasonal loss rate >10% stops everything for an investigation**.

[Card 10-05 Plant-Derived Insecticide Decoction] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: bitter-acrid materials (per the local survey: chinaberry leaves/bark, mugwort, tobacco leaves, garlic, chili, pyrethrum flowers if registered), fresh leaves chopped or dry leaves at half quantity; water (10× volume); a little soap solution (Chapter 23 soap or lye) as a spreading-sticker; mugwort dry powder (for granary mixing).

2. **Tools required**: pottery pot or stone boiling (Chapter 6, low-fire grade); straining cloth or woven basket; sprayer (gourd, perforated vessel); balance or standard measure (Chapter 3, for grain weight and powder weight). Calibration source: strength by "10× water, simmered to about half" plus the half-quantity rule for dry leaves; the mixing ratio weighed at 1%–2% of grain weight.

3. **Sequence and control points**: harvest materials each season, dry and register (resource map supplement page) → chop fresh leaves (half the quantity if dry), add 10× water → simmer on a low fire in a pottery pot or stone boiling down to about half → strain off the dregs → add a little soap solution as a spreading-sticker (so the liquid coats the leaf instead of rolling off) → **spray at dusk or on overcast days** (sunlight breaks it down); in the granary use dry powder — mugwort powder mixed into the grain (1%–2% of grain weight) or scattered in layers. Control points: the sticker is mandatory; **seed stock is never mixed** (it may hurt germination; trial on a small sample first).

4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: 10× water, simmered to about half; dry leaves at half quantity; 1%–2% for grain mixing (experience value, calibrate locally); sprayed vegetables are picked only after rain has washed them; **no kill-rate promises** — it is primarily a broad-spectrum repellent, and effect is booked as insect density and grain-loss rate against an unsprayed control sack.

5. **Observations to record**: material type and fresh/dry state, harvest date; simmering time and remaining volume; spray date, weather, and time of day; insect density and grain-loss rate in the treated and control sacks; any leaf scorch.

6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = the treated sack shows lower insect density or grain loss than the control and no leaf scorch appears; rework = too dilute and ineffective — simmer longer or add material and re-boil; scrap = crop damage (leaf scorch) or a fall in germination after seed mixing — stop that batch and use it for spraying only.

7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) no effect after spraying → too weak (over-simmered or too much water) or sunlight broke it down: switch to dusk or overcast days and re-boil one grade stronger; (2) leaf scorch → too strong or too much soap, dilute and respray; (3) the liquid rolls off → the soap sticker was left out; (4) germination falls after seed mixing → seed stock must never be mixed; retest that seed per 10-04; (5) insects rebound → repellence alone is not enough, stack it with drying, smoking, and sealing.

8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one batch boiled and sprayed with an insect and grain-loss comparison record; L2 = one season of granary powder plus field spraying on the control account, with seasonal loss inside the 10% red line; L3 = a local "material — strength — effect" comparison table in the ledger.

### 10.6 Cellars, Ice Cellars, and Other Storage Methods

There is no pottery at this stage — long-term liquid storage, sealed moisture-proofing, and direct-fire boiling are all impossible; this is the biggest weakness of the current food system (closed by Chapter 11).

**Transitional container lineage**:

| Container | Good for | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| Woven basket | grain, tubers | needs lining |
| Mud-lined basket | short-term storage of floury grain or seed grain | cannot hold water for long |
| Gourd | water, oil, seed | small capacity |
| Fire-bored wooden trough | dry goods, short-term water | seeps slowly; heavy |
| Hide bag | short hauls of liquid | holds water once grease-sealed |
| Cellar / ice cellar | tubers, grain, fresh produce | no liquids in the cellar |

[Card 10-03 Earth Cellar] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: a site (low water table, no seepage after rain, stable soil, away from tree roots and building foundations); tubers, grain and the like for storage; sun-dried sand (for sand burial against insects); dry grass (floor padding, replaced regularly); lid board, straw curtain, and a bundle of bamboo with the nodes knocked through (vent pipe).

2. **Tools required**: digging and hauling tools (digging stick, spade, baskets); A-frame level (Chapter 3); torch and cord (entry oxygen test); a strip of dry paper (humidity indicator); record sheet. Calibration source: cellar temperature by the "cool" hand-feel grade, humidity by the hung dry paper, oxygen deficiency by the torch — none of the three has an instrument.

3. **Sequence and control points**: choose the site → dig an opening that fits one person (about 2 chi square), 2–3 cubits deep to the cool layer → hollow it wider below into a bulging chamber with a small mouth → vault the roof and cover it with earth against seepage, dig a drainage ditch ringing the cellar, install the vent pipe (bamboo bundle) into the chamber → lid board + straw curtain at the mouth → **ventilate for half a day before entry, then lower a lit torch; if the flame weakens the air is deficient and entry is forbidden**; the person descending wears a rope at the waist with one person standing watch at the mouth. Control points: wide belly, small mouth (a small mouth seals, holds heat, and holds humidity); the torch test is a red line.

4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: 2–3 cubits deep to the cool layer (ice cellar 3+ cubits); mouth about 2 chi square; half a day of ventilation before entry; temperature criterion "cool in both winter and summer"; ice-cellar criterion "ice still at the bottom in midsummer", with ice-stored fresh goods extended by days to weeks (experience value, must be field-calibrated); hauling loss about 30%–50% (experience value).

5. **Observations to record**: temperature by hand-feel (winter and summer separately); humidity (whether the hung paper goes limp); odors (mustiness = turn out and air); seepage and standing water; rat signs; tuber sprouting; ventilation time and torch condition before entry.

6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = "cool" grade stable in winter and summer + no seepage and no asphyxiation incident; rework = seepage — dig drainage and air the cellar; scrap = collapse (soil unsuitable, choose another site).

7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) cellar too warm → thicken the earth cover, tighten the seal; (2) seepage → ring drainage ditch, air the cellar before reuse; (3) tubers sprout early → temperature too high or a shallow-dormancy variety, switch to sun-dried sand burial; (4) rats → rat guards plus a rigid lid at the mouth; (5) dizziness on entry or a weakening torch → leave at once and ventilate half a day, never force entry.

8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one cellar built and one batch stored safely under the torch test and waist-rope protocol; L2 = "cool" grade stable in winter and summer with no seepage and no asphyxiation incident; L3 = the ice-cellar midsummer ice and fresh-goods extension account on file, with two consecutive seasons of temperature records.

### 10.7 Animal Power and Simple Transport Systems

**Domestication candidate criteria (prefer animals meeting all five at once)**: herd-living with a hierarchy (control the leader and the whole herd is controllable); broad diet that does not compete with human rations (ruminant grazers first); can breed in captivity; temperament that can be tamed (handling from youth builds obedience); body size matched to the use. Goats/sheep, cattle, and donkeys are the general candidates; pigs fatten fast but compete for grain; horses are costly and come later. If domesticated dog packs exist in the surroundings, use them first for herding and guarding (legacy status, not counted in the manufacturing chain).

**Draft use from scratch — pack first, pull later**:
- Pack frame: side pannier racks + breast strap + belly band + soft padding (against rub sores). **Harness Iron Law: pull force must land on the chest-shoulders and the haunches; throat-strangling (harness) collar pulling is forbidden** (suffocation and permanent injury);
- Load: pack load ≤1/5–1/4 of body weight (experience value); after half a day's travel, unload and water without fail; first packing starts at 1/10 body weight and increases day by day — **lameness means stop**;
- Pulling: first the drag sled (double shaft poles), then the two-wheeled solid-wood wheel cart (wooden pin axle + animal-fat lubrication). Loads rise 3–5× over human back-carry (experience value). Any pack gear or vehicle is validated at 1.5× static load per Section 7.4 before use.

**Feeding and hygiene**: pens downwind of the dwellings (Section 13.8 layout); stock overwinter hay at "about 2%–3% of body weight per animal per day × days of winter"; in grass-poor years do not expand the herd; water changed twice daily; dung cleared the same day for compost and kiln fuel (manure mixed with straw is a standard fuel in Chapters 11 and 12).

**Animal-power threshing**: spread the ears on a rammed-earth floor and drive the herd to tread in circles for half a day — saves labor over the flail.

### 10.8 How a Stable Food Surplus Forms

**When agriculture beats foraging — decision criteria**:

| Dimension | Foraging wins | Agriculture wins |
|---|---|---|
| Energy account | foraging radius < half a day's travel | radius > half a day's travel, or wild resources contested by multiple groups |
| Risk account | wild resources seasonally stable, no memory of failed harvests | dry/smoke/ferment/cellar storage mastered; good years can be banked against famine years |
| Labor account | everyone foraging daily just barely feeds everyone | non-farming trades have appeared that need fixed labor |

**Trigger rule: any two of the three holding = commit to opening fields.** Before that, farm tools and compost go full tilt into soil building; no large fields are opened.

**Dual-track transition**: foraging + small fields in parallel for at least three seasons; field expansion no more than doubling per year. First-season failed-harvest data is booked as usual (failure data costs the same).

**Reserve safety line (quantified targets)**:
- Basis: hulled-grain equivalent, about 0.5–0.7 kg per person per day (experience value, must be field-calibrated);
- Target line: 45 days in year one; steady state 90–120 days (carry over to the next season's harvest);
- Structure: drawdown in layers — the eating layer (current month), the untouched layer (safety line, drawn only in famine years), **the seed layer (sealed separately, not to be touched under any circumstances)**;
- **Nutritional red line**: grain equivalents secure only calories. A long-run single-grain diet brings **scurvy (no fresh fruit and vegetables) and beriberi-type disease (a single refined grain)** — the reserve system must include dried fruit-vegetables, legumes, and sour pickles as standing categories (symptom recognition and treatment in Chapter 48; the system is established here).

**Losses and accounts**: a monthly stocktake once; mold, insects, rats, and seepage logged separately; seasonal loss >10% triggers an investigation. Ledger fields: item name, intake date, moisture spot check, drawdown record, loss category (this is where the grain page of the Chapter 38 materials account comes from).

**Food surplus → division of labor**: only grain to spare can pin people to their workstations. The Chapter 9 assembly line and the workshops of Chapters 11–12 all take this chapter's reserve safety line as their labor precondition; the sickles, rope, and carts the workshops turn out in turn raise agricultural efficiency — this part's first positive loop. Administratively: agricultural surplus is booked as exchanges against labor-hours (Chapter 37 labor account).

## Safety Boundaries

- **Edibility-test discipline outranks hunger**: one person, one item, one time window; mushrooms are banned from testing as a whole class; no other new food on test days.
- No anaerobic meat storage (botulism risk cannot be detected); moldy grain discarded whole-batch; no carrion, dead fish, or shellfish from discolored sea areas.
- Cellar entry: ventilate first + torch oxygen check, rope on descent and a watcher topside; the smoking watch executes the four Iron Rules of 6.8.
- Children keep away from animals being tamed; no throat-strangling (harness); lameness means unload.
- The granary keeps fire-separation distances from fire sources; the seed layer is sealed separately and untouchable.
- Insecticide decoction and spraying: keep out of the eyes (bitter-acrid irritants); pick sprayed leaves only after rain has rinsed them.
