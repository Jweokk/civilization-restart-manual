### 11.1 Clay Selection and Purification

Three requirements for pottery clay: **fine** (you can add coarse material to it, never the reverse), **medium or better plasticity** (8.1 bar test), **soluble salts as low as possible**.

**Soluble salts: identification and hazard**: salt-rich clay grows white frost on its surface after firing, absorbs moisture and powders, and a water-storage urn is ruined within months. Identification: white salt bloom on the dried crack faces of source clay lumps; or take one part soil plus three parts water, stir and let settle, then drip a drop of the supernatant onto a clean stone slab and sun-dry it — a conspicuous white crust means salt is high (a comparative criterion, not quantitative). Treatment: spread the soil and let rain leach it for several weeks (natural leaching), or run the panning process below with several extra decantings — the salt dissolves into the water and leaves with it.

**Panning–settling method (water winnowing)** — the core purification process of this chapter:

1. Sun-dry the clay lumps and break them with a wooden mallet (remove stones, roots, and grass debris).
2. In a large wooden trough (or a pit lined with hide), add soil:water = 1:3–4 (measured by volume bucket), stir into a uniform slurry, and let stand.
3. Graded settling schedule: coarse sand settles in 30 s–2 min; silt in several minutes to half an hour; clay particles stay suspended for hours. After standing 2–5 min, **while the sand has settled but the clay has not**, pour the upper suspension through a straw sieve (screening out leaves and insects) into a second trough — this is the first separation.
4. Let the settling trough stand 12–24 h; layers form in the vat: gently decant the clear water above, and keep the dense slurry below for concentration.
5. Concentration: load the thick slurry into straw or hide bags, hang to drain for half a day, then spread and air-dry to the plastic-range hand feel (8.2: forms a ball without sticking, like an earlobe). Never sun-bake it into a crust — a crust locks in uneven moisture, the root cause of uneven wedging.
6. For finer material (thin-walled vessels, refractory facing layer): repeat steps 2–5 on the thick slurry for a second panning.

Yield rule of thumb: one batch of raw soil yields fine clay of roughly thirty to forty percent by volume (empirical value, must be field-calibrated). Closed by-product loop: the coarse sand settled at the bottom of the first trough, washed clean and sun-dried, is the standard sand temper of 11.2.

[Card 11-03 Levigation and Settling Classification] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: raw clay from a known pit (checked against the three 11.1 requirements: fine, plastic at least medium, low soluble salts); water at **1:3–4 by volume**. Do not expect levigation to save a salty clay — the salts sit in the pore structure and washing will not remove them. Change pits.
2. **Tools required**: wooden trough or clay vat for settling, stirring paddle, fine sieve (removes coarse sand and root), graded containers with labels. Calibration source: grading rests on **settling time**, not on judging fineness by eye.
3. **Sequence and control points**: soak the raw clay → stir to slurry → sieve out the coarse → settle and draw off in stages: what drops in **30 s to 2 min** is coarse sand (discard or keep as temper), what takes **hours** is the clay fraction (this is the potting body) → settle each fraction → pour off clear water → dry in shade to the plastic range (18–28% water) → wedge. Control point: **take only the middle fraction** — the top is too fine and shrinks hard, the bottom is too coarse to throw a coil.
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: soil:water 1:3–4 by volume; coarse sand settles in 30 s–2 min, clay fraction in hours; yield **30–40% by volume of fine clay** (empirical, varies by pit — measure once per pit).
5. **Observations to record**: per batch — pit number / soil-water ratio / coarse layer thickness / yield / days to shade-dry / feel after wedging (result of the three coil tests).
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = yield in the 30–40% band and all three coil tests passed; rework = low yield (settling times misjudged, too much coarse fraction taken), redo; scrap = white bloom after firing (high soluble salts — not a levigation problem, change pits).
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) sticky clay that cracks badly → the too-fine top fraction was taken, switch to the middle; (2) will not form a coil → coarse fraction taken, or not wedged through; (3) very low yield → the raw clay is too sandy; use it as a temper source, not a body; (4) white bloom on fired ware → soluble salts, back to 11.1 for a new pit.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one batch with yield and test records; L2 = three batches from the same pit with comparable yield and shrinkage; L3 = a second person levigates from this card alone, yield within 5 percentage points.

### 11.2 Particle Size, Water Content, and Forming Behavior

**Particle size–property relationship**: clay that is too fine shrinks heavily, has a strong dry-cracking tendency, and is sticky when wet; clay that is too coarse cannot be rolled into coils, leaves a rough surface, and has low fired strength. Engineering practice does not chase "the finest"; it chases "gradation + controllable temper" — the point of purification is to obtain a stable starting point first, then use temper to tune properties to target.

**Temper system (core parameters of this section)**: all ratios below are **volume ratios, relative to dry clay volume**, and all are marked as **empirical starting values, to be field-calibrated via the 8.6 gradient tests** (0/10/20/30, four groups, n≥3 per group):

| Temper | Grain size / treatment | Starting blend ratio (volume) | Main function | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Washed quartz sand | 0.5–1 mm | 10%–20% | Reduces shrinkage; general strengthening | A single size fraction suffices; mixed coarse and fine is better |
| Mussel shell / eggshell powder | Crushed and passed through a coarse sieve | 10%–20% | Good thermal shock resistance; first choice for cookware | **Must first be calcined through, then slaked with water and used dry** (prevents residual quicklime in the green body from expanding and cracking when it meets water — same risk family as the lime of Chapter 13) |
| Crushed pottery (grog) | Old pottery pounded up, in coarse and fine grades | 15%–30% | Low shrinkage, refire-resistant; mainstay for refractory material | Activated from this chapter onward (at 8.2 there was no pottery yet) |
| Rice husk | Sieve out the fines | 5%–15% | Light and insulating; cookware base material | Leaves micropores after firing, lowering density; not used for water-storage urns |

Logic of use: thin-walled small pieces lean fine with low temper; large thick-walled pieces lean coarse with high temper; cookware takes shell powder or rice husk; water-storage urns take sand for density. **Each time you switch clay sources, re-calibrate the entire gradient** — empirical values must not be transplanted across sources.

**Water content (in-field determination of the plastic range, ◎ three hand-feel tests)**: all forming happens in the plastic range (about 18%–28%, empirical value, must be field-calibrated). **Coil building uses clay on the stiff side of the range; roll a test coil and run the three tests — proceed to building only when all three pass**:
1. **Bent-ring test**: bend a clay coil (thumb-thick) around four fingers into a ring — no break and no necking-down = pass; snaps on bending = too dry, rework with slurry;
2. **Palm-stick test**: press and lift the coil quickly with the palm — lifts without sticking (or only the faintest trace) = pass; sticks and pulls clay onto the palm = too wet, let it dry;
3. **Fuzz test**: fold the coil over and twist it once — the surface does not fuzz or split = pass; fuzzing = too dry or poor plasticity (wedge more or change material).

Misjudgment compensation: for each batch, first roll a coil and weigh it, air-dry half a day, and weigh again — if weight loss ≤2% and the three tests still pass, that batch becomes the "standard water-content grade"; record this grade's weight in the ledger, and afterward proportion water by weight (not by feel memory).

**Wedging and aging**: spiral knead-press 10–15 min until the cross-section shows no air pores and no dry or wet lumps; when conditions allow, seal in damp cloth and age 3–7 days, then knead once more — **aging must not replace wedging for expelling air**.

[Card 11-02 The Three Coil Tests and Standard Water Bands] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: wedged clay (levigated body plus temper, with temper tried at 0/10/20/30% steps); water. Every change of pit or body means retesting — the previous pit's band does not carry over.
2. **Tools required**: flat glass or stone slab (for rolling coils), scored rule, balance (optional, for the shrinkage test). Calibration source: all three tests are hand actions, so **each criterion is written as a measurable form** (length, bend diameter, crack count).
3. **Sequence and control points**: (1) **lick test** — touch the tip of the tongue, spit out and rinse at once — judges sand and salt; (2) **coil test** — roll a 6 mm coil to 15–20 cm without breaking; roll a 3 mm coil and bend it into a ring without breaking for high plasticity; (3) **shrinkage test** — a 4 cm ball dried in shade for 24 h, then check for cracks. Control points: **the lick test is spit, never swallow**; **the shrinkage test needs the full 24 h** (reading early confuses "not yet dry" with "will not crack").
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: plastic range **18–28% water**; 6 mm coil to 15–20 cm unbroken; 3 mm coil bent to a ring unbroken = high plasticity; 4 cm ball, 24 h; total shrinkage **8–15%**, and **a batch-to-batch spread over 2% means the process needs checking** — either the body or the water content.
5. **Observations to record**: per batch — pit / temper type and proportion / the three test results (coil length, ring success, crack count) / water feel / total shrinkage.
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = all three tests passed and shrinkage within 8–15%; rework = coil breaks (blend in a more plastic clay or change pit), ball cracks (reduce water or add 10–20% temper); scrap = deep cracking persists after tempering (that pit is a temper source only).
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) coil breaks immediately → water low or sand high; add water and retest, then adjust temper; (2) sticky and unformable → water high; spread out and dry to the plastic range; (3) dries into a crack network → shrinkage high; temper with quartz sand 0.5–1 mm at 10–20%, or crushed shell at 10–20%; (4) uneven color after firing → not a test problem; check levigation and whether pits were mixed.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one batch of three tests; L2 = three pits, one batch each, with shrinkage and temper comparable; L3 = a second person judges the same batch from this card alone, reaching the same conclusion.

### 11.3 Hand Forming and Drying

**First-article specification** (set the target low, raise the success rate): straight-cylinder pot, capacity 2–4 L, mouth diameter 15–25 cm, height 15–25 cm, wall thickness 0.8–1.2 cm. A straight cylinder is chosen because it has no shoulder or neck, can be coil-built, and can be cooked in — the first pot's mission is "fires through and is usable," not beauty.

**Coil-building procedure**:

1. Make the base: cut a circle from a clay slab (scribe the circle around a fixed stake, 3.2), about 1.2 cm thick, and set it on a sand-dusted backing board (the sand lets the green body slide freely as it shrinks, preventing tear cracks).
2. Roll coils: coil diameter 2–3 cm; length whatever one hand can handle.
3. Build: ring upon ring, **stagger the joints between layers**; score and slip each mating surface first, then press and seal with the fingers; smooth and press inside and outside simultaneously.
4. Pause and rest the ware: after every 3–4 rings, stop for half an hour to an hour, letting the lower body build load-bearing strength before continuing — otherwise the belly bulges outward and collapses.
5. Paddle-and-anvil: at half-dry (before leather-hard), brace a pebble against the inside with one hand and beat with a wooden paddle outside, working around the vessel ring by ring to compact and shape — the paddling also raises density, directly tied to fired strength and seepage.
6. Burnish: smooth and burnish the inner and outer surfaces with wet hands or a wet stone (reduces porosity; the key step for water-storage urns).
7. Trim: at leather-hard, scrape the base flat and trim the rim.

**Drying (the crack-prevention critical process)** — overall principle: **let moisture leave the whole vessel evenly and slowly**.

1. Shade drying: indoors or under tree shade, with the body covered in damp grass or large leaves. Fully covered for the first two days, then half-covered day by day; 3–7 days total (depending on wall thickness and weather; empirical value, must be field-calibrated).
2. Prohibition list: direct sun (surface dries first while the interior is wet → cracks), drafty spots (same), fire-baking (same and worse), stacking pressure (deformation).
3. Large pieces: bag in straw for slow drying; if cracks appear at the rim → the rim dried ahead of the body; cover with damp cloth over the rim only.
4. Bone-dry criteria (all four at once): color turns lighter, no cool feeling against the lip, weight unchanged for two consecutive days, knuckle tap sounds dull yet solid.

Dry green ware has limited strength; carry it with both hands under the base. Before kiln loading, give every piece a visual inspection: cracks, delamination, exposed pores — cracked pieces need not be fired; record them and return them to the wedging trough.

### 11.4 How to Reduce Shrinkage Cracking

**The two sources of shrinkage**: drying shrinkage (water leaves, particles draw together, commonly 5%–10%) + firing shrinkage (sintering densification, another 5%–10%); total shrinkage commonly 8%–15% (all empirical values, must be field-calibrated). When laying out, green-body size = target fired size × 1.1–1.2, and every batch of clay is measured by the scribe-line method (8.2: 10 cm standard line) — never from memory.

**Master table of the seven crack types (valid before and after the kiln, ordered by probability — when cracking appears, match the type first, then act)**:

| No. | Crack type | Stage | Signature | Action |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Uneven-drying crack | Shade drying | Fine long crack starting from rim/thin spots | Cover and dry slowly; redo |
| 2 | Thickness-step crack | Shade drying / firing | Crack runs in a straight line along the thick-thin boundary | Thickness ratio ≤2:1; transition band ≥2 finger-widths |
| 3 | Air-bubble blister crack | Firing | Blisters, spalling, pores at the fracture face | Return to the wedging trough and re-wedge to expel air |
| 4 | Temper blowout | Firing | Angular breccia-like burst openings (unslaked shell) | Redo the temper calcination-slaking regime |
| 5 | Board-pull crack | Drying | Radial cracks on the base | Always sand the backing board |
| 6 | Salt-bloom spall | Weeks after firing | Scaling skin beneath white surface frost | Pan out the salt; two extra decantings |
| 7 | Dunting | Firing / cooling | Crack faces neat as a knife cut (the 573 °C zone, or opening the kiln too fast) | Rise and cool slowly through that zone; shut the kiln with a gap left open for slow cooling |

(Underfired seepage and overfired deformation do not count as "cracking"; see the 11.6 failure-mode table — **identification order: first separate cracked from uncracked, then assign the stage, and only then match the type**.)

**Each batch's shrinkage rate is the basis for laying out**: the same source with the same mix should give a stable shrinkage rate — if the scribe-line shrinkage rate differs by more than 2% between two batches (empirical criterion), raw material or water content is out of control; check the process before opening the kiln.

**Cookware base special item**: thicken the flat base, where fire strikes hardest, to 1.5× wall thickness, and blend in shell powder or rice husk to raise thermal shock resistance — base blowout is the number-one cause of cooking pots being scrapped.

### 11.5 From Open-Air Firing to Simple Pottery Kilns

A three-tier route; each tier fixes a specific defect of the tier before it:

**Tier 1: bonfire firing** — the learning tier. Lay dry grass and fine brush as a bed (about one palm thick), set the ware in the middle, pile soft fuel around and above it, and seal the outside with a smeared clay coating, leaving 3–4 finger-thick air vents. Light and burn 1–2 h. Temperature is 400–700 °C, uncontrollable and strongly wind-affected; yield 30%–60% (empirical value, must be field-calibrated). The value is not output but low-cost observation of "what happens to clay in a fire."

**Tier 2: pit kiln** — the first passing pot fires here. Dig an earthen pit 40–60 cm deep and 60–100 cm across; first burn a charcoal bed at the pit bottom, set the ware above the fuel layer (ceramic shards or stones between ware and fire), cover the ware with firewood, and cap with stone slabs or sod, leaving gaps. Heating is more even than bonfire firing and heat retention is good; reaches 700–900 °C; yield 60%–80% (empirical value, must be field-calibrated).

**Tier 3: shaft kiln (updraft vertical pit kiln)** — the starting point of stable production and the forerunner of the Chapter 12 furnace. Dug down from the surface (or into a slope): a vertical combustion chamber (chamber diameter 60–80 cm, height 80–120 cm) plus a fire port dug in obliquely from the lower side (the stoke hole). Inside the chamber, build tiered racks from clay coils; load the ware on the racks by layer; feed fuel continuously at the fire port; hot gases rise through the chamber from bottom to top and exit the top opening. Reaches 800–1000 °C and above; yield and output are stable.

**Physical firing stages and the temperature ramp (the basis for reading a firing curve; the stoking rhythm uses smoke color as its metronome)**:

| Temperature zone | Process | Visual criterion | **Stoking rhythm (empirical starting point)** | Operational meaning |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Room temp–150 °C | Residual water driven off | Heavy white steam | **No fuel while the white steam persists** — check the steam once per interval (water clock); add fuel only when the steam weakens | Rise slowly; guard against steam explosions |
| 150–500 °C | Combined water and organic matter burn out | Smoke turns white → yellow-brown | Add a handful as soon as the smoke changes color; set the beat by smoke color | May speed up gradually |
| 500–600 °C | Quartz inversion (about 573 °C) | No obvious visual change | **Skip one interval without fuel** (climb over the inversion point on stored heat) | Rise and cool slowly in this zone; going fast means dunting |
| 700–900 °C | Carbonate decomposition; vitrification begins | Smoke turns pale blue; fire color bright red and above | Steady frequent small handfuls | At temperature, soak |
| 900–1000 °C+ | Densification; strength forms | Fire color orange-yellow | Sprinkle charcoal in handful by handful to push temperature | The shaft-kiln + good-fuel zone |

**Preheating and moisture purge**: the night before loading, ring the dry ware around a fire one arm's length out and bake it overnight — the ware enters the kiln warm, and the first ramp stage can proceed unhurried.

[Card 11-01 Firing Curve and Stoking Rhythm] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: dry ware (pre-warmed and dried overnight — ringed around a fire at arm's length so it enters the kiln warm); graded fuel (grass-dung, soft wood, hard wood, charcoal); record sheet.
2. **Tools required**: water clock (Chapter 3, as the beat timer), firing hook, peep hole. Calibration source: **the metronome is smoke color, not mood** — read the smoke once per beat, and let it decide whether fuel goes on.
3. **Sequence and control points (five temperature stages, checked one by one)**:
   - **Ambient–150 °C** (residual water driven off, heavy white steam): **no fuel until the steam clears**; slow rise to prevent steam spalling;
   - **150–500 °C** (bound water and organics burn off, smoke turns from white to yellow-brown): add a bundle each time the color shifts; rhythm follows smoke, may speed up;
   - **500–600 °C** (quartz inversion near 573 °C, no visible change): **skip one beat — add no fuel**, coast across the inversion on stored heat; slow both up and down, speeding through here causes dunting;
   - **700–900 °C** (carbonates decompose, vitrification begins; smoke pale blue, fire bright red or above): small frequent bundles, hold at temperature;
   - **900–1000 °C+** (densification, strength develops; fire orange-yellow): scatter charcoal in small lots to push the temperature.
   Control points: **skipping one beat at the quartz stage is a hard rule**; every beat records smoke color and bundle count.
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: the smoke sequence white → yellow-brown → pale blue is the primary criterion; the 573 °C stage is compensated by "skip one beat" in place of a pyrometer; fuel grade decides whether a stage can be climbed at all (if not, switch to hard wood or charcoal).
5. **Observations to record**: per beat — time / smoke color / bundles added / fire color / test-piece change (the 6.7 sample regime).
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = complete smoke sequence and two successful firings whose records match; rework = underfired pieces go back for refiring; scrap = dunting (match against the seven crack types in 11.7 — usually the quartz stage was climbed too fast).
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) stalls at a stage → fuel grade too low, switch to hard wood or charcoal; (2) smoke sequence out of order → kiln overloaded, flue blocked (Card 11-06); (3) dunting on opening → no skipped beat at 573 °C, or cooling too fast; (4) surface underfired, interior overfired → wrong loading position, the flame path did not reach.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one complete beat-by-beat record; L2 = two successful firing records that match; L3 = a second person runs a firing from this card alone with a yield no lower than the lead's.

### 11.6 Fuel, Airflow, and Firing Temperature

**Fuel grading table**: dry grass and animal dung — light fast, low heat value; kindling and bed fuel only. Softwood — the mainstay of the ramp stage. Hardwood — the mainstay of the soak stage. Charcoal (product of the Chapter 6 earth-mound kiln) — reinforcement in the push-to-temperature stage, sprinkled in handful by handful when the shaft kiln attacks 800 °C and above. Fuel consumption rule of thumb: one pit-kiln firing needs roughly 10–20× the total green-ware volume in firewood (empirical value, must be field-calibrated) — the real cost of pottery is in the wood, not the clay; site selection must lean on fuelwood stands (verify this item against the Chapter 2 resource map).

**Kiln loading**: ware spacing ≥2 finger-widths (leaves flame channels); large pieces on the bottom load-bearing layer, small pieces above; separate layers with clay coils or old ceramic shards — dust the separators with sand against sticking. Loading density directly changes airflow: packed too tight, the fire cannot travel and firing is incomplete; too loose wastes fuel. Record the piece count and a layout diagram for every kiln.

**Airflow**: reuse the 6.7 principle of "low in, high out": keep the pit-kiln lid gap at a high position; for the shaft kiln, fire port low and facing the wind, top port exhausting; total smoke-port area ≈ 1.5–2× total vent area (empirical value, must be field-calibrated); with too much back pressure the kiln temperature will not climb.

**Firing record (mandatory for every kiln)**: stoking intervals and counts, smoke-color sequence (white → yellow-brown → pale blue; 6.6 criteria), fire-color stage (auxiliary criterion), test tile condition (6.7 standard test tile), loading layout, yield and defect classification. The records of two successful kiln runs should match — if they do not, the process is out of control and you are riding on luck.

**Cooling**: cool naturally until the next day, until it can be touched by hand, then open the kiln. **Water quenching is forbidden**; opening the kiln above 600 °C into a cold wind = mass dunting losses. The opener inspects with a long pole, guarding against rack collapse and scalds.

**Failure-mode cross-reference table**:

| Symptom | Diagnosis | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Dull sound, earthy red color, seeps water | Underfired (insufficient temperature/soak) | Can be refired |
| Surface blisters, deformation, pieces fused together | Overfired | Cut fuel, cut draft, shorten the soak |
| Cracks neat as a knife cut | Dunting (fast cooling / rushed quartz zone) | Cool slowly; check crack depth before refiring |
| Black heart, black core (black cross-section) | Poor exhaust; organic matter smoldering | Improve the flue; extend the oxidation stage |

[Card 11-05 The Three-Part Sintering Check] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: ware from the opened kiln; test pieces from the same firing (left by the 6.7 sample regime); water for the absorption test.
2. **Tools required**: balance (Chapter 3, for dry and saturated weights), water basin, small hammer, magnifier or a plain look at the fracture. Calibration source: absorption is the only figure you can calculate; ring and fracture are supporting evidence — all three must be read together.
3. **Sequence and control points**: (1) **absorption** — weigh dry, saturate, weigh again, compute percent; (2) **ring** — a clear, long note is mature, a dull thud is underfired; (3) **fracture** — fine and dense, no laminations, no black core. Control point: all three must be read on the same piece from the same firing — **never compare this kiln's ring against that kiln's absorption**.
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: absorption bands — **dense <5%, general ware <10%, tile <12%** (a grading criterion, not a "lower is better" number; it depends on use); total shrinkage 8–15%, a batch spread over 2% means the process needs checking.
5. **Observations to record**: per firing — test-piece absorption / ring grade / fracture color / crack count and location / comparison with the previous firing.
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = absorption in the band for the intended use, clear ring, dense fracture; rework = high absorption with a dull ring (underfired), refire; scrap = laminations or black core (heating too fast or organics unburned) — the whole firing is downgraded.
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) wide spread across one kiln → uneven flame path, see Card 11-06; (2) uniformly underfired → insufficient push at the top or low-grade fuel, back to the fifth stage of 11-01; (3) uniformly overfired and deformed → held too long at temperature or fire too fierce; (4) absorption passes but it seeps in use → unglazed pottery absorbs by nature (11.9); not a sintering fault.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = three checks recorded for one firing; L2 = three firings comparable and the grade stable; L3 = a second person judges the same firing from this card alone, agreeing on the grade.

### 11.7 Pottery Strength, Water Absorption, and Heat Resistance

**The three-part sintering criterion (the core acceptance for qualified pottery; all three mandatory)**:

1. **Strike**: flick with a finger or tap the rim lightly with a stick — a crisp "ting" means fired through; a dull "thud" means underfired (comparison demo: keep one deliberately underfired reference piece).
2. **No seepage**: fill with water and let stand 24 h — no damp beads on the outer wall, no obvious drop in water level; quantitative method: weigh on a balance before and after; 24 h weight loss <2% (mass; empirical value, must be field-calibrated).
3. **No cracking on refire**: pull 1 piece from a passing batch and refire it along with the next kiln load; it comes out uncracked and unspalled — proof that sintering is complete with no latent defect (an underfired body can slip through the first firing; it cracks on the refire).

**Water absorption, quantitative (grading index)**: dry weight m1 (weighed bone-dry) → soak in water 24 h → wipe off surface water and weigh m2; absorption = (m2−m1)/m1. Targets: cookware and water-storage urns <5%, general storage vessels <10% (empirical values, must be field-calibrated). Pottery over the absorption limit is downgraded to dry-goods containers.

**Thermal shock (cookware-specific acceptance)**: fill the pot to 80% with water, bury it at the edge of the charcoal bed, heat over a gentle fire to boiling and hold 10 min; no cracking = a qualified cooking pot. **Cold-water quench tests on passing product are forbidden**: quench tests are done only on scrapped pieces, as demonstration; shards from bursting inferior ware can injure people.

**Graded use table**:

| Grade | Criteria | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Dense grade | All three parts pass + absorption <5% + thermal shock pass | Cooking, water storage |
| Standard grade | Three parts pass + absorption <10% | Grain storage, goods storage |
| Downgraded | Strike passes but seeps | Dry-goods container (hide lining against moisture) |

Chapter 10's three targets are thereby delivered: water urn (dense grade + lid), grain urn (standard grade + lid + sealing groove at the rim), cooking pot (dense grade + thermal shock + shell/husk base). **The advent of pottery also feeds back into every capacity-limited process of Chapter 8**: lye can be boiled down concentrated, hide glue properly rendered, oils stored — the vessel bottleneck is lifted; record it in the ledger.

**Repair boundary for broken ware**: with no metal staples, adhesive repair (8.5) is only for dry-goods containers; never repair heat-used pieces.

### 11.8 From Vessels to Brick, Tile, Pipe, and Refractory Material

**Brick**: in a wooden mold (brick mold made by Chapter 9 woodworking; internal 30×15×8 cm, empirical value), dust the inner walls with sand; ram in plastic clay — strike off level — demold — shade-dry (same 11.3 slow-drying regime) — load into the kiln and fire. Fired brick consumes much fuel, so **use it only for kiln linings, fire ports, chimneys, and high-humidity spots**; ordinary building walls use sun-dried adobe (Chapter 13) — do not confuse the two divisions of labor. Fired-brick acceptance: crisp strike, absorption <10%, two bricks struck together shed no grit.

**Tile**: form a clay sheet over a curved mold (a half-round wooden mold), shade-dry and fire; flat tiles overlap ≥ one third of their length. Tile absorption allowance is relaxed to <12%.

**Ceramic pipe**: wrap a clay sheet around a round wooden rod into a tube, press-seal the seam, shade-dry and fire; sections 30–50 cm long, one end slightly necked into a spigot. Joints are sealed on site with oakum + clay or mortar (Chapter 13). Uses: kiln air ducts (Chapter 12), water conveyance (13.6).

**Refractory material special topic — this chapter's most important boundary statement**:

> **Not everything that has been fired is refractory.** Ordinary pottery clay carries high fluxing impurities — potassium, sodium, iron, calcium — and begins to soften and deform at 800–1000 °C; build ordinary pottery brick into a high-temperature furnace as lining and, above 1100 °C, it will melt, slump, drip slag, and contaminate the charge. Refractory or not is decided by raw material and formulation, not by "whether it has been fired."

**Three standards for refractory material**:

1. Refractoriness: at the highest attainable temperature (shaft kiln + forced draft, about 1100–1200 °C, empirical value), the test piece does not melt, slump, or badly deform.
2. Thermal stability: bury in a red-hot charcoal bed, withdraw and air-cool; three cycles without crumbling or cracking.
3. Slag resistance: for now, the initial judgment is "does not bond severely with wood-ash glaze slag" (the formal slag standard awaits Chapter 18 furnace-slag experience).

**Raw material sourcing**: seek "lean" clay — whitish, gray-white, or pale in appearance, obviously gritty, lowish plasticity (a marginal pass on the three-test bar is enough to collect it). Kaolin (the 8.4 white entry) does not fizz in acid, which distinguishes it from chalk — the first choice; register it on the resource map. Ordinary red-brown pottery clay is not refractory material.

**Grog system (the core of the refractory formulation)**: fire plastic refractory clay through first (above 900 °C) → pound and sieve into two grades: coarse grog 1–2 mm, fine grog <0.5 mm → proportion by volume: **grog 50%–70% + plastic refractory clay 30%–50% (empirical starting values, must be field-calibrated)**. Principle: grog has already fired, its shrinkage is set; it serves as the skeleton; raw clay is only the binder. The higher the grog ratio, the smaller the firing shrinkage and the better the refire stability and thermal shock — but forming gets harder. Form refractory brick by hand ramming; do not seek plasticity.

**Inspection**: make 12×3×3 cm test columns, stand them upright in the hottest position in the kiln, and after firing measure: lean/bend <5% of column length, no bonding to the bed material, cross-section dense with no lamination. Passing formulations are registered as the local refractory base mix, for Chapter 12 furnace construction.

First applications: shaft-kiln fire-port brick and furnace-lining precast blocks — all of the next chapter's high-temperature engineering starts from these few bricks.

### 11.9 Glazes: Formulation, Melting, and Safety (New in v2)

**What a glaze is**: a thin layer of glass deliberately melted onto the pottery surface — it seals pores to cut absorption and cleans easily. **Unglazed pottery is always absorbent** (burnishing reduces but never eliminates it); glaze takes the storage urn's 24 h water loss down another grade.

**Local glaze route (wood-ash glaze, the only self-sustaining route)**:

| Formulation (parts by volume; empirical starting point; calibrate by gradient) | Composition | Melting behavior |
|---|---|---|
| Ash 1 : refractory clay 2–3 | Ash as flux; clay as skeleton and binder | Vitrifies at about 1100–1200 °C (more ash lowers the melting point and the glaze runs easily) |
| Ash 1 : clay 1 ("rich glaze") | Flux ample | Melts at about 1000–1100 °C, but high risk of the glaze running and pooling on the base |

**Test regime (mandatory before production)**: mix the same formulation at three steps (ash:clay 1:1 / 1:2 / 1:3), brush onto the upper half of test tiles and load into the kiln (**paint only half — the other half serves as the control for judging the glaze-body bond**); grade after firing:
- Qualified glaze surface: bright and continuous, no running or heaping, glaze does not flake when struck (glaze and body shrinkage matched);
- Glaze cracks in a tortoise-shell pattern = glaze shrinkage greater than the body — add clay, cut ash;
- Glaze retracts into beads = the glaze does not wet the body — too much ash or a dusty body surface; retry.

**Ash sources and selection**: straw ash and wood ash differ in composition (high-calcium vs. high-potassium ash differences move the melting point directly) — **re-calibrate the gradient every time the ash source changes**; the ash must pass a fine sieve and be made into glaze slurry (yogurt-thickness class, ◎: dipped with a brush it hangs in an unbroken thread).

**Lead-glaze red line**: lead makes beautiful low-temperature glazes, but **lead-glazed vessels holding food = chronic poisoning** (excluded by the General Constitution and the Chapter 8 red line) — lead glaze is permitted only on non-foodware with explicit marking; this route defaults to not making it.

**Glaze limitations, honestly registered**: a glaze surface that has cracked (later-stage crazing) harbors grime — the water-storage urn's baseline is "plain dense surface + burnishing," with glaze as an upgrade item.

[Card 11-04 Glaze Gradient Test Tiles] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: wood ash (same family as the Chapter 8 lye ash), clay or a feldspar substitute, water; bisque-fired test tiles (unglazed, same body, same batch). **Unglazed pottery always absorbs water** — burnishing only reduces it; glaze is not decoration, it is pore sealing.

3. **Sequence and control points**: mix ash and clay in gradients (**1:1, 1:2, 1:3** to start) → thin to a slurry → coat tiles to the same thickness → fire together → read the melt. Once the gradient fixes the local optimum, record it in this chapter.

8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one round of three gradient tiles; L2 = the local optimum fixed and reproduced in one more round; L3 = a second person reproduces it from this card alone, both reading the same tile's melt identically.

### 11.10 Detailed Kiln-Loading Procedure (New in v2)

Section 11.6 gave only one floor rule, "ware spacing ≥2 finger-widths"; to push yield from "it fires" to "a stable 70% and above," kiln loading must have a procedure that can be drawn, checked, and reproduced. The essence of loading is **building roads for the flame**: fire enters at the fire port, travels along the channels you left past every piece, and leaves through the top opening — wherever a channel is blocked, that spot is underfired.

**Flame-channel design (text version of the loading layout diagram)**:

- **Four levels of fire path**: main fire channel (directly facing the fire port, 2 palms wide, straight to the kiln center; no ware may occupy it) → branch fire paths (radiating from the main channel to the sides and upward, 1 palm wide) → ware-to-ware gaps (≥2 finger-widths, the 11.6 floor) → top smoke zone (≥1 palm between the top ware layer and the kiln ceiling; never packed solid).
- **Gap specification table**:

| Location | Minimum gap | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Ware to ware | ≥2 finger-widths | Flame-channel floor (11.6) |
| Ware to kiln wall | ≥1 palm | The wall is the main rising flue; touching it blocks the draft |
| Ware to spacer-pad contact face | One dusting of sand | Against firing fusion (if fused, opening the kiln tears the base out with it) |
| Top layer to kiln ceiling | ≥1 palm | The smoke-gathering exit; packed solid, the whole kiln's back pressure rises |

- **Spacer pads**: pads of old ceramic shards or refractory-clay trimmings, diameter 2–3 finger-widths greater than the ware base they carry, with sand on the working face. Every piece gets its own pad; two pieces must never share one — sharing means the two shrink out of sync, tip into each other, and crush-crack.

**Layering principle for large and small pieces**: large pieces (storage urns, cooking pots) go on the bottom load-bearing layer and occupy the hot positions near the fire — thick walls can take fierce fire; small pieces (bowls, plates) go above; **the standard test tile always goes in the highest-temperature position** (the reference for the 11.7 three-part judgment must represent the harshest spot in the kiln); thin-walled pieces avoid the direct blast of the fire port (localized rapid heating = dunting, seven-crack table item 7). The principle in one line: **heavy below, thin away from the fire, test tile on top**.

**Shrinkage-gap allowance (compute loading gaps from the 11.4 shrinkage rate)**: ware enters the kiln at wet size and shrinks in firing. The loading gap must be ≥ each piece's expected shrinkage, or two pieces fire fused into one. Algorithm: **loading gap ≥ the piece's maximum linear dimension × batch shrinkage rate × 1.2 (safety factor)**. Example: pot diameter 20 cm, batch shrinkage rate 12% (measured by scribe-line method, 11.4) → shrinkage 2.4 cm → loading gap ≥2.9 cm (about two and a half finger-widths). For a new clay batch with no measured shrinkage rate, always compute gaps at the 15% upper bound.

**Kiln-layout diagram archiving system**: every kiln requires two simple drawings into the kiln-run file — one top view and one cross-section, marked with: each piece's number and vessel form, layer count and layer separators, the routing of the main fire channel and branch paths, and the measured minimum loading gaps. After opening the kiln, recheck the layout diagram against defect positions: center underfiring and dunting at fire-port positions can all be traced to causes on the diagram. A layout that achieves ≥70% yield in two consecutive kilns is transcribed as the "standard kiln-loading diagram" and archived; thereafter, load each kiln from that master with minor adjustments (empirical value, must be field-calibrated).

[Card 11-06 Kiln Loading Layout] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: dry ware (pre-warmed and dried); wads and a dusting of sand (to prevent sticking); graded fuel. **Loading decides the yield** — the step from "it can be fired" to "a steady seventy percent or better" is mostly here.
2. **Tools required**: scored rule for gaps, wads, sand bag. Calibration source: gaps measured in fingers and palms; the loading gap from a formula.
3. **Sequence and control points (four-level flame path plus gap specification)**: (1) **main flue** — facing the fire mouth, **2 palms wide**, running straight to the kiln center, **no ware may occupy it**; (2) **branch flues** — radiating from the main flue to both sides and upward, **1 palm wide**; (3) **gaps** — ware to ware **≥2 fingers**, ware to wall **≥1 palm** (the wall face is the rising main flue; touching it blocks it), top zone **≥1 palm**; (4) **sand a layer on the wad contact face** (stops sticking; stuck ware tears the base off when the kiln is opened). Control points: the main flue and the wall gap — block either and the firing is lost.
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: main flue 2 palms, branches 1 palm; ware gaps ≥2 fingers, wall ≥1 palm, top ≥1 palm; **loading gap = linear dimension × batch shrinkage × 1.2** (the 1.2 is an institutional factor, not an empirical value).
5. **Observations to record**: a layout sketch per firing (text form is enough) — flame path routing / measured gaps per zone / piece count and type distribution / sanding / yield by position after opening.
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = all gaps met and grade differences between positions small; rework = a zone consistently underfired (flame path did not reach), change the layout next firing; scrap = the main flue occupied or the wall gap blocked and the firing lost (keep the layout sketch as a negative sample).
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) big inside-outside grade gap → main flue occupied or branches not radiating; (2) wall-adjacent pieces underfired → wall gap under 1 palm; (3) bases tear off on opening → wads not sanded; (4) top overfired, bottom underfired → flame path only at the base, not radiating upward.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one layout sketch plus yield; L2 = three sketches with comparable yields, steady above seventy percent; L3 = a second person loads from this card alone with a yield no lower than the lead's.

### 11.11 Pipe Fittings and Special Shapes (New in v2)

The straight pipe of 11.8 solves only "straight"; kiln air ducts and water lines need bends, branches, and larger bores. This section covers three classes of special shapes, each with its own method and acceptance; bore conventions follow the 11.8 straight pipe (sections 30–50 cm long, one end necked into a spigot).

**Ceramic pipe elbows (coil-built bends)**: two grades, 45° and 90°, with two methods.

- Method 1 (coil building): pile damp sand into a curved-face core (radius of curvature ≥1.5× pipe diameter, empirical starting point, must be field-calibrated), compact and smooth it, dust it with sand as a release layer, and coil-build on it to a wall thickness of 1–1.5 cm; at leather-hard, lift the piece from the sand pile and trim both ends into bevels. The curved face stays smooth where it should be smooth, with no arrises — an arris is a stress concentration.
- Method 2 (segmented lobster-back bend, no core): fire 3 straight sections per the 11.8 straight-pipe process, bevel-cut both ends of each section at the required angles (for the 90° three-section bend: 15° on each end of the middle section, 15° on one end of each end section; cut cardboard angle templates on site for comparison); score and slip the bevel mating faces and press them together, add an exterior corner-fillet coil (3 finger-widths wide), then shade-dry and fire the whole assembly.
- **Acceptance (both methods)**: ① clear bore — a clay ball of diameter = 0.8× the bore, dropped in one end and rolled through on a tilt, rolls out smoothly = pass (tests the narrowest point of the bend); ② the strike sound at the joints matches the pipe body (a muffled sound = a false bond; same 11.7 strike criterion); ③ filled with water for 24 h, no seep-damp on the outer wall (a simplified pipe version of the 11.7 no-seepage criterion).

**Tees (core-mold forming)**: build the main barrel per the 11.8 straight pipe; form the side opening with a core — a round wooden stick wrapped in grass rope (the rope layers let the core withdraw), stick diameter = side-port bore − 0.5 cm (leaving slip allowance; verify with calipers). Sequence: coil the main barrel to side-port height → cut the side hole while wet, score the hole rim and turn a flange → insert the core, its end butted flush against the main barrel's inner wall → coil-build a short side section around the core (length ≥1.5× pipe diameter, enough for later socketing) → add exterior corner-fillet clay at the side-port root (a curved transition, guarding against the thickness-step crack, seven-crack table item 2) → at half-dry, withdraw the core with a gentle twist; hard pulling is forbidden. **Acceptance**: roll the ball test once in each direction, main and side; the strike sound at the root fillet zone matches the body; filled with water for 24 h, no seep at the root. The side-port root is the weakest point of the whole piece; during shade drying, cover the root alone with damp cloth for slow drying (same as the 11.3 rim method).

**Large-diameter pipe (slab rolling)**: above 15 cm bore, coil building is too slow and the whole piece too heavy; switch to slab rolling: roll a clay slab to 1.2–1.5 cm thick, cut a rectangle (length = target circumference × 1.1 including overlap; width and core diameter both laid out with the batch shrinkage rate, 11.4), roll it around a rope-wrapped wooden core into a tube, lap the longitudinal seam with a bevel ≥3 finger-widths, score and slip it, press the seam flat from the inside, and at half-dry twist out the wooden core. **Acceptance**: ovality — measure the long and short diameters with calipers, rotate 90°, measure again; long-short difference <5%; strike test on the longitudinal seam and the clear-bore ball roll as above.

◎ Slip-jointing hand feel — "slip full but not runny; on pressing, a single line of slip squeezes out": practice method: score and slip 10 practice joints on a scrap slab, cut the cross-sections open and look — voids = too little slip; running = slip too thin; within 10 joints find the "squeezes a line, does not run" feel. Misjudgment compensation: weigh after joining — a gain of 3%–6% over the dry joint (empirical value, must be field-calibrated) is the passing slip-quantity grade; above the upper limit, let it dry; below the lower limit, re-slip.

[Card 11-07 Clay Pipe Tees and Bends] (eight fields)

1. **Input materials**: straight-pipe body (11.8 process), coils or slabs, wet sand (for the curved core), a round wooden rod wound with straw rope (a withdrawable core), joining slip. Kiln flues and water lines must turn and branch; straight pipe cannot.
2. **Tools required**: core former (rope-wound rod — the rope layer lets the core withdraw), scored rule, angle gauge (Chapter 3), test ball (for the bore check). Calibration source: the bore is proven with a **rolling ball**, not by eye.
3. **Sequence and control points**:
   - **Bend, coil method**: pile wet sand into the curve core (**radius ≥1.5× pipe diameter**) → compact and smooth → dust with sand as a release layer → coil the bend → withdraw the sand;
   - **Bend, lobster-back method** (no core): fire 3 straight sections, cut each end at the angle (for a 90° three-section bend: **middle section 15° at each end, end sections 15° each**) → join with slip;
   - **Tee, core-and-mold**: main barrel per the straight-pipe process; the branch uses a **core — a rod wound with straw rope, rod diameter = branch bore − 0.5 cm** (allowance for the slip layer) → slip and join → **fillet 3 fingers wide** (try 2/3/4 fingers in steps).
   Control points: the radius may not fall below 1.5× diameter (the test ball will not pass); fillet width comes from a gradient, not "the thicker the better".
4. **Empirical parameters and calibration**: bend radius ≥1.5× diameter; **bore acceptance = a ball of 0.8× the bore diameter enters one end, rolls through on a tilt, and exits freely**; tee fillet 3 fingers (2/3/4 gradient); core rod = branch bore − 0.5 cm.
5. **Observations to record**: per piece — method (coil / lobster-back / core) / radius or cutting angle / fillet width / ball-pass result / crack location after firing.
6. **Pass / rework / scrap criteria**: pass = ball passes freely, joint uncracked, angle matches the gauge; rework = local distortion corrected while wet (once dry, remake as a smaller piece); scrap = joint cracked through (the root of every leaking flue and weeping water line).
7. **Common failures and diagnosis order**: (1) ball jams → radius short or a clay lump on the inner wall; (2) joint cracks → the two sections differ in water content; equalize before joining; (3) branch sags → core withdrawn too early; wait until the wall stiffens; (4) fillet cracks → fillet too thick or not at the same water content as the barrel.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = one tee or bend passing the ball test; L2 = one bend by each method plus one tee, all passing; L3 = a second person makes one from this card alone and passes the ball, both reading "passes freely" the same way.

### 11.12 Waste Pottery Recycling and the Grog Production Line (New in v2)

Scrap is not garbage; it is grog ore. This section turns the raw material of the 11.8 grog system (pounded old pottery) into a production line: scrapped pottery → sorting → crushing → sieving → warehousing, establishing the upstream supply for Chapter 12 refractory material — only with grog stock in the storehouse do refractory base mix calibration and furnace-construction supply have material to work with.

**Scrap sorting system (executed at kiln opening; three classes)**:

| Class | Criteria | Disposition |
|---|---|---|
| Refirable | Underfired (dull sound, seeps) with no cracks and no glaze | Refire once; still underfired after the second firing → grog class |
| Grog-eligible | Overfired/deformed pieces, cracked pieces, broken finished ware, scrap pads and separators — **plain surface only, no salt bloom, no black core in cross-section** | Crush, sieve, warehouse (coarse and fine grades, as in 11.8) |
| Discard | Salt-bloom pieces (soluble salt in the grog makes finished ware frost and blister), glazed pieces (glaze introduces fluxes that drag refractoriness down), black-core pieces not yet decarbonized, pieces of unknown provenance | Single pile at the discard ground; count and register |

Black-core exception path: pieces with black cross-sections go through one more empty kiln run to burn out the carbon; once the cross-section returns to the original color and re-inspection shows no black core, they move to the grog class.

**Grog crushing and sieving line**: a fixed workstation with four operations — ① coarse break (stone anvil and stone hammer, down to walnut size) → ② medium break (fine pounding to pea size) → ③ two-grade sieving (coarse grog 1–2 mm, fine grog <0.5 mm; grades as in 11.8) → ④ bag, warehouse, and tag (batch, source kiln run, grade, volume). Prefer wet operations; the dry-pounding station stands upwind (safety boundary, dust clause). **Line calibration**: one scrapped 2–4 L plain pot yields about 1.5–2.5 L of grog across the two grades (volume; empirical value, must be field-calibrated: record the actual output of three consecutive pieces and average); crushing takes half an hour to an hour per piece (the medium break takes the majority; empirical value).

**Recovery ratio registration (ledger fields)**: after each kiln's sorting, record — piece count and estimated volume per class, grog volume warehoused (measured with the volume bucket), pieces refired, pieces discarded. Monthly accounting: **plain-scrap grog conversion rate = grog volume warehoused ÷ plain-scrap volume, target ≥70% (empirical value, must be field-calibrated)**. Cross-check grog stock against the 11.8 refractory base mix and the furnace-construction plan: when stock falls below the equivalent of one kiln's refractory brick requirement, sorting and crushing are designated a priority task.

### 11.13 Preliminary Research on Sectional Coil Building of Large Urns (New in v2)

> This section is a **preliminary-research file (tiers 1–2)**: the target vessel (a person-height storage urn, 200–400 L class) cannot be made yet — declared in the capability boundary. This section delivers the upgrade path: the sectional process plan, joint-strength criteria, and a failure-case registration system. Tackle it per this section once population scales up and the demand materializes.

**Why it cannot be coil-built in one piece**: three bottlenecks — ① a body taller than a person cannot be built upward before its lower part has developed load-bearing strength (above 1 m, the 11.3 pause-and-rest rule means an unacceptable schedule plus belly-bulge collapse risk); ② layered drying: when the lower layer is bone-dry the upper is still wet, and uniform drying has no solution; ③ the current shaft kiln chamber, 80–120 cm high, cannot hold a whole urn. Conclusion: **sectional drying → join the sections → one final firing of the whole**.

**Sectional process plan (preliminary parameters)**: split into 3–4 sections, each 30–40 cm tall (empirical starting point, must be field-calibrated); coil-build each section per 11.3 to leather-hard, bag it in straw for slow drying until all four bone-dry criteria pass, then take it off the rack. **Reserve the inter-section overlap shoulder during coil building**: the upper section's mouth flares outward into an external shoulder; the lower section's mouth turns inward into a receiving face; overlap width ≥4 finger-widths. **Joining**: score both mating faces → brush on thin slip → press together → add an exterior corner-fillet coil at the joint (4 finger-widths wide, thickness ≤1/2 the wall, honoring the 11.4 thickness ratio ≤2:1) → shade-dry the whole piece another 3–5 days (empirical value; calibrate to conditions). Hard conditions: **both sections must come from the same clay batch and the same water-content grade** (the 11.2 ledger weight grade) — joining different water-content grades = desynchronized shrinkage = certain cracking.

**Joint-strength criteria (upgrade acceptance; three gates)**: ① test-section gate — before the first real urn, make joined test pieces (two short tube sections, 15 cm tall, 25 cm diameter); when dry, fill with one dou of water and let stand overnight; the joint neither seeps nor cracks = pass; ② pre-firing gate — the joined whole urn's strike sound at the joints matches the body, and the body's weight is unchanged for two consecutive days (the 11.3 bone-dry criteria extended to the joined piece); ③ post-firing gate — the whole urn passes the 11.7 three-part acceptance, and the joint zone's water absorption is no higher than the rest of the body.

**Failure-case registration system**: register every joining test (test section or whole urn) piece by piece: date, clay batch number, water-content grade, overlap width, fillet dimensions, shade-drying days, result (pass/crack/seep); keep cross-section samples or traced drawings of cracked pieces. **Upgrade threshold: three consecutive test sections all pass before the first whole urn may be attempted**; on any whole-urn failure, drop back to test sections, re-verify, then attack again. Unregistered failures may not be discarded — every cracked urn is the most expensive teacher the later batches will have.

| Elbow joint cracks | Small bevel mating face; fillet missing | Crack runs straight along the joint | Reapply the fillet coil; damp-cloth slow drying; refire |
| Large-urn section joint cracks | Joining different batches / water-content grades; insufficient overlap | Crack runs a full ring along the joint | Pair same batch, same grade; overlap ≥4 finger-widths; test sections pass all three gates first |

## C. Unresolved Dependencies

- Fast wheel (potter's wheel) throwing: needs stable high-speed rotation and bearings; an upgrade item — current coil-building capacity can carry the book's schedule; turntable trimming can first transition with a slow wheel (hand-spun turntable + shallow-socket support).
- Quantitative temperature measurement: firing judgment rests on smoke color, fire color, test tiles, and inference from finished ware; the slump-cone set comparison method is established in Chapter 12.
- Glaze: the wood-ash glaze (ash slurry brushed on and fired hot) can vitrify near 1100–1200 °C; listed as an upgrade route — register small samples first, with no production commitment; lead glazes and the like need materials from Part 4 onward.
- Metal cutting wire and trimming knives: for now replaced by the cord bow and stone/bone scraper blades — inefficient but self-sustaining.
- Large urn (person-height storage urn) sectional coil-building technique: demand appears once population scales up.

## Safety Boundary

- Kiln areas enforce all Chapter 6.8 fire rules; tamp the edges of pit and shaft kilns against collapse; open kilns only after natural overnight cooling; watering is forbidden.
- Stoking indoors follows the four CO iron rules (6.8); the shaft-kiln stoker stands to the side of the fire port, guarding against flashback.
- Dry-ware grinding and grog crushing produce dust: prefer wet operations; wear a damp cloth over nose and mouth for dry grinding and stand upwind.
- Shell temper must be calcined and slaked before entering the mix (11.2), to prevent residual quicklime in finished ware from meeting water, expanding, cracking, and bursting into injurious fragments.
- Cookware quench demonstrations use scrapped pieces only; passing product gets only gentle-heating acceptance.

## Acceptance Criteria

- **L1 proof of principle**: complete one panning run yielding fine clay; coil-build one straight-cylinder body that shade-dries without cracking; fire one non-seeping vessel by bonfire or pit firing.
- **L2 repeatable manufacture**: two consecutive kilns at yield ≥60% (pit kiln) / ≥70% (shaft kiln) (empirical values, must be field-calibrated); the three-part sintering criterion executed piece by piece as acceptance and recorded; water absorption data on file (dense grade <5%); one cooking pot passes thermal-shock acceptance; one accepted batch each of brick, tile, and ceramic pipe; local temper gradient tests and refractory base mix calibration complete.
- **L3 sustainable production**: both sources — clay and fuelwood — registered with monthly extraction quotas; kiln-run files continuous and reproducible (a second member fires same-grade ware from the records); the grog–refractory brick loop (waste pottery recycling) running; two or more members independently complete the full panning-to-kiln-opening flow; pottery stock covers the Chapter 10 three-target demand with a loss account.
