# Chapter 41 Paper, Ink, and Writing Media

> **Link with Section 4.9**: The paper of this chapter is the formal replacement for the 4.9 provisional media (clay tablets / wooden boards / bark / leather). When upgrading by transcription, follow the Section 4.10 borrowing registry; old media is archived, not destroyed.


> This chapter opens Part 8 (Knowledge Media and Transmission) and answers the structural criticism of the first review round: Chapter 4 requires readers to copy cards, Iron Law Six requires "acceptance leaves evidence in the files," and Chapters 37–40 require ledgers, shift rosters, and account books — all of this presupposes that paper, ink, pens, binding, and insect and moisture protection already exist, yet nowhere in the book up to this point has any chapter answered "where do they come from." A manual that demands twenty years of use and requires its readers to pass experience to the next generation cannot leave its own physical medium hanging in the air; without a medium, "acceptance criteria into the files," "process cards," and "cross-generational transmission" would be institution without substance. This chapter fills that gap: handmade paper, pine-soot ink, pens, binding, and archive protection. From here on, the "record, file, transmit" demanded repeatedly by the first forty-four chapters has, for the first time, a base that can be touched.

## Capability Boundary

**Can do:**

- Reliably form handmade paper from paper mulberry bark / mulberry bark (Grade-1 feedstock), establishing the full-chain process from retting, alkali boiling, and beating to sheetforming, sizing, and burnishing; the product meets the archival criteria of "writes with saturated ink without bleeding, survives 30 days of accelerated sun aging without turning brittle or cracking."
- Make sticks from pine soot taken at the charcoal kiln flue plus skin glue, establishing a grinding-consistency criterion and a water-resistance criterion; establish three tiers of writing tools — reed pen, bamboo-fiber brush, and hair brush.
- Establish an archive-protection system of binding, insect proofing, moisture proofing, fire protection, and dual off-site copies, with observable periodic inspection criteria.

**Cannot do:**

- **The whiteness, uniformity, and printing-suitability precision of machine-made paper.** Without a mechanical beater, a calender stack, and chemical bleach, basis-weight variation and thickness uniformity of the paper can only be controlled by consistency calibration and technique. Handmade paper can be hand-copied and rubbed; carved-block and movable-type printing are listed as an upgrade route (41.1.3) and are not promised in this chapter.
- **Quantitative acid–alkali measurement.** Without indicators and titration, lye concentration can only be calibrated by the slippery hand-feel (qualitative presence/absence) plus the effect-based method of "fibers disintegrate when hand-torn after boiling"; all concentration figures are empirical values.
- **Making iron gall ink durable.** The acid produced by the iron-salt–tannin reaction corrodes paper — a defect at the level of principle that cannot be eliminated before refined acid–alkali means exist; hence iron gall ink is banned in the archival layer (41.4.4).

**External dependencies (counted into the C list):**

1. Fiber-source plants (paper mulberry, mulberry; secondarily bamboo and hemp) with ≥2 stable stands within a half-day round trip of the settlement.
2. Pine soot depends on pine or resin-rich firewood; in pine-less regions the soot color turns brownish and the ceiling of ink blackness drops.
3. Insect-repelling plants (Amur cork tree, Sichuan pepper) are regional resources; where absent, insect proofing degrades to smoking + sunning, compensated by more frequent inspection.

## 41.1 Options and Costs of Knowledge Media

### 41.1.1 Comparison of Five Media: Why the Archival Layer Chooses Paper plus Ink

| Medium | Durability (natural storage) | Cost (per 100 characters, person·hours) | Copying cost | Dominant failure mode | Positioning |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Stone inscription | Century-to-millennium order | 20—60 | A rubbing 0.5—1 person·h | Earthquake, deliberate chiseling, weathering with moss overgrowth | Permanent layer: Iron Laws, weights-and-measures standards, master catalog |
| Ceramic tablet (incised then fired) | Fire- and moisture-resistant; decades to a century | 10—30 | Incised piece by piece; no low-cost copying | Breaking; 5—10% firing shrinkage distorts incised lines (shrinkage allowance required) | Kiln critical parameters, hazard warning signs |
| Wooden board + charcoal pencil / wax tablet | 1—3 years (can be scraped and reused) | 1—3 | Hand copying | Moisture warping and cracking, insect damage, mold | Working layer: drafts, trial calculations, circulation copies |
| Parchment / tanned leather | Decades in dry environments | 30—80 (one hide yields only 2—4 pages) | Hand copying | Mold in humidity, pages sticking together | Very small numbers of permanent contracts (when livestock scale allows) |
| Handmade paper + pine-soot ink | 20—100-year order with proper protection | 0.5—2 | Hand copying 0.2—0.5 person·h; rubbing of the same order | Moisture mold, insect damage, acid corrosion, sun embrittlement | Archival layer: all process cards, ledgers, account books, plate atlases |

Reading: stone is the most durable but costs tens of person·hours per 100 characters; copying a whole manual onto it equals building an irrigation channel — it can only be reserved for the dozen or so least-regenerable Iron Laws and standards. Parchment is hard-constrained by livestock scale. Wooden boards are reusable but do not keep. For a twenty-year horizon that must be copied, circulated, and issued one copy per person, the only medium is paper: it is the sole option satisfying "low cost × copyable × durable enough" simultaneously — **provided all three gates of sizing (no bleeding), insect proofing, and moisture proofing are passed**; these three gates are the main body of 41.3—41.5. Paper does not survive fire, so the permanent layer must keep stone as the backstop: a fire may destroy the archival layer, it must not be allowed to destroy the Iron Laws.

Hence we establish the **layered-medium system** (written into the settlement's archive regulations):

1. **Permanent layer (stone inscription / ceramic tablet)**: the seven Iron Laws, length and mass standards, master catalog. Inspected yearly (visual check, moss scraping, rubbing backup).
2. **Archival layer (paper + pine-soot ink, stored in insect- and moisture-proofed cabinets)**: process card originals, ledger annual volumes, materials-and-energy account books, acceptance evidence, plate atlases.
3. **Working layer (wooden boards / waste paper / iron gall ink)**: drafts, circulation copies, external packaging markings. Replace when worn; never into the cabinets; carry no transmission responsibility.

Order-of-magnitude self-check (third rule of number discipline): suppose the whole settlement needs 20 copies of the manual at 100 pages each (quarter size), 2000 pages in total. Per 41.6: 100 folio sheets cut into quarter sheets give 400 pages at 6—9 person·days, so 2000 pages convert to about 0.5 hundred-folio-sheet units, roughly 30—45 person·days, plus copying labor (0.5—1 person·h per page, about 20 person·days) — one full-time craftsperson can outfit the whole settlement within a season, at bearable cost. The same page count carved in stone, converted at 20—60 person·h per 100 characters, would take on the order of a hundred person·years — two orders of magnitude apart. That is the arithmetic basis of "stone as the floor, paper for daily use."

### 41.1.2 Standardization of Sheet Sizes

| Size | Trimmed dimensions (mm) | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Drawing size | 450×600 | Engineering plate atlas and master drawings (the later engineering drafting chapter adopts this series directly; no separate sizes will be created) |
| Full sheet | 300×420 | Large tables, master ledger pages |
| Folio | 210×300 | Standard process card size (all cards in this book are cut to this) |
| Quarter | 150×210 | Ledger daily pages, index cards |

Standardization has triple significance. First, the papermaking screen and frame mold are built as a single set to the full-sheet deckle dimension 340×460; after forming, each sheet cuts into 1 full sheet, 2 folios, or 4 quarters. Second, archive cabinet shelf depth is built to 320 mm (full sheet plus allowance), so all four sizes fit the same cabinet. Third, folio cards and quarter ledger pages are bound by the same method from the same stock — one system governs all paper documents. Cutting criterion: the two diagonals of each sheet differ by ≤3 mm (Chapter 3 measurement methods); for deckle-edged paper, cut one edge straight as the reference, then cut the opposite edge.

### 41.1.3 Upgrade Route (Declared, Not Promised)

Carved-block printing, movable-type printing, and machine-made paper are listed as upgrade routes for when conditions mature: movable type requires metal type-casting capability and paper of higher uniformity and smoothness than this chapter's, plus a literate population reaching the output of the Chapter 4 teaching system — starting printing before all three are in place only prints errors. The copying means this chapter promises are two: hand copying (one copy per person; master–apprentice card transcription is itself a transmission assessment — see the Chapter 4 system) and rubbing (41.4.6, physical copying of stone-inscribed patterns and incised-line plates).

## 41.2 Fiber Pulping

### 41.2.1 Feedstock Grading

| Grade | Feedstock | Fiber traits | Sheet quality | Use boundary |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Grade 1 | Paper mulberry bark, mulberry bark | Long fibers (5—15 mm per fiber order of magnitude, empirical), moderate mucilage easily removed | High strength, fold-resistant, light color | The only feedstock allowed for archival paper |
| Grade 2 | Young bamboo split slivers | Stiff, short fibers | More brittle, yellowish | Packaging, drafts; not into official archives |
| Grade 2 (shortcut) | Rags, hemp scraps, unlaid old rope | Already degummed, no retting needed | Depends on original cloth quality | Leftover-resource shortcut from Starting Point B; must be unpicked of stitching, washed, cut into pieces |
| Grade 3 | Dry grass, rice straw | Short, brittle fibers | Brittle, dark paper | Packaging and kindling only; banned from the archive and working-writing layers |

Harvesting and retting: strip bark during the active cambium season of spring and summer, taking only 30 cm segments per tree; girdling the full circumference is strictly forbidden (reuse the Chapter 7 quota). Tie fresh bark into bundles and soak in the retting pool 7—14 days (take the lower limit at water temperature ≥20℃; longer in winter, shorter in summer; empirical values, must be field-calibrated). The fully retted criterion reuses Chapter 7: outer bark separates from the woody core with ease, and pulling raises a fiber web. Over-retting criterion: fibers snap when pulled, color blackens, surface turns slimy — over-retted stock is downgraded to Grade 3 or discarded. The Chapter 7 warning on decay bacteria in retting pools is reused: wash hands after handling; keep wounds out of contact with retting water.

Bamboo preprocessing (Grade 2): cut young bamboo into sections, split into slivers, crack into strips; first soak in clear water 10—20 days to soften (empirical; must be field-calibrated; criterion: a strip can be bent by hand without breaking while fibers fuzz), then alkali-boil 6—12 h. Bamboo pulp yield is lower than bark's (empirically 20—35%); the paper is brittle and yellow, used only for packaging and drafts — this grading is a hard boundary: blending bamboo pulp into archival paper lowers fold endurance and aging resistance across the whole sheet, and the blend is undetectable afterward — a textbook silent failure. Therefore it is ruled that **archival paper may be charged with Grade-1 bark only; blending is forbidden**, and charge purity is audited through the intake column of the paper mill's daily log.

Disinfection of the rag shortcut: rags and hemp scraps must first be boiled 0.5 h (to kill microbes and insect eggs), unpicked of all stitching, cut into pieces under 50 mm, and only then alkali-boiled; otherwise eggs on the cloth enter the cabinets with the paper and the insect-proofing system is defeated at its source.

### 41.2.2 Ash-Alkali Digestion

Purpose: retting removes only most of the mucilage; residual mucilage and lignin must be broken down by alkali boiling. Undercooked, the paper yellows and turns brittle and sizing will not hold ink; overcooked, fibers are damaged and strength collapses — the two look alike from outside, a typical silent failure, and must be controlled by the withdrawal-sample hand-tear criterion.

- Charge ratio: 1 kg dry bark to 8—12 L wood-ash leachate, liquor covering the charge (use the Chapter 8 leachate directly, no further concentration).
- **Lye concentration calibration (effect-based method, replacing all concentration numbers)**: slippery hand-feel; no phenolphthalein-type indicators — use the Chapter 8 wood-ash leachate as-is, and **judge concentration by whether fibers disintegrate when hand-torn after boiling**. If a withdrawal sample still cannot be torn after 2 h of boiling, judge the alkali weak: change the next batch to ash:water = 1:3, or take a second leachate to strengthen it; if within 1 h fibers rub into paste, judge the alkali too strong: change to 1:5 and retry. Recalibrate each time the ash batch changes, and transcribe the calibration result into the ledger (Iron Law Six).
- Operation: charge bark into a ceramic pot or iron kettle (Chapters 11 and 18 vessels), add lye, bring to a boil, then hold at a bare simmer 2—6 h (Grade-1 bark, empirical; bamboo 6—12 h). **Every 1 h withdraw a small handful of fibers with a long-handled fork, rinse clean in cold water, and judge doneness by hand-tearing**:
  - Tears at once into fiber bundles, pale yellow, not prickly to the finger — cooked through; discharge immediately;
  - Cannot be torn, still in sheets — keep boiling, sample again next hour;
  - Rubs into paste, snaps when pulled wet, dark brown — overcooked; discard this handful; if the whole pot is so, downgrade to packaging pulp, re-check alkali strength and duration, and record the loss in the ledger.

### 41.2.3 Washing and Sun Bleaching

Discharge into clear water and wash by panning 5—8 passes, until the wash water runs clear and fibers feel not slippery to the hand. **Residual alkali is the common root of two failures — brittle paper and sizing that will not take ink** — so the washing criterion must be executed pass by pass; counting passes is not allowed (if the final rinse still feels slippery to the hand, keep washing). Bleaching: spread the wet pulp on straw mats, sun by day and dew by night for 3—15 days (empirical), turning once daily; stop when the color turns from deep yellow to pale cream. Criterion: two shades lighter than the numbered Day-1 kept sample by visual comparison. Bleaching changes color, not strength; archival paper does not demand whiteness — 3—5 days of bleaching suffices.

### 41.2.4 Beating ◎

Tools: stone mortar with wooden pestle, or wooden mallet on stone slab (Chapters 5, 9, 11). Pound the boiled and washed fibers 1.5—3 h (lower limit for Grade-1 stock, empirical), until fibers are separated and fibrillated.

Criterion (observable): **lifted pulp shows a cloud-fluff structure** — lift a pinch of diluted pulp with the fingers against the light: fibers disperse like cloud wisps, no clumps or knots; then rub open a single fiber: the ends fuzz out like a tiny broom (fibrillation).

◎ Hand-feel: the landing point and rhythm of the blows. Practice: pound 200 strokes on an already qualified pulp sample for comparison, sensing the resistance change from "springy-hard" to "soft-giving" and the spatter change from "carrying pulp stars" to "raising a fuzzy mist", then return to fresh stock and pound until the same feel. Misjudgment compensation (hand-feel is never the sole basis): every 30 min of pounding, take pulp and form a 100×100 mm test sheet; when dry, hand-tear — if the fracture shows fibers pulled out (fuzzy stubble with drawn strands), beating is sufficient; a flat, brittle, clean tear means insufficient — keep pounding; if the wet sheet snaps when merely lifted, or the dry sample cracks on a single fold, it is over-beaten — downgrade the batch, or blend in unbeaten fresh pulp to dilute and reform into coarse paper.

## 41.3 Sheetforming

### 41.3.1 Bamboo Screen and Frame Mold

**Figure 41-1 Sheetforming screen and pulp vat (schematic cross-section)**

```
   Pulp vat (wooden vat)
   ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~ ← pulp slurry (cloud-fluff texture)
   ════════════════ ← bamboo screen (fine bamboo filaments side by side; screen gaps = paper grain)
   ┌──frame mold──┐       screen + frame sink into the pulp: one sway, one lift
   └────────┘       → wet sheets stacked → pressing → wall-drying → sizing and burnishing
```


The screen is woven from fine bamboo-strip slivers (1.5—2 mm wide, about 0.5 mm thick, Chapter 7 strip-splitting method) with horse-tail hair or fine hemp cord into a 360×480 mm screen face; **the screen gaps are the paper grain** — only an evenly woven screen forms even paper, and the screen is this chapter's first critical tooling. Three acceptance criteria: (1) laid on a planed board, the screen face lies fully flat with no gaps or curled edges; (2) after 2 h soaking, the slivers do not come loose and the screen face does not warp; (3) on a trial sheet, the wet sheet lifts off intact and no pulp residue clings to the screen. Frame mold (screen bed): a hardwood frame with inner clear opening 340×460 mm that clamps the screen on all four sides. Screen damage handling: 1—2 broken slivers can be rewoven in place; once the screen face deforms or edges curl, the whole screen is scrapped and rewoven — making do is forbidden — paper from a deformed screen is necessarily uneven in thickness, and the operator will mistakenly blame their own technique (the tooling source of silent failure).

### 41.3.2 Slurry Consistency Calibration

Charge the sheetforming vat (100—150 L) with 400—700 g of dry pulp (corresponding to 4—7 g/L, empirical) and stir even. **The consistency window is calibrated by "forming one uniform, unbroken sheet" — the three-test-sheet method**:

- Too thin: paper thin, cloud blotches in transmitted light, holes — add pulp and retry;
- Too thick: pulp clings to the screen, heavy edges, uneven thickness — add water and retry;
- Three consecutive sheets uniform and intact — this becomes the vat's standard consistency; record the "dry-pulp charge" into the ledger; from then on reproduce the charge by weighing, no more mixing water by feel.

Suspension (optional empirical method): fibers settle as soon as stirring stops, so stir as you form. If the locality has plants whose cold-water infusions yield mucilage, such as elm bark or carambola twigs ("slippery water"), draw 2—4 L of infusion into the vat to aid suspension and improve formation — this is an empirical method: first trial it in a small vat to establish whether it gives a "slippery, film-clinging feel", keep a record, and only then use it; where unavailable, substitute frequent stirring: the papermaker stirs the vat one round every 3—5 sheets.

### 41.3.3 Forming Motion ◎ (One Sway, One Lift)

Steps: hold the screen level in both hands inside the frame mold and insert it into the vat at a slant — leading edge first, trailing edge following, letting the pulp wash over the screen face. **One sway**: tilt the screen forward and then back once each, with an amplitude such that the pulp flows across the screen face once each way; **one lift**: at the instant the screen breaks the surface, level it and raise it vertical or slightly forward-tilted; when the water drains off and fibers lie evenly across the screen face, a wet sheet is formed. Pre-lift check: hold the level screen against the light and inspect formation; any obvious cloud blotch or hole — pour the pulp back into the vat and reform (a spoiled sheet goes back to the vat; only labor is lost, not material).

◎ Hand-feel: the sway amplitude and the lift timing. Under-swayed, the paper is thick in the middle and thin at the edges; lifted unevenly, one side is thick and the other thin. Practice: with an empty screen in a clear-water vat, drill "slant-in — sway — level lift" 50 times until, on lifting, the water film on the screen is uniform and does not run toward one corner; only then move to pulp; for the first 20 sheets aim for intactness, not uniformity. Misjudgment compensation: after each sheet is lifted, inspect against the light (transmitted-light uniformity is objective evidence), and tally each person's daily spoiled-sheet rate into the ledger — if one person's spoiled-sheet rate exceeds 20% for 3 consecutive days, send them back to drill the empty screen in clear water; grinding on and wasting pulp is not allowed. The formation pass line is "no cloud blotch visible to the naked eye in transmitted light".

### 41.3.4 Pressing and Dewatering (Staged Loading)

Stack wet sheets on the backing board as they are formed, coarse cloth between layers; after stacking 50—100 sheets, cover with a board and load stone: first light pressure (board's own weight plus 2—3 kg of stone) for 0.5 h, then add stone in stages to 15—25 kg, total pressing 2—6 h (empirical). **The purpose of staging: expel free water first, then consolidate — rushing it presses water lines into the lower sheets and sticks them together**. Criterion: after pressing, test-peel one sheet; if it lifts off whole and does not stick to the sheet below, pressing is done; if it sticks, press another 1 h and test again. Forced peeling of a stack that pressed through incompletely or has stuck together is forbidden; handling is in the failure-mode table.

### 41.3.5 Drying (Wall Hanging)

After pressing, lift sheets one by one and stick them onto a smoothed wall or an upright smooth wooden board (optionally first brush on a thin coat of rice water for adhesion — empirical, not required), and dry in shade with airflow. Criterion: paper edges curl up naturally, and the whole sheet pressed to the lip gives no cooling sensation. Avoid blazing sun (wrinkling, embrittlement) and baking near fire (localized brittleness and cracking). Full drying takes about 0.5—2 days (weather-dependent, empirical). Cut dry paper into the four sizes per 41.1.2.

Seasonal window (for production scheduling; links to the Chapter 37 farm-busy labor pullback): sheetforming is water work — stop once vat water nears freezing (wet sheets freeze and tear on lifting; the screen turns brittle and breaks); in overcast rainy seasons drying time doubles and sizing will not dry, so pre-plan capacity at a 0.5 reduction. The year's production window centers on the dry season; before the rains, rush-form stock up to ≥3 months of use (aligned with the 41.6 stock baseline); the retting pool and open-air drying are equally season-governed — the paper mill's annual plan follows the seasons, not orders.

### 41.3.6 Sizing (Surface Application)

Unsized paper always bleeds ink. Two methods (try one first; switch only if it fails):

| Sizing method | Ratio (by mass, empirical) | Operation | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|---|
| Starch paste | Starch:water = 1:20—1:30 | First slake in a little cold water, then pour into boiling water to gelatinize into a thin paste; brush onto the paper warm, once, and dry in shade | Does not stick pages when damp; first choice for archival paper |
| Gelatin water | Skin glue:water = 1:25—1:35 | Dissolve in hot water (do not boil); brush on or quick-dip once; dry in shade | Tough, abrasion-resistant film, but pages stick when damp |

**Core criterion: ink written on does not bleed** — with a pen saturated with ink, dot the paper and hold 1 s, draw a 100 mm line, and write 10 characters: pass requires no halo or feathering at stroke edges and a dot-blotch diameter ≤2 mm. Bleeding means fail: apply again (at most twice); if it still bleeds after two applications, go back and check whether washing in 41.2.3 left alkali — alkali-carrying paper cannot hold sizing; this is the true cause hiding under the false appearance of "uneven sizing".

### 41.3.7 Burnishing

Lay dry paper flat on a smooth stone slab or hardwood bench and roll back and forth once with a river pebble or large shell. Criteria: no fuzzy stubble or upturned fibers against the light; even resistance as a dry fingertip strokes across; an ink dot after stroking still does not bleed (the size film was not rolled off). Burnishing also consolidates the size film; archival paper is burnished without exception.

7. **Common failures and troubleshooting order**: Uneven thickness → first three test sheets to set consistency → then check stirring frequency → last check sway/lift technique (streak direction locates it); sticking → first check whether loading was staged → then check the interleave cloth.
8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = 10 intact sample sheets and the sheetforming daily log; L2 = spoiled-sheet statistics for 100 sheets on each of 3 consecutive days, 10 sheets sampled all passing the no-bleed criterion, 3 sun-aging test sheets each with a matched control (numbered and archived); L3 = two months of paper accounts (feed—output—requisition three-way match) and two people independently passing L2 with skills-matrix sign-off.

## 41.4 Ink and Pens

### 41.4.1 Collecting and Refining Pine Soot

Source: the flue inner walls, bends, and the underside of the cap of the Chapter 6 charcoal kiln — soot deposits only in the relatively low-temperature stretches of the flue (below the zone that feels "hot but not touchable for long"). Pine-firewood kilns give the blackest soot; in pine-less regions substitute pine branches, birch bark, or resin-rich old pine firewood, and the soot color is brownish (a Class-C limitation). Operation: only after the kiln is shut down and cold through, scrape with a long-handled wooden scraper; wear a felt-cloth mask against inhalation; work in pairs, one person standing by outside the flue mouth; each kiln yields 50—200 g (empirical). Refining (water levigation, reusing the Chapter 8 method): stir the soot into water in a trough so it clouds; sand sinks and is discarded; after brief settling, pour the upper suspension into a second trough, let stand 12—24 h, siphon off the water, take the bottom layer, and sun-dry. Criterion: no grittiness when rubbed between fingers, jet-black color; if any sandy feel remains, levigate once more.

### 41.4.2 Pounding into Ink Sticks

Ratio: pine soot : skin glue (by dry glue weight) = 10:3—10:5 (mass, empirical). Hot-dissolve the skin glue at glue:water = 1:6 into a thick solution, mix in the soot powder, and pound in a mortar 1—2 h, until it **forms a ball when squeezed by hand, does not stick to the mortar, and shows a fine, lustrous fracture when broken open**. Glue-amount calibration (three-block test method): take 100 g of soot, split into three portions, and mix glue at 3:100, 4:100, and 5:100 respectively; pound a small piece of each and roll each into a 3 mm slender rod bent into a loop — a loop that bends without breaking means glue is sufficient; breaking apart at one bend means too little glue (add glue and re-pound); sticky and stringy means too much glue (add soot powder and pound again). Set the batch at the lowest glue tier that still bends without breaking, and record it in the ledger. Qualified material is pressed in a wooden mold into sticks, then **shade-dried 7—30 days**; blazing sun and direct drafts are forbidden (dry outside, wet inside — it will surely crack).

### 41.4.3 Grinding Criteria ◎

Before use, grind with a little water on an inkstone (a coarse stone slab with a ground-in hollow, or a ceramic plate, Chapter 11). **Consistency criterion: drop on paper does not bloom, drawn line is even** — grind until a drop of ink liquid on a sized test sheet does not bloom into a ring, and a 100 mm line drawn with the pen is even and unbroken: that is writing consistency.

◎ Hand-feel: grinding force and number of circles. Practice: grind the same new ink stick 50 times until the criterion passes, and record "water added × grinding circles" as the baseline; thereafter grind by the baseline and re-verify with the drop-on-paper test. Misjudgment compensation: acceptance always uses the dual criteria of drop-on-paper plus line-drawing; judging by visual darkness of ink color is not permitted — ink darkness is affected by paper color and paper thickness, a common source of misjudgment.

Water-resistance criterion (archival ink must pass): after the writing is fully dry, moisten with one drop of clear water for 1 min; the strokes must not blur or spread. If it fails, the glue is light — re-check the ratio and redo a small batch.

8. **L1 / L2 / L3 evidence**: L1 = 1 stick plus trial-writing paper; L2 = all 3 sticks of the batch passing the four criteria, trial-writing paper numbered and archived; L3 = two months of monthly inventory of the soot-glue account, stick stock and per-kiln soot scrape (50—200 g per kiln) on the books.

### 41.4.4 Iron Gall Ink (Conditional Route; Banned from the Archive)

Formulation route: crush gall-type insect galls (galls on Chinese sumac, Rhus potaninii, or oak leaves) and boil for an infusion; add ferrous sulfate (green vitriol — iron-salt source: the blue-green crystals of the mine-pit oxidation zone, Chapter 16, or made by acid acting on iron, Chapter 23); add a little skin glue to make ink. It writes pale at first and oxidizes to deep black over several days; adhesion is good and its no-bleed requirement is lower than soot ink's.

**The price (written into the usage regulations)**: the iron-salt–tannin reaction produces acid that corrodes fibers continuously; over years to decades the paper browns and perforates at the writing — a defect at the level of principle that cannot be eliminated before acid–alkali refining means exist. Therefore: **iron gall ink is banned in the archival layer and used only for working-layer daily marking and external package numbering**; documents written in iron gall ink may never enter the archive cabinets as originals, and when transcribing duplicates they must be converted to soot ink.

Preparation and storage: iron gall ink is mainly mixed fresh for immediate use; bottle it in ceramic vessels (Chapter 11) with wooden stoppers, the bottle body carrying the dual marking of red band plus black dot (the Chapter 8 hazardous-goods marking system), stored and used in a zone separate from soot ink and inkstones; the preparation workshop must be ventilated (grinding green vitriol dust irritates the respiratory tract). If the settlement has not obtained the two feedstocks — galls and green vitriol — this route does not start at all; daily marking is uniformly substituted by soot ink at light consistency (grind with extra water) — the function substitutes, durability counts as soot ink's, which is in fact better for archiving.

### 41.4.5 Pens: Reed Pen, Bamboo-Fiber Brush, Hair Brush

| Pen | Making | Use | Pass criterion |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reed pen | Cut reed or a bamboo sliver to a 30°—45° oblique point; slit a fine ink channel along the tip axis | Hard lines, graduation lines, tables | One dip draws a 300 mm line without ink break, line width even |

### 41.4.6 Rubbing Reproduction

Lay wet paper over a stone inscription or incised-line plate; tap lightly with a felt brush to press the paper into the incisions; when half-dry, dab ink with a rubbing pad (a bamboo-fiber brush wrapped in cloth holding soot-ink powder): raised surfaces take ink, incised lines stay white; peel off and you have a copy. Criteria: incised lines complete and continuous, paper undamaged, ink tone even. Uses: copying stone-inscribed standard patterns and simple figures — the only bulk-reproduction means besides hand copying, and the forerunner of carved-block printing (upgrade route, 41.1.3).

## 41.5 Binding and Archive Protection

### 41.5.1 Folio Folding and Thread Binding

- Folios: quarter sheets (150×210) folded once into 105×150 pages, 20—30 sheets (40—60 pages) per volume; align the fold creases, weight with a wooden board and stone 0.5—1 h to set.
- Piercing: use the graduated rule (Chapter 3) to lay out equal divisions along the spine line; pierce 4—6 holes with a bone awl (Chapter 5) or iron awl (Chapter 19 onward, more durable) — four-hole binding — hole diameter ≤2 mm.
- Thread binding: fine hemp cord 1—2 mm (made per Chapter 7, passed static-load calibration), double thread locked back and forth, ends threaded back into a hole and knotted hidden. Three pass criteria: (1) the closed volume lies flat without cockling, and any page flips without the gathering loosening; (2) after pinching the spine and shaking 10 times, the binding thread stays tight; (3) pulling any sewn segment produces no shift.
- Labels: cover pasted with double-layer heavy paper or cloth (bonded with skin glue or starch paste), bearing the pigment label (Chapter 8): number, start date, original/duplicate mark.

### 41.5.2 Insect Proofing (Empirical Methods, Applied in Combination)

**All of the following are empirical methods with no single-item guarantee — they must be applied in combination, backed by periodic inspection; no single method may ever claim on its own that "insects are prevented."**

| Method | Practice | Criteria and notes |
|---|---|---|
| Paper dyed with Amur cork bark | Cork-tree inner bark:water = 1:10, boil 1 h for the decoction; archival paper dipped or brushed once, dried in shade | Yellow-tinged paper means treated — the yellow ground itself is the visible "insect-proofed" marker; the bitter principle suppresses insects |
| Smoking | Hang bound volumes on the smoking rack in cold smoke 1—2 days (same method as the Chapter 15 hair-material smoking) | Repels insects and wards off mold; paper color darkens slightly |
| Pepper decoction | Brush Sichuan-pepper boiled decoction onto cabinet inner walls and seams | Repels insects; re-brush every 1—2 years |
| Seasonal sunning | Pick a dry day each season to air the volumes in the sun | Doubles as the occasion for insect checks |

Amur cork tree and Sichuan pepper are regional resources; where absent, the system degrades to "smoking + seasonal sunning + sealed cabinet", insect-proofing strength drops, and this is compensated by intensified inspection (quarterly checks become monthly) and recorded as a downgrade entry in the archive regulations.

Insect-inspection criteria (executed each season): leaf through spines and page corners — on finding any one of fine sand-like powder (frass), irregular bore tunnels, or rice-grain-like egg masses, inspect the whole shelf, isolate and sun-expose 2 days, smoke to kill the insects, and register the damaged volumes and their disposal.

### 41.5.3 Moisture Proofing (Lime Moisture-Absorbing Cabinet; Continues Chapter 13)

Cabinet construction: wooden cabinet with an internal liner shelf; a bottom drawer holds cloth bags of quicklime (0.5—1 kg per bag, wrapped in densely woven coarse hemp cloth). All quicklime burning, slaking, and storage safety follows the Chapter 13 regulations; slaking protection during lime changes is the archive room's highest-hazard routine operation (see Safety Boundary).

**Dryness criterion: the dry paper strip hung inside the cabinet does not go limp** — hang a dry paper strip high inside the cabinet; every ten days open the cabinet, take the strip, and bend it: a crisp, audible brittleness on bending passes as dry; going soft and silent means moisture absorption has hit its limit — change the lime immediately. Corroborating sign of spent lime: the bag contents cake hard or the bag bulges; change on discovery. Empirical lime-change cycle: 1—3 months in the humid season, 3—6 months in the dry season; **the paper-strip criterion governs, not the calendar**.

### 41.5.4 Three-Tier Cabinet Protection and Dual Off-Site Copies

Three tiers of protection (missing any one, originals may not be stored):

1. **Off the ground**: cabinet feet set on stone or ceramic plinths ≥300 mm, against floor water, rats, and ant runways.
2. **Shaded from light**: cabinet away from windows, doors kept closed, shut immediately after retrieving files — direct sunlight is the number-one accelerator of paper aging (the L2 aging test uses sun exposure precisely as its accelerating means).
3. **Fire compartment**: archive room separated from furnaces and kilns, the kitchen, and fuel piles (≥15 m or separated by a solid-walled building, Chapter 13 compartmentalization principle); open flame banned around the cabinet; lighting by a minimal single-flame lamp, out when the person leaves.

Dual off-site copy system (Iron-Law grade; written into the archive regulations):

- Every official archive item (process card originals, ledger annual volumes, acceptance evidence) is copied once as a duplicate; originals and duplicates are stored in two separate buildings, ≥50 m apart and in different fire compartments.
- Duplicates are verified every half year: check against the catalog, replace missing pages, dual signatures.
- The borrowing registry has three columns: date taken/returned, handler, purpose; originals never leave the archive room on loan.

## Safety Boundary

This chapter's safety boundary has equal standing with the process itself; not executing it counts as not completing the process step (Iron Law Seven):

1. **Lye burns** (41.2.2, 41.3.6): splashes of boiling lye and hot glue liquid injure eyes and skin irreversibly. Protection: closely woven long-sleeved bast clothing, face curtain or wide-brimmed hanging veil (Chapter 15 work gear), long-handled ladle for charging, stand crosswind; keep a bucket of clear water at the stove side at all times; on skin splash, flush immediately with clear water for 15 min — flush first, wipe after, never rub. All alkali vessels are marked with a red band without exception (reusing the Chapter 8 system).
2. **Digestion CO** (41.2.2): poor kitchen ventilation lets CO accumulate; head pressure or nausea means evacuate people and ventilate at once (Chapter 6 symptom and response regulations). The stove house must have a low-level air inlet plus high-level smoke exhaust; tending the fire solo is forbidden (Chapter 37 two-person grading).
3. **Flue-collection falls and asphyxiation** (41.4.1): entry only after the kiln is shut down and cold through; work in pairs, one person standing by outside the mouth; each entry ≤15 min, then come out for air; stay low, guarding against residual-heat burns; those of unsuitable build do not enter — scraping from outside the mouth with the long-handled scraper is the primary means.
4. **Retting-pool infection** (41.2.1): reusing the Chapter 7 warning — wash hands after handling; keep wounds out of contact with retting water.
5. **Lime-change slaking injury** (41.5.3): quicklime slaking follows the full Chapter 13 protection set (cover, face curtain, oiled gloves, stand upwind, add to water in batches); lime changes in the archive room are two-person operations.
6. **Pounding and burnishing**: keep ≥150 mm between the pestle/mallet landing point and the hand holding the stock; guard toes when moving press stones (Chapter 7 handling regulations).
